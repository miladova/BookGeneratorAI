**A note on names and money**

Polish names are given as the characters used them. In 1938 a village outside
Mława spoke of *ojciec* and *matka*, of *pan* and *pani*, and used the diminutives
that no official document ever recorded: Zosia, Róża, and, in one household only,
*Zosieczka*. The narrator has kept them. A word about the surname Wierzbicka: the
Office's files spell it correctly. Most of the people in this book do not.

Money is in złoty. In 1938 a farm labourer earned about sixty złoty a month; a
pair of good boots cost fifteen; a cow, three hundred. In 1958 a teacher earned
eleven hundred a month and a decent coat cost nine hundred. The reader may find
these figures useful; the people in this book certainly did.

# Prologue

*Warsaw, October 1971*

The parcel came in October, eleven years after the funeral of a woman I had not seen since the year I turned eighteen. It was wrapped in brown paper and tied with string knotted twice, the way people who lived through the war tie everything, as if string were still rationed. It came from a notary's office in Mława, a town ninety-odd miles north of Warsaw, about which I knew two things: that my mother was born in a village nearby, and that my godmother had asked to be buried there.

Inside were a photograph, a tin of letters, a leather pouch, a silver lamp wrapped in a horse blanket, and a sealed envelope.

The photograph is the one I keep on my desk. It was taken on the sixth of January, 1958, in the church of St. Anne on Krakowskie Przedmieście, without permission, by a young man in the gallery above the nave with a camera inside his coat. The film was taken off him three weeks later in a cell on Rakowiecka Street; the negative was developed in a laboratory there and filed in a folder with forty-one other photographs of the same morning. The folder is mine now. I will explain how that happened, and why the explanation is not a comfort.

In the photograph, a priest stands in a pulpit of pale stone, his hands on the rim, his face lifted. He is thirty-eight. Below him the nave is full: students in dark coats, a boy holding a violin case between his knees as if it were a dog. At the back, near the door, a woman in a grey coat and a red scarf. She came in late and will leave before the last hymn. She is thirty-eight, an official of the state, and she has spent the morning deciding what to do about the man in the pulpit.

Someone in the laboratory drew a pencil circle around her head. The circle is not crooked. It was drawn by a careful person, a person who had been given a ruler and a name.

Her name was Zofia Wierzbicka. She was my godmother, by proxy — she stood for me in a village churchyard in the powiat of Mława in 1943, because I could not be brought to her and she could not be brought to me, and the Church has allowed that since the missions. The man in the pulpit was Father Michał Żurawski, who baptized me that same year, in secret, in a kitchen in the Old Town, with water from a kettle, and who was ninety miles away from her that winter and did not know that she had done it.

* * *

I was fourteen when I understood that my godmother was someone. It was 1956, the year of the thaw, and she took me to the Bristol for hot chocolate, and in the street outside a man in a black overcoat followed us from Nowy Świat to Bracka. When I asked whether we were being followed she said: *Of course we are, Inka. It's Thursday.* Then she stopped in the snow, took off her glove, and straightened the collar of my coat, and said: *Never be afraid of people who follow you. Be afraid of people who agree with you.*

* * *

I am a translator. My trade is other people's sentences — French and Italian into Polish if the text is long and the money short, and sometimes the other way round for the state press agency, which is how I came to know men who keep folders. For three years I have been learning what my godmother was doing in that church on the sixth of January, 1958. I have the minutes she wrote with her own hand, the carbon of a document that was never filed, and one sentence, added in pencil at the bottom of a page, that has kept me from sleeping for a month.

The sealed envelope is addressed to *ks. Michał Żurawski, biskup, Płock*. It weighs nothing. It is the heaviest thing I own.

* * *

My mother was a nurse, and she had been a girl in a village near Mława who came to Warsaw in 1946 and married a man named Balcerzak and never once went back, and she told me once, drunk, on the anniversary of something she would not name, that in a village with a river and a mill and a wooden church with a leaning tower, a boy and a girl made a promise; then the war came and took them both, and when they met again nineteen years later they stood on opposite sides of a table covered with papers, and neither could put the papers down. When I asked who the boy was, she said: *The bell.*

That is not a name. But in our family, names were like the lights in windows during the blackout: you kept them covered.

* * *

I have the minutes. I have the photograph with the careful circle. What I do not have is the night of the twenty-seventh of December, 1958, when the snow came down on the Mława road and the two of them drove north in a car of the Office. That night I can reconstruct only from a report of a journey in a government file, a letter that was never opened, and the word of a bishop who is still alive and will not talk to me.

Where I have put words in their mouths, I have put only words the documents, or my mother, or my own memory of that decade will support. If my godmother had read this book, she would have corrected my Polish, and then she would have burned it, and then she would have made me a cup of tea.

# Chapter One: The Silver Lamp

*Warsaw, 6 January 1958*

The Church of St. Anne was cold enough to see the congregation breathe.

Zofia stood at the back by the holy water stoup, where the door let in a draught every time someone came in, and watched the nave fill. There were more people than the church was built for; the students stood three deep along the side aisles, stamping their feet quietly. The smell was the smell of every church of her childhood — cold stone, wet wool, candle wax, a ghost of incense in the plaster.

She was not here in her official capacity. She had said so to the driver, who was called Krzysiek, and he had nodded the way you nod at a child's story.

The priest came up into the pulpit after the Gospel. He was tall, and he climbed slowly, as if the stone might not hold him, and stood looking down at the crowd with his hands flat on the rim. His hair was grey at the temples, the way ash is grey on a morning when the fire has gone out.

Zofia's ears began to ring.

She had read the file — twice, at her desk, with the lamp angled so the shine of the paper would not give her away to the woman at the other desk. It said: *Żurawski, Michał, s. Stanisława i Anny, ur. 1919, Świerczyna pow. Mława. Święcenia: 1952. Rektor kościoła akademickiego św. Anny od 1956.* And further down, in the small typed letters of a report: *Czynnik opiniotwórczy w środowisku akademickim. Nastawienie: nieustępliwe w sprawach katechezy.*

She had read the file and she had not known him. The photographs in it were bad: a gaunt man in a black coat crossing a courtyard, his hat pulled down. There were three Żurawskis in the Warsaw telephone book. She had noticed nothing.

Now, in the pulpit, he began to preach, and she understood how an intelligent woman could hold a file in her hands and not know that the man in it was the boy who had put his coat around her shoulders in 1938.

His voice had not changed. The voice had aged the way floorboards age, but underneath it was the same — low, a little hoarse, with a Mazovian softness in the vowels he had tried to file down in the seminary and not entirely managed. He said: "They came from the East, and they asked where the child was born."

Epiphany. Of course. She had chosen the hour because the church would be full and she could be nobody in a crowd, and it had not occurred to her that the sermon would be about the Magi.

"They had a star," he said. "They had a learned tradition, and a caravan, and probably a map. But when they came into Jerusalem, into the city where the wise men were, the star was not enough. They had to ask. *Where is he who is born king?* They had to ask the people who did not want to tell them."

"Because the king they were looking for was not a king anyone in Jerusalem wanted," said the priest. "Herod heard about a child, and he was afraid. Not of the child. Of the child's existence. A single child, lying in the straw, and the whole machinery of the state was afraid of him." He paused. He looked out at the nave, and for a second — a second is a very long time, when a man is looking — his eyes came to the back of the church and stopped.

Zofia did not move. She had learned not to move in rooms where a man with a folder was watching.

"And then they were warned in sleep not to return to Herod, and they went back to their own country by another road. Not by the road they came. By another road. They had brought gold, and frankincense, and myrrh, and they went home the long way, through passes they did not know, because the road they knew belonged to a king who wanted to kill a child."

His voice dropped. "Every one of us has a road he came in by. Every one of us has a house he cannot go back to by the same way. That is not a tragedy. That is the Gospel. It is the only instruction the Holy Family ever gave to the wise: go home, but go home another way."

Zofia left before the offertory. She went down the steps into the cold, and the man who had been standing by the pillar in a grey cap fell in behind her, twenty yards back, and did not catch up.

There was a black Warszawa parked on the curb on the wrong side, where no car could legally park and any car could, in those days, park, if the man who got out of it looked like Krzysiek.

"The Minister wants you at eleven."

"I know what the Minister wants."

"Then get in, I'm freezing."

He drove badly and fast, as all the ministry drivers did. Through the windscreen the city went by in flat white light: the ruins along Marszałkowska, green with frost; the new frontages with their scaffolding; the Palace of Culture with its crown of ice; a queue outside a shop on Krucza that had formed, she knew, at five in the morning.

The Office was a stone building on a side street off Marszałkowska, with a courtyard where the cars were kept and a boarhound called Pastuch who had been a gift from the Ministry of Public Security in 1951 and had bitten a courier in 1952 and was thereafter kept chained to a wall. In the corridor the big portraits hung: Bierut at the top, and below him the men who had come after his portrait learned to hang itself in the spring of 1956. The lower portraits had been rehung so often that the wall behind them was a map of rectangular ghosts.

"Tosia," she said, coming into the outer office. "Has the post come?"

"Twelve letters, a summons from the prosecutor's office about the Silesian proselytes, and his Excellency the Minister's assistant telephoned twice." Tosia Grabska was sixty, had been a teacher before the war, had lost her husband and her son in 1944, and had worked for Zofia for six years, in the course of which she had learned to say *his Excellency the Minister* in a tone that could strip paint. "And the electric kettle has been promised."

"The kettle has been promised since November."

"He has promised it for December. He does not say which year."

Zofia drank the tea, which was made the way they made it in the Office, strong, with a slice of lemon that came from a jar and tasted of the jar, and opened the folder on her desk.

It was marked *Poufne — do wglądu w gabinecie* — confidential, to be read in the office — and it had come from the Ministry of Public Security through the liaison, a Major Stolarczyk, who coughed when he was afraid of a document. Inside were eleven pages. The first was a list.

The Primate had sent a list to Rome in the last week of December, and Rome had not answered and would not for months, because Rome in January 1958 was a dying man at Castel Gandolfo and a Secretariat of State afraid to move. The list had five names. Four were bishops, veterans of the German camps, whose weaknesses the Ministry had catalogued like the answers to a crossword puzzle. The fifth was not a bishop at all:

*k. Michał Żurawski.*

Under it, in pencil, a clerk had written: *najmłodszy od 1816? — sprawdzić.* Youngest since 1816 — check.

Zofia sat with the list in her hand for a long time.

Then she opened the second folder, the one on the parish of St. Anne, signed out of the archive in October for the building permit: a transcript of Father Żurawski's sermon of the first Sunday in Advent, and a carbon of a letter he had written to the Presidium about the catechetical rooms, in which he had used the words *nasze dzieci* — our children — four times.

At the bottom of the third page the clerk had written: *Mówi jak człowiek, nie boli go to, że go słuchają — patrz: ton. Ocena: trudny.* He talks like a man, and it does not hurt him that he is being listened to. Assessment: difficult.

She pressed the intercom.

"Tosia. The rector of St. Anne's has been asking for an appointment about the parish hall. Has he been given one?"

"The twelfth," said Tosia.

"Move him to today."

"Today." Tosia paused, and in the pause Zofia could hear everything Tosia had learned in four occupations and twenty-five years. "At what hour?"

"Three."

"It's half past eleven."

"Then he can come at three."

She spent until three reading the eleven pages twice, and at twenty to three she stood at the window of the corridor, which looked out over the courtyard where Pastuch lay in a wheel rut with his nose in his tail, and watched a man in a black coat come through the gate, stamp the snow off his boots, and touch his hat to the guard.

He came up the stairs slowly. She heard him ask the way at the landing, and heard the soldier of the Internal Security Corps who did the reception answer with the exaggerated politeness he used for priests, an irony that would have been serious in 1952 and was only stupidity in 1958.

She went into her office, shut the door, and sat behind her desk with her hands on the desk where he would see them.

He came in with his hat in his hand. And the thing she had not prepared for, the thing no file had told her, was that the years had not destroyed him; they had narrowed him. He was tall and thin, and the thinness was the thinness of the camp and had never entirely left. His face had been broken at the nose, long ago, and had healed in a way that made it grave rather than ugly. The collar was too big for his neck. His eyes went to her face and stayed there, quiet and unsurprised, and his mouth made the small involuntary movement of a man who has walked into a room expecting a stranger and found the only person in the world.

"Pani director," he said.

"Father." She indicated the chair. "Please."

They sat. The clock on the wall behind her was loud. She had owned it since 1949 and had never before heard it.

"It's about the hall," he said.

"It's about the hall."

"The parish hall at the back of the churchyard. We applied in September, and in October a commission came and said the drainage of the site was not provided for, and the engineer who provides the drainage says the plan of the street precludes the drainage, and the plan of the street was laid down by the Ministry of the Interior in 1952, and the Ministry of the Interior tells me to ask the Ministry of Education, and the Ministry of Education tells me to ask you. Where the chain of command ends, pani director, is where the Catholic Church begins." He set his hat on his knee. "I have two hundred and thirty-one children in catechesis. I have them in the church, in the sacristy, in a room where the parish organist used to live, and in the corridors when it is raining. It has been raining since 1945."

"Two hundred and thirty-one."

"Four came on Monday."

"From what schools?"

He looked at her. "Pani director knows the schools."

She did, better than he did. School No. 29 on Bednarska had been a convent school before the war and had a state pedagogy council that would not permit the use of its premises; the new school on the corner of Dobra had a director who permitted anything that could not be proven. The children came at four, after the shop windows were lit, with their exercise books under their arms, and some were the children of men who had been in the Party since 1944, because in this matter the Party's children were exactly like everyone else's.

"Why have you come to me?" she said. "The Office did not refuse you. The Office asked for the drainage."

"The Office asked three times, and each time for a different reason. The Office has not said yes and the Office has not said no. I have been a priest for six years, pani director. In six years I have learned the difference between a minister who says no and a minister who says *we will see.*"

The word he used was *zobaczymy.* They both heard it hang in the room.

"And what do you think I can do?"

"You can say yes."

"Can I?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's why I'm asking."

She took a sheet of paper and wrote on it, in her neat small hand, a name and a department and a number, and turned it so that it faced him.

"This is the clerk at the Municipal Board who signs for the drainage. Go to him on Monday at nine. The site will be inspected the following Thursday and the drainage will be found to be in order. Your hall will be built."

He looked at the paper. He did not touch it.

"In exchange for what?"

"Does it have to be something?"

"It has always been something."

"Then let me save us both a winter," said Zofia, "and tell you that no one in this building has asked me for anything in your matter. And when no one asks you for anything, Father, it usually means that the thing you want is a hole in the ground, and the question is not what it costs, but who is standing at the edge. I will tell you what it costs, since you insist. It costs me nothing yet. It will cost me later. Everything in this building is paid for later."

"That's a very good answer," he said. "Who taught you to talk like that?"

"Warsaw."

"Ah." He looked at the paper on the desk. "I knew a girl in Warsaw once. She would have answered the same."

Zofia heard the clock.

"Father Żurawski," she said. "You were born in Świerczyna, in the powiat of Mława, the son of Stanisław and Anna, and baptized in the parish church there by Father Ludwik Sokołowski, who died in the prison at Wronki in 1955 — I am telling you this so that you know what I already know, and so that we do not waste each other's time. You were a bell-ringer at that church. You were taken in the roundup at Mława in April 1943, and photographed, and sent to Oświęcim, and you have the number on your forearm, and you keep your left cuff buttoned even in the summer. You had a sister called Jadzia, who died in 1929, and a brother called Tomasz, who was taken to the Reich in 1939 and did not come home. Your father was Stanisław, a miller, who told four men from the Security Service in 1946 that he did not know where his son was, and was telling the truth."

The room was very quiet.

"And what else does the file say?" said Michał.

"It says that in 1938 there was a girl," said Zofia, "and that her name was Zofia Wierzbicka, and that she is sitting on the other side of this desk."

* * *

What happened next took eleven minutes by the clock, and the minutes do not exist, because Zofia did not make any.

He did not stand up. She had planned for him to stand up; she had lain awake the night before and planned for the shouting and for the terrible, necessary dignity of an old friend who walks out of an office. He sat with his hat on his knee and looked at her, and the look had in it neither accusation nor the tenderness she had been afraid of. It was the look of a man reading a document he had been sent at last, and finding it shorter than he had expected.

"Your file," he said. "The one in this building. Has it got a photograph?"

"A photograph from 1952."

"Not of me. Of you."

She did not answer.

"There is a photograph in your file," said Michał. "I have not seen it. But I know it exists, because there is a file on every man and woman in this building, and a file on the files. What does it look like?"

"You know what it looks like."

"I want you to tell me."

"It is a woman of thirty-three in a dark dress with a white collar, standing against a wall with a measuring scale on it," said Zofia. "It is not a flattering photograph. The scale is behind her head. It makes her look as though she has been arrested."

"That is what a photograph is," said Michał. "It is a thing that has been arrested." He turned his hat over. "Zosia. I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it as though you were answering it in the confessional, which is the only room in Warsaw where the answer does not go into a file."

"You have no right to ask me in the confessional."

"No," he agreed. "I have no right at all. I am asking as a man who was in the loft of a mill in the summer of 1941 with a girl who told him she was going to be somebody who decides what happens." He looked up. "Did you read my file before you read the list, or after?"

"After," she said.

"Ah."

"I read the list on the eighth of January. It had your name on it. I read the file the same evening, after the office was empty, until three in the morning." She looked at him. "I did not know it was you. That is not a lie and not a defense. I had read your file before, in 1952, at a meeting, and I had not known it was you. I read the name and did not see it."

"But now you have seen it."

"Yes."

"And what are you going to do?"

"That is not a question a man asks a woman in an office," said Zofia, and her voice was level and hard, "and if you ask it again I will have you taken out of the building. Not because you are a priest. Because you have asked me to be a girl in a loft, and I am the deputy director of the Catholic section of the Office for Religious Denominations, and there are eleven people in this building who would like my chair, and one of them is listening at the door."

Michał looked at the door. He did not look away from her face to do it; his eyes moved, and came back.

"Then let us talk about the drainage," he said.

And they did.

He told her about the roof, the tiles, the glazier, the inspector who had not come. She asked the cost of the tiles and he told her, in złoty, without hesitation, and she wrote it down. She asked why the numbers in catechesis had gone up, and he said, "Because in October 1956 I told the parents that anyone who sent a child to the state's ethics class in place of the catechism would answer to me, and the parents believed me, and the state did not."

"That is a threat," said Zofia.

"It is a promise," said Michał.

She wrote that down too.

"In the camp," he said, "the clerk who wrote the report was tired, and the officer who read it was afraid of his superior, and the superior was afraid of Berlin. Give a man one document he can put in front of his superior without being beaten and he will love you, and he will do the thing. That is not a theology. It is a miller's view of the world. The stone grinds whatever is put into it. You cannot stop the stone. You can choose the grain."

Zofia was silent.

"That is what you believe," she said.

"That is what I believe."

"Then we believe the same thing," she said, "and we have believed it for fifteen years, on opposite sides of a door, and neither of us has ever been able to say it out loud to anyone in this city who would not write it down."

He stood at the door with his hand on the handle.

"Friday," she said. "At ten. Bring the inventory."

"Pani director," said Michał, and the title was a wall, and he stood behind it, "the boy in the third row. His name is Piotr. He is fifteen. His father was killed in the war and his mother works at the telephone exchange. On Sunday he asked me whether it was a sin to be afraid."

Zofia did not answer, and he went out.

* * *

She watched him cross the courtyard in the snow. The dog Pastuch lifted his head and did not bark. The gate closed behind him, and the snow went on falling in the flat white light, filling his footprints the way it fills everything in this city, patiently, from above.

Then she opened the door, and Tosia was at the typewriter.

"Tosia. The rector of St. Anne's is coming on Friday. Enter it twice. Once for the drainage. Once for the roof." She stood in the doorway. "Did anyone come to this floor in the last hour?"

"The Minister's assistant, to say his Excellency will be late."

"Anyone else?"

Tosia looked at her over the typewriter with the flat black eyes of a woman who had been a teacher before the war and had lost her husband and her son and had decided, some time in 1945, to be useful in the only way left.

"There is a man at the end of the corridor," Tosia said. "He has been there since two. He has a briefcase, and he has not come in, and he is not from the Ministry, because the Ministry's men cough when they come up the stairs and this one does not."

Zofia went out. The man was at the end of the corridor, by the window, in a good grey coat with the collar turned up.

"Colonel Wesoły," she said.

"Pani director." He turned. He was forty-three, with a broad face and the mild eyes of a schoolmaster, and he had been in the Ministry of Public Security since 1944, and he had a wife and two daughters in Żoliborz, and he played chess on Sundays with a priest from the parish of St. Stanisław Kostka. "I was in the building on other business and I thought I would wait for you."

"You have a briefcase."

"I always have a briefcase."

"What is in it?"

"A photograph," said Wesoły, "which I think you have seen, and a question, which I will ask you on Monday at the Ministry. It is a small question. It is about a student who was in the church on the sixth of January with a camera inside his coat, and who is now in the cells on Rakowiecka, and who has told us a thing about the priest of St. Anne's that I would like to discuss with you before I write it down."

"If it is not written down, it is not evidence."

"Everything is evidence," said Colonel Wesoły. "The only question is who is holding it."

He touched his hat, and walked away down the corridor.

* * *

The flat was in Mokotów, two rooms on the third floor of a building built in 1936 for clerks of the Ministry of Posts and requisitioned in 1945 by the same ministry, which had given her the flat because she had asked in 1951, in writing, with three reasons, of which the third was *służba państwowa* — state service — and the first two had been true.

She had a kitchen table with a good lamp on it, a shelf of books, a photograph of her mother in a frame, and no telephone, because a telephone in the flat was a window that could not be curtained, and being careful had kept her alive.

On the shelf above the stove, on a saucer, stood the lamp.

It was silver, the length of a hand, with a round bowl and a wick and a ring at the top for a chain. It had hung in the parish church at Świerczyna for a hundred and forty years, before the side altar of the Mother of God, and in January 1945, when the retreating Germans fired the church and it burned with the village, her mother — who had been in the village that winter, because Warsaw was a ruin and a woman had to be somewhere — had gone into the burning church and taken the lamp down off its chain and carried it out wrapped in a horse blanket.

Her mother had given it to her in 1946, when Zofia came north for a week, and had said: *It is not ours. When there is a church again, take it back.* Zofia had carried it to Warsaw in a satchel and put it on a shelf, and in 1947 she had bought a bottle of lamp oil, and in 1948 she had lit it for the first time, on the sixth of January, the day of the Epiphany.

The sixth of January was the day the Germans had come to Świerczyna in 1940, and taken the parish silver and the registers and eleven men. It was also the day, in 1938, when the whole village had come to the Epiphany Mass in the cold, with the lamp burning before the side altar, and Zosia had stood at the back with Michał, and he had held her hand inside his coat pocket. She had been carrying that coincidence for thirteen years: the day of the light and the day of the taking, the same square on the calendar.

She lit the lamp.

She put it on the kitchen table and sat down opposite it with a glass of tea and her coat still on, and watched the small flame stand up straight in the cold air of the flat.

It was not a prayer. She had stopped praying in 1944, in the forest, and the stopping had gone on so long it had become a practice. The lamp was not a prayer. The lamp was a bill. Every year on the sixth of January she lit it and let it burn all night, and in the morning she looked at the black ring it left on the ceiling, and she paid something, and the payment was not specified, and no one had ever told her what it was for.

At nine the telephone in the corridor rang — the caretaker's telephone, which pani Kubiak answered and which was, in the building on Górnośląska Street, the village well — and pani Kubiak knocked at the door and said the Minister's office had called, and would she come at eight in the morning, not eleven.

"Thank you, pani Kubiak."

"Cold night."

"Cold night."

She went back to the table. The flame had not moved.

For a long time she sat and looked at it, and at some point after ten she said, out loud, in the empty kitchen, in the voice she used for the Office, the voice that could make a clerk put down his pen:

"No."

The flame burned.

"A bishop," she said. "They want to make him a bishop."

The flame burned, straight and small and steady, with the black ring of the year before still printed on the ceiling above it, and the year before that faintly visible through it, a smudge of smoke on white plaster, the record of a debt paid in the only currency she had left.

She did not turn it down. At midnight she got up and put a glass of water by the bed and checked the lock on the door, and in the morning the flame was still going, and there was a new ring on the ceiling, and she looked at it for a long moment before she put on her coat and went out to save what could be saved.

# Chapter Two: The Chronicle

*Świerczyna, 1926–1945*

The bell in the river was a story the children were not supposed to repeat.

In the Swedish wars, three hundred years ago, the people of Świerczyna had carried their church bell down to the Mławka to hide it from the horsemen, and the raft had turned over in the spring flood, and the bell had gone down in eight feet of black water at the bend below the mill. The old people said it rang sometimes, under the water, when the ice was thick and the river low. The children believed it the way they believed that the miller's daughter had once turned into a hare. It was a good story, and it explained the crack in the village bell, which is real: the bell was cast in 1658 out of the metal of an older one, and it was dropped on the day of its hanging by a man named Szymon Wrona, who is the reason that in Świerczyna every mistake, however small, is still called *a Wrona*.

Zofia was six in the summer that she met Michał Żurawski, and the meeting took place at the top of a bell tower, because she had climbed the two hundred and six steps of it to see the world and he was already there, being sick.

"It's the bell," he said, when he could speak. "It goes right through your chest."

"You should not stand under it when he pulls the rope. You have to stand over here." She showed him. "The rope hangs here and the bell is up there and the sound goes out. Why did you come all the way up if you knew it would make you sick?"

"Because from the top you can see the river to the bend."

"You can see that from the ground."

"From the ground," said Michał, with the reasonable air of a boy who had already decided what kind of boy he was going to be, "you cannot see the river, because there is a village in the way."

That was the whole of it. At the top of the tower, where the wind came off the fields and the pigeons had made a floor of droppings and the bell hung enormous and quiet and older than the church, two children discovered that they had both, all their lives, wanted to see the river on the far side of the village. It did not seem, then, like much to have in common.

* * *

Świerczyna in 1938 was a village of sixty-one houses, a wooden church painted yellow in 1921, a mill, a post office that shared a room with the co-operative, a dairy that made a cheese no one in the village could afford, a school with two teachers and one stove, and a river that was wide enough in April to drown a cow and narrow enough in August to cross on the stones without wetting your knees.

Zofia's father, Jan Wierzbicki, taught the older classes at the school and played the organ at the church. He had been a soldier against the Bolsheviks and had come home with a limp, a medal he never wore, and a set of opinions. He was a Socialist of the old kind: he believed the Polish state, which had imprisoned him for three weeks in 1920 for making a speech, would one day belong to schoolteachers and dairy co-operators and to no one else. After Mass on Sundays he would argue with Father Ludwik on the church steps about whether the Resurrection was a fact or a symbol, exactly as long as it took his wife to make him come home.

Her mother, Anna, was from a farm on the other side of the river and had no opinions, which is to say she had a thousand of them and expressed none. She made the best sour rye bread in the parish, kept the accounts of the dairy, and on the first of April every year told her daughters the same thing: *Today you may be a fool, but do not be a fool with your mouth open.*

Zofia had a sister, Róża, older by two years, who was quick and funny and could haggle a trader into tears, and who made the village address her as if she were the elder, which in law she was.

The Żurawskis had the mill.

It was a wooden mill with a wheel replaced in 1904, on a stone race below the village, and it ground the parish's grain for a fixed share that Stanisław Żurawski, Michał's father, described as the smallest any miller in Poland had ever taken. Michał's mother, Ania, was a bell-ringer's daughter and could sing anything from a lullaby to the *Bogurodzica*. His brother Tomasz was older by five years and had a way with horses. There had been a third child, a girl called Jadzia, who died in the influenza in 1929; her photograph hung over the door of the mill kitchen, and on her name day nobody worked.

* * *

The years before the war were poor in the way a farmer is poor: not hungry, but never anything over. There was a good harvest in 1937, and the price of grain in 1938 was a scandal; the farmers sold the wheat and bought the flour back. The children lived on the surface of all that, the way you live on the surface of water in summer, and the surface was warm.

Zofia and Michał had three rules and a meeting place. The place was the gallery behind the bell, where the ladder came up out of the dark and the roof opened on a slope of grey tiles and you could climb out and sit on the corner of the cornice with your legs hanging over the village. The rules were: no lying; everything goes in the chronicle; and when one of us is in trouble, the other comes. The chronicle was a copybook that Michał had stolen from the school and Zofia had decorated on the cover with the drawing of a bell and the words *KRONIKA ŚWIERCZYNY, A.D. 1937*.

They wrote everything in it.

*10 April 1937. The first swallows. Roma K. says spring is here but she also said that in March.*

*3 August 1937. Found a helmet in the fields with a hole in it. J.W. says it is from the 1920 year and we are not to bring it into the house. We are keeping it in the loft.*

*19 December 1937. Michał fell through the ice by the mill and I pulled him out and he would not let go of the bag with the flour in it and it is all ruined. He says he would have let go of the flour before he let go of me. I have written it down so that he can never take it back.*

They were seventeen and eighteen in 1937, and other things were happening on the surface, though not the ones in the chronicle. There was the night of the harvest festival, when they sat on the mill roof until three in the morning and Michał put his coat around her shoulders and neither of them said anything for so long that they began to laugh from nerves.

They were not in love, or not only in love, or not in the way the word was used in the village. They were in something older and more serious than love, which is to say they were in the habit of each other. They had been each other's witness since the tower.

* * *

In December 1938 the parish gave its annual vigil for the feast of the Holy Family, and the Wierzbickis attended it in force, because Jan played the organ and the choir needed Anna's alto.

The church that night was full to the door. The men stood at the back with their caps in their hands, and Zofia, who had been home from the school in Mława for a year and was tall for it, was with the girls' sodality in the second row from the front, and Michał was in the loft. At the end, when the light had been lowered and Jan had begun the organ for the *Te Deum*, some of the men at the back turned toward the door.

There was a woman in the doorway with a child on her hip and a bundle in her other arm. She stood in the cold and did not come in, because she was not from the village, and everybody there knew it by her coat; if she had come in nobody would have had to be unkind to her, she would simply have stood in the aisle and the men would have looked at the floor.

Zofia turned to look for her mother, and her mother was already looking at her, and nodded.

So Zofia went down the length of the aisle, in her confirmation shoes, past the second row and the third row and the pews of the people she had known her whole life, and she took the woman's bundle — it was a sack of potatoes, and the woman was a widow from Grzybowo, three miles off, with a child on her hip and nowhere to sleep — and carried it for her to the porch, where a man of the parish arranged a bed for her in the co-operative's loft.

That is all that happened. That is the whole of it. A girl of eighteen carried a sack of potatoes.

But on the way back up the aisle the organ was at the height of the *Te Deum*, and Zofia lifted her head to find her place, and in the loft above the last pew Michał was standing at the rail with the bell rope in his hands, and he was not looking at the priest or the choir or the door; he was looking at her, and his face was the face of somebody who has seen something and knows it is not a story.

Her mother said afterward, in the kitchen, with the tea: *You took her bundle. That is the whole of the faith, Zosia. Everything else is the noise around it.*

And Zofia, who was eighteen and had never in her life contradicted her mother, said: *Then why is the church so full of noise?*

* * *

They met afterward at the top of the tower, where they always met, and Zofia, who was cold, had tears in her eyes from the wind, and this is what was said.

"I saw you," said Michał.

"You were supposed to be watching the priest."

"The bell rings itself when the priest says the right word. Everyone knows that." He put his coat around her, which was now a ritual, and it smelled of horse and flour and cold iron. "Zosia. When I am older I am going to be somebody."

"You are somebody now."

"Not that kind of somebody. I mean somebody they listen to. My father says a miller is a man nobody looks at twice. I would like to be looked at. Not the way they look at the count or the priest. The way they look at the man who says what happens."

"Then you will have to be a good man."

"I will be a good man," said Michał, "and also a great one. Both. There is no law against it."

Zofia looked at the village spread below them, its roofs under the snow, the mill race making its black line through the white, the river a dark thread to the bend, and beyond the river the fields, and beyond the fields the dark.

"I am going to be somebody too," she said. "But not somebody who says what happens. Somebody who decides it."

He did not laugh at her. That was the thing she remembered all her life. He looked at her with an expression she had never seen on a boy's face before and would see perhaps three times again, and said, seriously, as though discussing the price of grain:

"Then we shall have to be on the same side."

They were silent.

"They say you can hear the bell in the river on Christmas Eve," Zofia said at last. "If you go to the bend at midnight and lie down flat, and put your ear to the ice, and the ice is thick and the river is low, then you hear it."

"That's for finding out who you'll marry," said Michał. "Róża says it's for finding out who you'll marry."

Michał looked out at the river.

"On Christmas Eve," he said. "Midnight. The bend. If you come, I will come."

"If you come, I will come."

They had no idea that they were making a promise. They thought they were making a plan.

* * *

The night of the vigil, when the boy went down from the loft to walk the girl home along the mill race, there was a man standing at the parish gate.

He was a man of forty or so, in a good grey coat with the collar turned up, and he was not from the village. He had come on the Mława road in a car with a driver, and he stood at the gate in the snow with a cigarette and watched the people coming out of the church, with the face of a man who was used to watching people come out of churches. When Zofia and Michał passed him he took out a small notebook and wrote in it, without any hurry, the way a man writes down a thing he has been expecting.

"Who's he?" said Michał.

"The paper?"

"Doesn't look like the paper."

"Then he's the tax office," said Zofia, "and he's writing down that you own a horse you haven't declared."

"He wouldn't be writing. He'd be rubbing his hands."

They laughed, and went on, and behind them the man in the grey coat closed his notebook and stood in the snow looking after them, and no one in the village ever learned his name.

* * *

The Christmas Eve came. There was twelve dishes at supper and a place set for the dead, and hay under the cloth, and Zofia's father read the Gospel of Matthew in a voice too loud for the room, and her mother said the same thing she said every year about the carp.

At half past eleven, Zofia got up and put on her coat and her boots. Her mother was in the kitchen with the pots.

"Where are you going?"

"Róża is going to give me the new ribbon."

It was not a good lie. Anna put down the pot and looked at her daughter for a long time, in the light of the kitchen lamp, with the snow at the window and the organ of the church just audible on the hill.

"You are eighteen," said her mother.

"I will be back by one."

"You will be back by one," agreed her mother, and turned back to the pots, and Zofia went out into the snow, and it was not until years later, in a cellar in Warsaw, that she understood that her mother had let her go, and that letting her go had been a kind of blessing.

The bend was a quarter of an hour's walk along the road and across the frozen meadow, and the ice was thick — it had been a hard December — and the world was so white and the sky so clear that the village seemed lit from inside.

Michał was there. He had been there since eleven.

He had brought a blanket and a bottle of the plum brandy from the mill's cellar, and she had never tasted anything so awful, and they passed it back and forth, and their ears got warm, and the stars were enormous, and neither of them could think of anything to say.

Then they lay down on the ice, flat on their bellies, side by side, their cheeks on the ice and their coats over their heads, and they waited.

"Do you hear it?"

"I hear the river."

"That's not the river. The river is frozen."

"Then it's my heartbeat."

"That's your stomach. You had twelve dishes."

The ice creaked. A dog barked somewhere and was answered. The cold came up through the wool of their coats into the bones of their chests, and Zofia, sixteen inches from his face, could see the moon in the ice and the moon in his eye.

Then, from below them, deep and slow and remote, as though it had come up through the water from a cathedral at the bottom of the world, there was a sound.

They lay without breathing.

It came again, and it was a bell.

"That's not the bell in the river," Michał whispered. "That's the church bell. They ring it at midnight."

"They ring it at midnight," said Zofia.

"Then it isn't the story."

"No."

They lay there on the ice for a while.

"So it doesn't tell you who you'll marry," said Michał.

"It's a very bad bell," said Zofia.

And then he turned his head, and his face was very close, and he said, in a voice that was not the voice of the miller's son but something low and steady and much older: "I know who I'll marry anyway."

The bell rang again, over the village, and the ice carried the sound in a long shudder, and Zofia turned her face away and looked at the sky and said:

"Well, then you had better not get me drunk."

* * *

The chronicle entry was written the next morning, in the loft, in a hand trying to be casual:

*25 December 1938. Zosia and I lay on the ice at the bend and heard the bell. It was only the church bell. The story is a lie. This is a great disappointment to K. Wróbel. We are closing the chronicle for the winter. Nothing else will happen.*

* * *

But something else happened at Easter, and I have to put it down, because it is the reason the curse works.

It was April 1939, and the parish was whitewashing the church for the feast, and the men were up on the tower with long poles, and Michał was with them because he was the bell-ringer's heir. Zofia was below with the women, cooking for the men, and Róża was there with her young man from Mława, Wacek, and everything was as it should be.

The men on the tower had rigged a pulley to swing out a board, and the board came loose and fell. It fell on a small boy — Kuba, the son of the co-operative's clerk, seven years old — and laid his head open, and the whole yard went white with his mother screaming.

Michał came down the tower with his hands already red.

They carried Kuba to the house of the district nurse, and Zofia held the mother's hand for two hours in a room where a boy of seven lay with a bandage round his head and did not wake.

He died in the morning. The bell rang that afternoon, because the village had to be told.

At the funeral, the mother did not cry. She stood at the graveside with her hands folded and her face turned up to the priest, and when the priest had done, she walked to Michał where he stood with the bell-ringer's family and the men who had rigged the pulley, and she said, calmly, so that the whole front of the church heard it:

"You are the boy who shakes the bell. May God let you hear it every day of your life, and may it never be for you anything but a funeral. May the bell of this parish ring for everyone you love before it rings for you."

It was a curse. People in the parish called it something else — they called it the widow's blessing, because it was said at a graveside — and the priest said afterward, in the sacristy, that it was not a curse, that grief was not a sin, and that the woman would be ashamed of it in a week and would take him a share of the milk every Saturday for a year. This turned out to be true: she took the milk, and had a place laid for Michał at her table every year on the boy's name day, and never once spoke to him of it.

But the village kept the words. Villages keep what they hear at a grave.

And Michał, who had carried the boy up the hill and whose hands had been red to the wrists, went up the tower that evening and sat with his back against the bell and did not come down until the third day. Zofia brought him bread and sat with him in the dust and the pigeon droppings, and he said:

"Zosia. If I ever become anything, I will be a thing that rings for other people and not for you. Do you understand? I want you to understand. I will not be a man who lives for his own bell."

And she said, because she was eighteen and did not know what else to say:

"Then I will be the bell, and you will be the river."

They laughed a little, in the dark tower, with the bell huge above them like a dropped sky.

* * *

Summer came.

There was the last summer, though nobody called it that. There was the harvest, and the fair at Mława, and Róża's engagement to Wacek announced at the fair with a good deal of noise and one broken chair. In August, Warsaw announced that the Germans were demanding a corridor, and Zofia's father stopped playing the organ after Mass and started going into Mława to meetings, and one night at supper he said, "There will be a war," and Anna said, "Eat your soup," and he said, "Anna," and she said, "Eat your soup, Jan."

On the twenty-ninth of August the women of the parish were called to the church to sew bandages for the army at Mława. Zofia was in the porch holding the linen while her mother turned the hems, and Michał came in with a message for the priest from the commune, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at her, and the whole porch saw him look at her, and the older women smiled and did not stop what their hands were doing.

On the thirty-first of August the mobilisation was announced from the loudspeakers on the post office. Zofia's father had a summons as a reservist, but he was lame, and was sent to the evacuation of the school records instead. Tomasz Żurawski went to Mława on the first of September and did not come home.

On the first of September, at a quarter to five in the morning, the war began.

There is nothing in the chronicle about September 1939. It ends on the thirty-first of August, in Michał's hand:

*31 August 1939. They have moved the horses out of the mill to Mława. Father says we are to burn the grain in the loft if it comes to it, and Mother says things do not come to it. There is a moon like a knife. Zosia is at the church with the bandages. I am going to the church. I have a thing to say to her and I am going to say it tonight before anything else happens.*

He did not say it that night. The planes came over at dawn.

---

* * *

By the third week of September the front had gone through Świerczyna twice: first the Poles going back, then the Germans going forward, and it was a week after that before anyone could count what was lost. The mill had no wheel. The school had no windows. The church had its roof and no door. The village had sixteen dead: nine soldiers, four Jews from Mława who had come out on the road, one old man who had not moved fast enough out of a field, and two children who had been playing in a barn when the strafing came.

Tomasz Żurawski had been at Mława and had not been at Mława, and through the autumn the family had no word. In November they heard, by way of a man who had been at Działdowo, that he had been taken in the roundup after the battle and was in a camp in the Reich. In January there was a letter in his hand, three sentences, and then nothing for two years.

The Germans came to Świerczyna on the sixth of January 1940, in two cars and a truck, and took from the rectory the parish registers and the church silver, from the school the maps and the globe and every book with the word *Polska* in it, from the mill the last of the rye, and from the village eleven men. They were going to take the priest, but Father Ludwik had gone to Mława the evening before, and he did not come back to Świerczyna for fifteen years, and when he did it was to be buried.

They did not take Jan Wierzbicki, because a German officer had made a note of him as a schoolteacher and a Socialist, and a schoolteacher and a Socialist is more useful to a German over the long term than a schoolteacher and a Socialist in the ground. He was told to open the school and teach the children German, and that the Vistula was a German river, and he did it every day in a room without windows, with a map that had no Poland on it, and came home every evening and played the piano very loudly until it was dark.

* * *

The letters began in the summer of 1940.

There was a priest in Mława who had started a school in the cellar of his church for the children whose schools had been closed, and he asked the man at the mill whether he had a boy who could carry letters. Michał was twenty-one. He carried letters between Mława and the villages to the north, in the lining of a cap, in a sack of flour, once under the water at the mouth of the mill race — he came up black and gasping and laughing, and Zofia hit him with both fists in the chest and then held him so hard that he could not breathe.

The letters were for people in the camps, for people in hiding, for people who had gone east in 1939 and had not been heard of. They were also, in a way no one could have said aloud in 1940 and the children knew anyway, the whole of Poland: a river of paper under the roads the Germans used, carrying the country's words from house to house.

Michał was good at it. He was quick and quiet and had a miller's face, the face of a boy who has been in the loft all his life and cannot possibly be up to anything. Zofia was better: nobody stopped a girl carrying a basket to her grandmother. Róża did it too, and Róża, who could talk herself into a German barracks and out again, was the best of all.

At night, when the letters were safe and the curfew had begun, Zofia and Michał met in the loft above the mill, which had no grain in it now, with the chronicle between them.

*4 March 1941. Z. carried the letters to Dąbrowa in a basket with eggs on top. Two German vehicles on the Mława road. Nothing happened. R. says the egg trick is good for a year more, then they will start eating the eggs.*

*17 May 1941. They have taken the Jews from Mława to a camp in the Reich. Father will not talk about it. I do not have the words for the chronicle. I am leaving a space.*

They were not children anymore, and what was between them had grown teeth. They could not hold hands in the street, or be seen at the mill together. The whole village knew, and the village said nothing. *Saying nothing* was, in 1941, a form of wealth, and the village spent it freely when it was for those two.

In the loft they were not the village's and not the Germans'; they were the two people who had discovered at six and seven that the other was the witness of their life. Once, in August 1941, he kissed her, and it was the first time, and the surprise was not that it happened but that it had taken so long.

"Zosia," he said. "When it is over."

"When it is over," she said.

"I am going to say a thing tonight because the war is teaching me that if you wait for a better night you do not get one. When it is over, I am going to ask your father for you. He will say no. He has wanted you to be a schoolteacher in Warsaw since you were six. But I have a mill."

"You have half a mill."

"I have half a mill," said Michał, "and I have a chronicle in which you have been in trouble and I have come, forty-one times since 1937. Zosia Wierzbicka, I have been in love with you since the day I was sick in a bell tower and a girl of six told me where to stand."

"That was you being sick."

"Perhaps I was sick from the beginning," he said, and she laughed and hit him, and he caught her hands and held them, and in the roof-light of the mill in the summer of 1941 they made each other a promise that did not require the bell.

* * *

In April 1943, three lorries of German police came into the market square at Mława and took five hundred people.

They took them because two German gendarmes had been killed on the road near Przasnysz, and in the arithmetic of the occupier five hundred Poles equalled two Germans. The people were taken to Działdowo, and from Działdowo to a camp in the Reich, and the camp was Auschwitz, and the numbering was done before they were tattooed, with a needle and a pen and a slow hand.

Michał was in Mława that day with a letter in his cap for a woman in the town, waiting at the tram terminus by the market because the contact was late, and when the lorries came into the square he should have gone into the church. He went into the church. The Germans went into the church. There are things a boy should not have to learn, and one of them is that a church is a building like any other.

They took the letter out of his cap and could not read it because it was in a mother's code, and they put it in a sack with the rest, and they put him on a lorry with a hundred men, and the war took him away from Świerczyna for two years and four months.

* * *

Zofia was not in Mława that day. Zofia was in the mill, because Anna Wierzbicka had been brought to bed with a fever, and Zofia was making the poultices.

She heard the lorries go by on the road. She heard them come back.

She went into Mława at five in the afternoon, on the road, with her mother's fever still in her ears, and she stood in the market square where the people stood, and where a woman told her that the Żurawski boy from Świerczyna had been taken with the lot, and where a German soldier sold cigarettes from a crate at the corner, and where the church was closed because there were people inside it.

She walked back to Świerczyna in the dark.

At the mill, Stanisław Żurawski was sitting in the kitchen with his hands on the table and his wife was standing at the window, and on the table between them were the chronicle and the boy's cap.

"Zosia," said Ania Żurawska. "Did you see him?"

"No."

"Then he is alive," said Ania, who was a bell-ringer's daughter and had learned the family trade of saying the thing that must be said in the voice that made it so. "If he is not in the square with his name on a list, he is alive. They will put him somewhere. We will find him."

They did not find him. In June a man who had been in Auschwitz and was released through some office of the Red Cross sent a card with a name on it — Żurawski, M. — and a number, 119,278, and a line of German that Stanisław read with a dictionary: *betrifft Familienangehörige* — concerning the family.

"Number 119,278," said Stanisław, at the table, reading it. "They have given him a number instead of a name."

"Then it is a name," said Ania Żurawska, "and it is written down, and they cannot take it back," and she took the card from her husband and put it in the family Bible, at the page with the births, and under the birth of Michał she wrote in pencil, in a small firm hand: *119278. Żyje.* He lives.

* * *

She went east in the spring of 1944, because the girl who carried letters in a basket could no longer carry letters in a basket, and because the boy with the number 119278 had been taken by the occupier, and the only people fighting the occupier in the east of Poland that spring were in the forest.

She was twenty-four. She had carried a sack of potatoes at eighteen and a rifle at twenty-four, and she did not believe in God, or thought she did not; it did not come up, in the forest.

She stayed in the forest nine months, and when she came out of it Warsaw had burned, and the German front had gone back through the villages three hundred miles wide, and the Red Army was on the other side of the Vistula.

In January 1945 she came back to Świerczyna.

The village was still there. The mill was still there. The church was not: the Germans had fired it on the way out, and there was a wall and a tower and nothing between them. Her mother was there, alive, in the house of her own mother. Her father was not.

* * *

It is in this part that the letter comes, and I am going to put it in whole, because the paper survives and I do not have the right to shorten it. It was written in July 1944 on a form with printed lines and a margin for the official stamp, sent to the parish office at Świerczyna, and received by Ania Żurawska in November 1945.

*To the Rev. Curia, Mława.*

*I am writing from the camp. I ask that this letter be delivered to my parents, Stanisław and Anna Żurawski, at the mill at Świerczyna, and that the curate read it to them, as they read slowly.*

*I am healthy. I weigh fifty-one kilos. They have asked us to write in German, and I will not; the censor has permitted this letter because a professor in the camp proved that it is in the public interest for a Pole to write in Polish, and he is a great professor and he will not survive. I am in the infirmary, which is the only place in the camp where a man may sit down.*

*The priest here is Father Leon. He says men in here are divided into two kinds, those who have lost their faith and those who have lost everything else. I am in the second kind, and it has made me quiet.*

*I have been thinking of the bell.*

*I want to write down that I am not sorry for the letters. If I had known in 1940 what the letters would cost, I would have carried them anyway. There is no arithmetic in which the letters cost more than they are worth.*

*There is a thing I need the curate to do. In the parish there is a girl, Zofia Wierzbicka, the schoolteacher's daughter. She is twenty-four, and she does not pray anymore. If she has not been taken, tell her that I am alive and that I am coming back, and tell her — the curate may read this aloud, he is a good man — that she is not to wait for me.*

*She is not to wait for me because a man in a camp is not a man but a husband of the dead, and the world the war ends into will not be the world we were promised on the ice, and I would rather she married a live man.*

*But if she asks whether I am alive, tell her yes, and tell her the number, 119278, and tell her that I have kept a chronicle in my head since 1943, and that she is in it, and that in it we are on the ice at the bend on the twenty-fourth of December 1938, and it is not the church bell that rings, and if that is a lie I have been living on it for a year and it tastes like bread.*

*Michał Żurawski*

*119278*

* * *

The curate read it to the Żurawskis in November 1945, in the kitchen of the mill, by the light of a lamp, and Stanisław Żurawski had to stop him twice because of what was in it, and Ania Żurawska did not stop him at all, and when it was read she put the letter in the Bible at the page of the births, on top of the card with the number on it, and shut the book.

The curate was a young man from Mława who had come to Świerczyna at the end of the war with a bicycle and a bag of altar linen and the news that the priest was in Wronki and would not be back. He said the Mass in the ruins with a board for an altar and the tower for a belfry, every day at six, which in January is before the light.

He had been told to find Zofia Wierzbicka. He found her in February, in the rectory, where she had come to arrange for the burial of her father.

The Germans had taken Jan Wierzbicki in November 1940 — not for the school and not for the piano, but because a man in Mława had denounced him for hiding a rifle, which was true, and they wanted his list of names. Whatever the list was, he did not give it. In 1946 the Polish Red Cross established that he had been shot on the second of December 1940, in the courtyard of Rakowiecka Prison, and buried in a field at Magdalenka with two hundred other men.

The curate told Zofia about the letter. He gave her the curate's copy, which is the one I have, a copy in a young man's careful hand.

She read it standing in the rectory, with her hat in her hand and her coat still buttoned.

"Is he alive?"

"In July he was alive. This letter is from July."

"July is seven months ago."

"Yes."

She folded the copy very small and put it in her pocket.

"Tell his mother," she said, "that I will not wait for him."

The curate looked at her.

"And tell her," she said, "that the last part of the letter is a lie. The last part is about the ice, and the ice is not true, and if he is alive I will tell him so myself."

She went out.

The curate stood in the doorway with the snow coming in and watched her go down the road in her good grey coat, and he thought — he told me this in 1969, over tea, and asked me not to use his name — *She is going to be very important, and she is already dead in the place where it matters, and no one will ever be able to tell her.*

# Chapter Three: Two Resurrections

*Warsaw, 1946–1952*

In the spring of 1946 Warsaw was a city built out of its own rubble. There were streets where the houses had been taken away to the level of the cellar and the streets themselves were gone, and men crossed them on planks. And there were other streets where the façades had been put back up against the ruins behind them like scenery in a theatre, and you could stand on the pavement and look through a doorway and see, thirty feet in, the sky.

Zofia Wierzbicka came to Warsaw in February 1946 with a Party card, a leather satchel, and a letter of introduction from the provincial committee in Mława to a man named Kosiński in the Department of Education.

She was twenty-six. She had spent a year in Świerczyna after the liberation: buried her father, nursed her mother through the fever that followed, watched the village argue about the wheat. And in April 1945 she had gone to the rectory in Mława and asked the curate, who had been in the forest himself, whether he had heard anything of Michał Żurawski.

The curate had. The Red Cross lists were coming through; a prisoner named Żurawski, number 119278, had been in the infirmary in January, had been on the march west in the snow, and there was a paper that said he had been liberated by the Americans in May.

"Then he is alive."

"Then he is alive," said the curate. "But, pani Zofio, the men who come back from there are not the men who went. I have seen six of them. They come back, and they sit down, and they do not speak for a year. Some of them go to the seminary. Most of them go to the vodka."

She had stood in the rectory with her hat in her hand.

"When he comes back," she said, "tell him I asked."

And the curate said, "I will," and then, because he was a young man who had learned in the forest how to be brave at the wrong moment: "Pani Zofio, he asked you not to wait."

"I know what he asked."

"It was a good letter. But he wrote it in the infirmary of a camp, and men in the infirmary of a camp are not issuing orders, they are saying goodbye. If he comes back and finds that you obeyed him, he will be a man who was obeyed by the only person he wanted to be wanted by, and I do not think there is a word for what that will do to him."

Zofia had looked at him for a long moment.

"You are a very clever young priest," she said, "and you have a great deal to learn about women."

She had gone to Warsaw that spring, and she went back to Świerczyna twice in 1947, and once in 1951 for her mother's funeral, and after that not for a long time.

* * *

The Warsaw she came into was the Warsaw of the Three-Year Plan, of bricks carried by schoolchildren from the piles to the sites, of the slogans painted on the boards that hid the ruins: *CAŁY NARÓD BUDUJE SWOJĄ STOLICĘ* — the whole nation builds its capital. It was a city in which a man who had been in the Home Army learned to cross the street when he saw two men in leather coats, and in which the men in leather coats were also, sometimes, the men who had been in the Home Army.

The Party in 1946 was not yet the Party of 1952. There were arguments in the cells; the old Socialists were still in it, or being pushed out of it, or discovering week by week what they had joined. Zofia had joined in the forest in 1942, not for the ideology but for the rifles, and in 1946 she transferred her card to Warsaw and discovered, in the Department of Education, that a young woman who had been in the forest, who could write a clean Polish sentence, and who did not flinch when a colonel entered the room, was a woman for whom the state had uses.

Her first post was in the Department of Schools and Curricula, and her job was to remove from the textbooks the things that had been in them in 1938. It did not feel, then, like the work of a censor. It felt like the work of a builder. The old textbooks had a drawing of Marshal Piłsudski on the first page and a prayer at the end of the reader; a chapter on the Polish colonies in Africa that was a lie; a paragraph about the Jews that she read with a cold face and marked for deletion with a pencil so hard the point broke. She was not taking anything away from the children. She was taking away the lies. She was twenty-seven, she had been in the forest with a rifle, and she believed, with the whole of her considerable intelligence, that a country could be taught to be good, and that she was one of the teachers.

There was a photograph of her from that time. I have it. She is standing at a table in a room with three men and a map of the new school districts, and she is the only woman, and she is not smiling, and she is pointing at the map with a pencil, and the men are looking not at the map but at her.

* * *

Her office in 1946 was on the third floor of a building on Hoża Street, and it had a window that gave onto a courtyard with a chestnut tree and a pump. Zofia's desk faced the window. On the second day she moved it so that it faced the door.

Her superior was a man of fifty named Wiktor Kosiński, who had been a teacher in Lublin before the war and had joined the Party in 1944 with the Soviet-backed committee, and who was in 1946 a sincere and frightened man. He taught her the trade: that a memorandum should have three parts, that the third part should be the recommendation and should be one sentence, that a minister reads only the recommendation, and that a recommendation that is right and one that is wrong are read with the same eyes, and therefore the sentence must be written as though it had been decided by God.

He also taught her what he called *the arithmetic*. "Every decision has a bill," he said. "You will be told that this is cynicism. It is not cynicism. It is bookkeeping. The men who do not count the bill are not better men than us; they are men who have not read the file. Count the bill, write it in a memorandum, and then decide whether the country can pay. That is the whole of government."

"And if the bill is a man?"

"Then write the man's name in the memorandum," said Kosiński. "Never write *a number of persons*. If the man is worth the price, his name will make the argument stronger. If the name makes the argument weaker, then you have learned something about the price."

She learned it. She was a good clerk, and then a very good one, and then, in 1949, when the Office for Religious Denominations was being organized and someone in the Presidium asked for a woman who could write, who knew the provinces, and who had a Party card from the forest and not from the university, Kosiński sent her file across the river with a note in his own hand: *Intelligent. Reliable. No family. Recommend.*

She went.

* * *

The Office in 1949 had four rooms and eleven staff and a mandate that could be read in two ways. It was created to *regulate the relations between the state and the religious denominations* — which meant, in the language of the time, to see that the churches did what the state had decided they would do.

Her section was the Catholic section, the only one that mattered. The other denominations had their own files and own tragedies and came through the Office in a slow unhappy trickle. The Catholic Church had fifteen thousand priests, eight thousand churches, and the loyalty of most of the population, and it could not be administered. It could only be managed.

She learned to manage it. The geography of the episcopate: which bishop was ill, which was brave, which was brave on Sundays and careful on Mondays. The language of the concordat that did not exist, of the decree of 1945 that had never been repealed, of the *porozumienie* of 1950, which both sides had understood as a truce and neither had intended to keep. She learned that a permit for a church roof could be used to obtain a report on a priest's sermon, and a report on a priest's sermon could be used to obtain a permit for a roof, and that the machine ran on this exchange, and that a person could grease the machine and be crushed by it in the same afternoon, at the same desk.

* * *

The 1950s came down on the Church like a shutter.

In 1949 the state began the process the newspapers called the *normalization* and the priests called the *persecution*: the confiscation of the Caritas charities, the closing of the Catholic University's faculties, the show trials of priests, the creation of the so-called patriotic priests, the men who would sign anything and whom the Office kept in a building on Wilcza Street with a telephone and a car. In 1953 the Primate, Stefan Wyszyński, was arrested at his own desk in the middle of the night and taken to a monastery at Stoczek, and then to Komańcza, in the mountains, where he was held three years, and where he wrote the text of the Great Novena on a typewriter smuggled in to him inside a crate of books.

Zofia was not in the room when the Primate was arrested. She was twenty miles from it, in a building on Krucza Street, taking notes at a meeting about the arrest of the Primate. She wrote: *The Primate's attitude is uncompromising. Recommend the matter be handled at the level of the Office of the Council of Ministers. Recommend no publicity. The Church will respond with silence and the silence will be reported abroad.*

She read it over. It was correct. It was the memorandum of a competent official who understood the machine. She did not sleep that night, and in the morning she went to work.

* * *

What happened to her in those years is hard to write, and I am going to write it the way she wrote it, in a document I found in the archive of the Office in 1968. It is not a diary; she did not keep a diary. It is a single sheet of paper, typed, with no heading, in a folder marked *Materiały nieaktowe* — non-current materials — which is where a clerk puts the papers he cannot bear to destroy and cannot bear to file.

It says:

> *The question is not whether the Church is right. The question is whether I am the woman who can do this work.*
>
> *In 1944, in the forest, I believed that the future of Poland was a question of who would build the schools. I still believe it. The children of Świerczyna were taught in a room without windows by a man who was told to teach them that the Vistula was a German river. The state that tore down that room and built the school on Bednarska is my state, and I am not ashamed of it, and I will not pretend to be.*
>
> *What I did not know in 1944 is that a state which builds schools must also decide what is taught in them, and that the decision is made by men who have never been in a school. I did not know that the men would be afraid. I did not know that fear is the actual material of government. Every memorandum I write is a door, and behind every door is a colonel who is afraid of a bishop, and behind the colonel is a minister who is afraid of the Presidium, and behind the minister is a man in Moscow who is afraid of nothing and knows nothing.*
>
> *I did not know that the machine would run on the fear, and that I would learn to feed it, and that I would be good at it.*
>
> *There is no God. That is not a tragedy; it is a relief. If there were a God, I would have to explain to Him what I did in 1949 with the file of Father Sokołowski, who is in Wronki, and who is dying, and whose file I read and did not appeal, because the appeal would have cost me the section, and the section is the only place from which I can do anything at all.*
>
> *I can do something. That is the whole of my faith. I can do something. I can keep the men who are worse than me out of the rooms where the decisions are made, and I can make the decisions that remain less bad than they would have been. Some days I do. Most days I do not. I have not been to confession since 1942, and I have not been to Mass since 1945, and I have not said a prayer since the night my father was shot, and none of that is because I do not believe. It is because I do believe. There is a God, and He is not on this earth, and the men who say He is are selling tickets.*
>
> *M. is alive. He is in a seminary in Kraków, or he was in 1948, and he will be a priest, and he has not written to me, and I have not written to him, and there is nothing to write. If I met him now, he would ask me what I have done, and I would tell him, and he would not be able to answer, and the not answering would be the end of it. That is why I do not write. That is the only reason. All the other reasons are the reasons a woman gives a colonel.*

The sheet is unsigned. It was typed on an Office machine with a broken *l* that I have seen in a hundred other documents.

I have read it perhaps forty times. The thing I keep returning to is not the confession. It is the sentence: *I can do something.* It is the sentence of a woman who has decided to save what she can save, and who knows the decision is not a decision but a daily practice, and who will spend the rest of her life doing it, and will be made to pay for it in the end.

* * *

Michał Żurawski was liberated in Austria in May 1945, weighing forty-four kilos, with a document that said he was a Polish citizen and a number on his arm.

He was in the American zone. There were officers who came through the displaced persons camps with lists, and a Pole in a British uniform who asked whether he wanted to go to England, and a man from the Second Corps who asked whether he wanted to go to Italy, and in September an American officer with a Polish name and a kind face who asked whether he wanted to go to America.

"No," said Michał.

"You understand what you are going back to?"

"Yes."

"There is no Poland," said the captain, who had been born in Chicago and had a photograph in his wallet of a street in Poznań he had never seen. "There is a Soviet province with a Polish flag. They will put you in prison if you say what you think. You have been in one camp; do you want to be in another?"

Michał looked at him.

"I want to go home," he said, "and I want to be buried in the ground I was born on. Everything else is a detail."

He came back in November 1945, through Czechoslovakia, on a train that took eleven days and stopped for a day outside Katowice because a bridge had been rebuilt by hand. He walked up from the station at Mława in December, in the dark, because the cart that was to meet him had been commandeered for the milk, and came into the mill kitchen at eight o'clock at night with snow in his hair, and his mother was standing at the stove with a pot in her hand, and his father was at the table with the accounts book, and no one said anything for a long time.

Then his mother put down the pot, and said: "You are thin."

"I am thin."

"You are alive."

"I am alive."

And Stanisław Żurawski, who was a taciturn man and a miller, and who had spent the war carrying grain in a cart with a false bottom, stood up from the table and went out into the yard and stood in the snow for a quarter of an hour, and came back with his face washed and said, "Sit down. Your mother has made soup."

That winter was the worst in living memory. There was no grain and no coal and the wheel was frozen, and in January the family ate potatoes and a soup made from the bones of a pig given to them by a neighbour who had been given it by a cousin at the Ministry of Agriculture. Michał weighed forty-four kilos in November, fifty-one in January, fifty-four in March; he had the cough half the men of his generation had; in the evenings he sat by the lamp and did not speak.

His mother, who had been a bell-ringer's daughter, watched him for six weeks.

Then one evening in February she sat down opposite him with the mending, and said, "You are going to be a priest."

Michał did not look up from the fire.

"I have been thinking about it," he said.

"I know what you have been thinking about, because I have been watching you think about it, and you have the face of a man who is going to do a thing and is afraid to say it in case he is not allowed." She put the mending down. "You are going to be a priest. There is a seminary in Kraków that takes men from the camps. You wrote Father Leon's name in the Bible."

"Mother."

"I am not asking you. I am telling you what you are going to do. Because in this house we have had one son taken to the Reich and one son come back, and the son who came back is not the son who went. The son who went was a miller. The son who came back has been in a place where they took everything a man has, and he has come back with a thing they could not take, and the thing is not for the mill."

Michał looked at her for a long time.

"And what is the thing?" he said.

"God," said his mother, "and the habit of getting up in the morning."

He laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since 1943, and it was a terrible laugh, a laugh with something broken in it, and it lasted a long time, and when it was over he was crying, and his mother held him, and the fire went down, and in the morning he went to Mława to see the curate about the seminary.

* * *

He was ordained in Kraków in June 1952, in the cathedral on Wawel Hill, in a ceremony that began at six in the morning because the authorities had refused permission for a public ordination and the bishop had decided that a private one at dawn was better than nothing.

There were nine men ordained that morning. Eight of them were in their twenties, from the seminaries, and one was thirty-two, with grey in his hair and a number on his arm, and when the bishop laid his hands on that one's head, the bishop closed his eyes and held them there a moment longer than he held them on the others, and there was a sound in the congregation, a low sound, and it was not a cough.

His parents came. Stanisław wore a black suit that had belonged to the parish organist, and Ania wore a hat that her daughter-in-law had made her from a pattern in a magazine, and they sat in the fourth row and did not hold hands, because the village did not, and when it was over and the new priests came down the aisle the old miller stood up and said, in a voice that carried to the back of the cathedral:

"*Bóg zapłać.*"

God repay. The old peasant thanks. And the priests laughed and the bishop laughed and the bishop's secretary, a young man who had never in his life been in a village, was seen to wipe his eye.

* * *

Zofia was in Warsaw on the day of the ordination, in the Office on Krucza Street, in a meeting with the Ministry of Public Security about the seminary in Kraków.

The file lay on the table in front of her: a thin file with a photograph clipped to the inside cover, a man of thirty-two in a black suit with his head turned away, and a typed page that said *Żurawski, Michał*, with the date and place of birth, the seminary, the date of the ordination, and a note at the bottom: *Wg doniesienia: wyjątkowo oddany Prymasowi. Przeszłość obozowa — opinia środowiska wysoka. Rokowania: twardy. Klasyfikacja: B.*

Classification B was the classification for a priest who was not yet dangerous enough to arrest and too dangerous to leave alone.

The colonel asked her a question about the seminary's intake.

She gave the answer.

The meeting moved on to the next item, and she wrote the minutes in her small neat hand, and at the end she carried the file back to the registrar, who stamped it and filed it among four thousand other files, and for six years nobody looked at it again.

* * *

The two resurrections came within a month of each other: she was made chief of the Catholic section in May 1952, and he was ordained in June 1952. Neither of them knew. The letters he had written to her in 1946 had gone to the rectory at Świerczyna, where the curate put them in a drawer and then, in 1948, when the rectory was searched and the drawer opened, into the stove, to save her name. The letter she had written to him in 1946 had gone to the Red Cross in Kraków, had never found him, and was returned to sender in 1947, and she had never opened it.

It was returned to sender. She had never opened it. It is in the parcel on my desk, with the tin and the pouch and the sealed envelope, and it is a letter from a woman of twenty-seven to a man in a displaced persons camp, and it is eight lines long, and the eight lines have defeated me for six weeks.

I have opened it. I opened it in October, after the funeral, with a knife, at my kitchen table, in the middle of the night, and I am not sorry.

It says:

*Michał.*

*I have heard that you are alive. I am in Warsaw. I have work and a room and a ration card and a good coat. I have been told to tell you that I am not waiting for you, and I have done it, and it is in writing now, and you may keep the paper.*

*But if you come to Warsaw, come to the Office on Krucza Street and ask for Wierzbicka, and I will come down the stairs.*

*Z.*

He never came. She never knew whether he had received it.

He had not received it. It came back in 1947 to the Office, to the address on the envelope, and was put in the tray of undeliverable post; a clerk, seeing the name, sent it up to the section, and the section read the name and sent it back down, and it was in a folder in her desk when she cleared it in 1956, and she took it home in her bag, and it has been in the tin ever since.

That is the whole of the debt, and this is the arithmetic: two people, both alive, both in the same city since 1946, both in the same file since 1952, and neither knocked on the door, because each of them knew the bill.

# Chapter Four: The Stone and the Rope

*Kraków and Warsaw, 1948–1957*

They put him in the seminary at twenty-six because a priest in a village told him he had a vocation, and he went because he could not think of a single reason to say no.

This is not the beginning of the story, but it is where the man in the black coat began, and I have to put it down properly, because everything that happens in the room with her in 1958 was built into him in the years when nobody was watching: the years between the tower and the altar, when a boy who had been in a camp learned the trade that would put him in a pulpit in Krakowskie Przedmieście with a congregation holding its breath.

* * *

The seminary was in a house that had been a monastery before the war and a storehouse during it, and the men who came to it in 1948 were not boys. There were twenty-eight of them in his year, and eleven had been in camps, and six had been in the Home Army, and one had been in the People's Army and did not talk about it, and one had been a village teacher who had taught in German through the occupation, and the others were careful with him in a way that was worse than contempt.

They rose at five. They prayed in an unheated chapel, and the water froze in the cruets, and the rector, an old man with one lung, said the Mass in a voice you could hear only within ten feet, and the men crowded forward, and the crowding kept them warm.

The course was four years. Michał took five, because in the first year he was ill — the cough that half his generation had, and a fever that kept him in the infirmary from November to February — and because he was, by the standards of the seminary, badly educated. He had eight years of village school and two and a half years of gymnasium in Mława before the war took it away, and he could read Latin like a man reading a map in a language he half knew, and he could not read Greek at all, and the professor of dogmatics, who was a kind and exacting man, told him in the first week that he had the mind of a miller: he could weigh anything and he could not imagine anything.

He learned to imagine. It took him three years and it was, he said afterward, like learning to use a hand that had been broken and set badly. The Summa in the evenings. The canon law, which he found to be a corrupt and magnificent machine, like the Austro-Hungarian railway. The history of the Church's diplomacy, which he found to be the same, and it was at that point that something in him turned over and lay still.

He had gone into the seminary to be a parish priest in a village like Świerczyna, to say Mass in a cold church and hear confessions and grow old among people whose grandparents he had buried.

He came out of it knowing he was going to be something else. It was not ambition, or not only. It was a piece of arithmetic he had done in the camp and could not unlearn. *The men who kill you are not the men who hate you. The men who kill you are the men who are afraid. The men who are afraid are the men with something to lose. And the men with something to lose can be talked to.*

He did not say this aloud in the seminary. He said it aloud for the first time in 1951, in the church of St. Catherine, on the feast of Corpus Christi, and it nearly ended his career before it began.

* * *

The Forty Hours' devotion was a thing the Church did in those years to keep the city awake: the Blessed Sacrament exposed on the altar for forty hours, and the parishes taking turns, and the students coming in shifts through the night, and the young priests preaching at the odd hours when the churches were full of the kind of people who came at three in the morning.

They gave Michał the three o'clock hour on the second day. He was thirty-one years old, a deacon, six months from ordination, and he had prepared a sermon on the Eucharist, and he had it in his hand, and he stood up in the pulpit and he could not read it.

He had been thinking, all through the night before, about the camp. Not the details; he had learned not to think about the details. About one morning in the winter of 1944, when a priest in his block had said Mass in the corner with his hands, without bread and without wine, and a guard had come into the block with his rifle and stood in the doorway and watched, and had not stopped it, and after four minutes had gone away.

He told it from the pulpit, in the plain voice of a man describing something he had seen. He did not tell them what it meant. He said that he had thought for years that the story was about the priest's courage, and that he had come to think it was about the guard.

"That is the whole of the mystery of the Church," he said, "and it is also the whole of the mystery of the state. A man is never one thing. The state is never one thing. The task of the Church is not to defeat the state. The task of the Church is to be present in the room where the state is deciding, and to be a man in that room, and to be the reason the guard who came into the corner with his hands does not come back the next morning. I am told that this is not a Polish doctrine. I am told that a priest's place is on the barricade. I have been on the barricade. I was on it when they came into the church. The barricade is a fine place for a young man. It is a very poor place for a bishop."

He came down from the pulpit into a silence he had not expected and could not read.

The rector sent for him the next morning. The rector was in bed, with the lung, and he had the text of the sermon on the counterpane in front of him, and a clipping from the diocesan curia's confidential bulletin beside it.

"Where did you get this?"

"I preached it."

"You preached it from a pulpit in this diocese at three o'clock in the morning, in front of two hundred students and a man from the curia who writes down everything, and now it is in the bulletin of the curia, and by Friday it will be in the file of the Office." The rector looked at him over his spectacles. "Father Żurawski. In this diocese there are twelve churches and two hundred priests and one bishop, and the bishop is seventy-one, and he has been in the office since 1931, and he has kept this diocese alive for sixteen years by knowing exactly how far he can go. You have just gone further than he can follow, in a church in the centre of the city, on a feast day, without asking him. Do you understand what you have done?"

"I have preached the Gospel."

"You have preached the Gospel," agreed the rector, "and the Gospel is not the question. The question is whether the bishop can protect you. He cannot. He is seventy-one and he is tired and he has a file in Warsaw with a photograph in it too, and he will not spend it on a deacon who says that the Church should be in the room with the Security Service." He pushed the papers across the counterpane. "But the Primate can."

"What?"

"The Primate has asked for the text of the sermon. The Primate has been at Komańcza for two years and has no access to a pulpit and almost no access to the world, but the Primate has read the sermon, and the Primate has sent a message, and the message is in my hand and it is one line. Do you want to hear it?"

"Yes."

The rector read it, in the flat voice of a man who did not entirely approve.

*"Tell the deacon he is right, and tell him to be quiet until I am out."*

* * *

He was ordained on the fifteenth of June 1952, in the cathedral at Wawel, at six o'clock in the morning, in a private ceremony that the authorities had refused to permit as a public one, and which the bishop had decided was better at dawn than not at all.

There were nine men ordained that morning. Eight were in their twenties, from the seminary, with their mothers in the front rows; one was thirty-two, with grey in his hair and a number on his forearm, and no mother in the front row, or so the young priests thought, until they turned at the end of the ceremony and saw, in the fourth row, an old man in a black suit that had belonged to the parish organist and an old woman in a hat made from a pattern in a magazine.

When the bishop laid his hands on Michał's head, he held them there a moment longer than he held them on the others, and there was a sound somewhere at the back of the cathedral, and it was not a cough.

Afterward the new priests came down the aisle, and the old miller stood up out of the fourth row and said, in a voice that carried to the back:

"*Bóg zapłać.*"

God repay. The old peasant's thanks. The priests laughed and the bishop laughed and a young secretary of the curia, who had never been in a village, was seen to wipe his eye.

* * *

They sent him to Nowa Huta.

Nowa Huta was the new steel city east of Kraków, and it had been built without a church on purpose, because the state had decided that the working class of the new Poland would not need one. By 1952 there were thirty thousand people in it, and a cinema, and a stadium, and a school, and a statue of Lenin, and a small chapel in a converted house on the far edge of the town, and a priest who said Mass in a room with a concrete floor and a portrait of Bierut on the wall of the corridor outside.

Michał was the third priest at that chapel. The first had been removed for a sermon about the cross in the foyer of the works. The second had been transferred to a parish in the mountains with four hundred souls and a leaking roof and no car, and was known to be happy.

He was there for two years and a half, and he learned to be a parish priest in the place where the state was loudest, and he learned the thing that is at the heart of this book: that in those years, the people who came to the room with the concrete floor and the portrait outside did not come because they believed in God. Most of them did not know what they believed. They came because they wanted to be in a room where the man at the front was not afraid.

He said Sunday Mass six times, from six in the morning to six in the evening, because the works kept three shifts, and the room held eighty people and there were between seven hundred and a thousand at the Masses on a Sunday, and they stood out in the yard in the snow in the winter to hear it through a loudspeaker that a boy from the works had rigged to a cable run under the fence.

He heard confessions in a cupboard with a curtain. He baptized sixty-one children in a wooden font that an old carpenter in the parish had made from the crate the altar came in. He buried fourteen people in two years, and three of them were workers who had been killed at the furnace and whose families could not get the accident register from the works; and when he went to the works to ask for the register, the director — a young man in a good suit, with a diploma from the party school — said, "Father, there are no accidents at the Nowa Huta works," and Michał said, "Then the three men were killed by God, and I will say so at the funeral," and the director gave him the register.

In March 1954 they took him in for questioning. Eleven days, in the office on Pomorska Street, and the questions were about a conversation in a kitchen in Nowa Huta: what had been said about the price of bread, what had been said about the cardinal, who had said it, who had been in the room.

He told them nothing and signed nothing, and on the eleventh day they let him go, and the officer who let him go — a man of about thirty, with a good suit and a tired face — walked with him to the door and said:

"Father. You are going to be somebody. Do you know how I know? Because in eleven days you did not once tell me a lie. Everyone tells me a lie. The liars are the ones I can use."

He walked back to Nowa Huta in the rain, nine miles, four hours, and stopped at a place where there had been a mill and was now a sign for a collective farm, and sat down in the ditch and shook for a quarter of an hour. Then he got up and went on, and said the evening Mass, and heard three confessions, and could not eat his supper.

* * *

In September 1954 he came to Warsaw, to the chancery of the cathedral, to work under a woman of seventy with a green eyeshade.

Her name was Maria Okońska and she ran the chancery of the Primate of Poland while the Primate was in a monastery at Komańcza in the mountains, and she ran it the way a stationmaster runs a railway in a war: with a ledger, a card index in her head, and an absolute refusal to be surprised. She had a table at the end of the corridor under the window, and she was in it every morning at seven, and she said the Rosary at noon with whoever was in the room, and she had known the Primate since 1931, and she had been arrested twice, and she had a way of looking at a young priest that made him tell her the truth, which was her only vanity and her chief use.

The work of the chancery in 1954 was to be the central address of a Church that was being taken apart.

Applications. Complaints. Reports from every parish in the country, sent by priests and nuns and schoolteachers and postmen, in ciphers that changed every month. Lists of priests in prison, and which prisons, and which had been tried, and which had been sent to a place with no name. The Novena — not yet the Novena, the Primate's plan, written at Komańcza in 1955 and smuggled out in the binding of a breviary, for the nine years of prayer that would prepare the country for the thousandth year of its baptism. Money. There was never any money.

He slept in a cell of the cloister. In the mornings he deciphered the mail; in the afternoons he wrote letters to bishops in a style he had been taught by pani Okońska, which was the style of a man who is not asking for anything and wishes you to notice it; in the evenings he read.

It was pani Okońska who discovered his gift, in February 1955, in a matter of a cemetery in Kielce.

The parish had bought the land in 1938. The town had taken the land in 1951 for a road that had never been built. The diocese had asked for it back in 1953; the office had answered in 1954 that the matter was under consideration; the parish priest had written a letter that had made the office regret the whole thing, a letter in the style of a man who knows he is right, and the file had gone into a drawer.

Pani Okońska gave it to Michał on a Monday and told him to write a memorandum. He wrote one, and it was good, and on Thursday she sent him to the Office with it.

He was in the room for two hours with a clerk of forty and a file on the desk between them. He had never in his life been in a government office, and he discovered in the second hour that he was not afraid: that the man opposite him was afraid, of his superior, of the drawer, of the question that the file might be raised in a place where questions were asked. And Michał, who was a miller's son, did what a miller does. He said: "The land is worth nothing to the road, because there is no road. It is worth a great deal to the parish, because the dead are in it. Give the land back, and I will write the document that makes you look like the man who gave it back, and the bishop will sign a letter of thanks to the Office that will be read in the ministry, and the story of the cemetery will end with your name in it."

The clerk looked at him for a long time.

"Who taught you to talk like that?" he said.

"Nobody," said Michał. "I am a miller."

The land came back in April. Pani Okońska wrote a note in her ledger in the evening and said, without looking up, "You were in the room with a man who was going to say no, and you came out of it with a yes. There are four of you in this chancery and there has not been one in fifteen years. I am going to tell the Primate."

"Pani Mario, it was a cemetery."

"Everything is a cemetery," said pani Okońska, and went on writing.

* * *

1956 was the year the world turned over.

In February, Khrushchev spoke in Moscow. In March, Bierut died in Moscow, and his body was brought back and lay in state in the Palace of Culture, and the city stood in a queue to look at the embalmed face of the man who had signed the order for the arrest of the Primate, and the queue was not a queue of mourners.

In June, the workers of Poznań came out into the streets and took the radio station, and the army fired on them, and the dead were sixty, or seventy, or a hundred, and the number has never been established; the monument in Poznań says *for freedom, bread and law*, and the stones of the square still hold the marks of the tracks.

In October, the Party stood at the edge of a revolution at its own Plenum. Gomułka — who had been in prison from 1951 to 1954, and had been let out when Stalin died — was brought back to the leadership, and the Red Army moved on Warsaw, and the workers of Warsaw did not come into the street, and the Red Army did not come in, and a man gave a speech about a Polish road to socialism, and the crowd sang what it had not been allowed to sing since 1948.

On the twenty-eighth of October, at the end of that month, three men in a car drove up to the monastery at Komańcza and took the Primate of Poland away.

There is a photograph of him on the balcony of the bishop's palace that night, in a black coat and a biretta, blessing a crowd of fifty thousand people in the snow, with the candles all the way down Miodowa Street. He had been three years in the monastery. He looked like a man who had come out of a cell and found his city on the other side of the door.

Michał was in the room at the end of the corridor when the Primate came into his own chancery. It was the fourth of November, at eight in the morning. The Primate came in with two men of the Security Service at the door and a leather bag in his hand, and pani Okońska stood up at her table and said, "*Księże Prymasie*," and could not say anything else.

The Primate put down the bag and went along the row of the chancery staff and took each of them by the hand.

He was fifty-five. He was thin. He had spent three years reading in a cold room and he would never again in his life be warm: that is what the men who knew him say, all of them, the ones who loved him and the ones who did not.

When he came to Michał, he stopped.

"You are the one from the camp."

"Yes, Excellency."

"Nineteen thousand, one hundred and —" The Primate closed his eyes. "No. Forgive me. I am tired. The number is in the file, and I have read too many files today. Tell me your number."

"One one nine two seven eight, Excellency."

The Primate held his hand a moment longer.

"The Church is going to ask you for something," he said, "and it will be a thing you would rather not do, and it will not be a thing that a man from the camp can refuse. But you must refuse it if it is wrong. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"No, you don't," said the Primate. "Not yet. You will."

* * *

He was made rector of St. Anne's in the autumn of 1956, in the first weeks after the Primate came back — the church on Krakowskie Przedmieście that had been the university church since 1928, where the students came, and the professors came, and where the young people of Warsaw came because the sermons were short and the confessors were kind and the choir was the best in the city.

The church had been burned in 1944 and had been under repair since 1946, and the tower had lost its roof, and the roof of the nave was tarred canvas over the greater part of it, and the walls were grey with the smoke of the fire and with the plaster that the parish had put on them in the winter of 1946, badly, in the cold. The nave held six hundred people. On a Sunday there were more than a thousand.

It was a student church, in a city of students, in a state that in 1956 had begun to wonder how many students a state could have and still be a state. The students came to St. Anne's in their hundreds. They came from the Polytechnic and the University and the Academy of Medicine, and they came for the sermons, and they came for the confessions, and some of them came because they had discovered that a young priest named Żurawski would not tell them that everything was a sin, and some of them came because he would.

In the autumn of 1957 he began the catechesis for the children. The state had put religion out of the schools in the autumn of 1956, at the high tide of the thaw, when everyone thought the thaw would go further than it did, and the parish had taken the children into the church and the sacristy and a room where the organist used to live.

Two hundred and thirty-one children. Forty-eight of them were the children of Party families. He knew this because he kept a list, and the list was a danger to him, and he kept it anyway, because he had learned in the camp that a list is a promise.

* * *

He did not see her in those years. He did not look for her.

He knew she was in Warsaw. In 1946 he had been told so by the curate at Mława, in a letter; in 1952 he had seen her name in a document he should not have seen, on a list of the officials to whom the curia's correspondence on the seminaries was to be addressed; in 1954, in the chancery, pani Okońska had said to him, without looking up from her ledger, "You know the woman Wierzbicka at the Office. She is the only one of them who reads the documents before she signs them. If you ever need a thing done in that building, go to her, and do not lie to her, because she can tell." And he had said, "I know," and pani Okońska had looked up then, and looked at him for a while, and gone back to her ledger.

He did not look for her. That is the truth, and it is also the cowardice, and I want to put it down carefully, because he was a brave man, and the cowardice was not in his nature but in his arithmetic.

He had written her a letter in the camp. He had sent it through the curia at Mława to a girl in Świerczyna, and he had told her not to wait for him, and a priest had read it out loud to his parents in the kitchen of the mill, and the priest had read the last part in which he said that in his chronicle they were on the ice at the bend and it was not the church bell that rang. He had meant it as a gift. He had meant it as the thing a man who is going to die gives to a woman who is going to live. He had not thought — he told me this himself, in the spring of 1971, on a bench in the garden of the bishops' house at Płock, in the sun, with his hands on his knees, and he was fifty-one and had been a bishop for twelve years — he had not thought that she would read it as an order, or that she would obey it, or that she would obey it for fifteen years out of a kind of loyalty that was the same as the thing he had meant to set her free from.

"I thought I was being generous," he said. "I was being nineteen. A man is never more selfish than when he is being generous on paper."

He did not look for her because he had told her not to wait for him, and if she had not waited, she was married, and he had no right to open that door. And he did not look for her because, in 1956 and in 1957, in the chancery, he had begun to hear her name in another context, and the context was the Office, and the Office was the enemy, and he did not know which of the two facts was the one that frightened him.

In November 1957 she read the file. He knows that now, because the file says so; the file has a note in her hand, and the note is dated, and the date is thirteen months before he was told that she had read it. But in November 1957 he did not know. In November 1957 he was in the church of St. Anne on a Tuesday morning with a painter from the conservation office, arguing about the colour of the ceiling, and he was happy, in the way a man is happy when he is doing work he was made for, and he had no idea at all that on the other side of the city, in an office on a side street off Marszałkowska, a woman in a grey coat was reading the first page of his file for the second time, and that this time she had seen the name.

# Chapter Five: What Sztachelski Wants

*Warsaw, February–April 1958*

The Minister's office had a window that did not open.

It was on the third floor of the building on Krucza Street, and it had been the office of a director of the Bank of Poland before the war, and the window was the original, a great sash of twelve panes that had been painted shut in 1945 and never opened since, because the frame had swollen and the state did not have a joiner who could be spared. In winter the panes ran with condensation. In summer the room was a greenhouse, and the Minister, who had been a minister of health before he was a minister of religion, sweated through his shirt and did not complain, and the officials who came to see him learned to bring their own handkerchief.

Jerzy Sztachelski was forty-seven years old and looked older, and he had the hands of a man who had worked with them in a laboratory and had never quite persuaded them to stop. He had been a doctor in Wilno before the war, and a partisan in the forest, and a minister of health after it; he had built the health service of a ruined country out of nothing, and it had been his pride; and in 1956, when the Party needed a man who could talk to the bishops without shouting, someone had decided that a doctor who understood the body might understand the Church.

Zofia had worked for him for two years. She had learned that he was the most intelligent man in the building and the most dangerous, and that the two facts were the same fact. He did not lose his temper. He did not repeat himself. He asked a question, listened to the answer, and made a small note in the margin of the document in front of him, and the small note was always the thing that mattered.

He was making the note now, with a fountain pen, at the top of the folder that she had brought him.

"The list," he said. "Rome has not answered."

"Rome has not answered."

"Rome is dying." He capped the pen. "The Holy Father is not expected to live through the year. The Secretariat is doing what a Secretariat does when its master is dying, which is nothing, and the bishops of Poland will not have an answer to their list until there is a new Pope or a miracle, and I do not expect either before the autumn." He looked up. "So we have a year. What do we do with a year?"

"We prepare," said Zofia.

"We prepare," agreed Sztachelski. "But I have read your memorandum and I am going to tell you what is wrong with it. You have written it as though the threat were the five names. It is not. The five names are bishops. We know what to do with a bishop. A bishop is a man with a diocese, and a diocese is an institution, and an institution has a telephone line that can be cut and a bank account that can be watched and a file that can be opened. If they make the young one a bishop, we will have a bishop. He will have a palace and a car and a secretary and an illness, and in five years he will be one of us in the only sense that matters, which is that he will be in a room he cannot leave."

"Then what is the threat?"

"The Novena."

Sztachelski stood up and went to the window that did not open and stood looking down at the street.

"Pani Zofio. You have been in this Office since 1949. In that time we have taken the Church apart with our hands. We took the charities. We took the schools. We took the Primate. We took the University. We put a hundred and seventy priests in prison and we broke a hundred more with a pen, and I know the number, because I signed the letters. And in October 1956 the whole machine stopped, because the Party was afraid of the workers in Poznań, and the Party let the Primate out of his monastery and let the religion back into the schools, and the bishops went home and did what bishops do, which is to say they thanked God and they waited."

He turned around.

"And then last May the Primate began his Novena. Nine years, he told the people. Nine years of prayer, of confession, of the rosary every evening, of the picture of the Mother of God going from parish to parish, and at the end of the nine years, in 1966, a thousand years of Poland, and a million people at Częstochowa. You have seen the crowds. I have seen your reports on the crowds."

"In the dioceses where the picture has been, the attendance is —"

"In the dioceses where the picture has been, the churches are full and the party cells are empty, and the local secretaries write to Warsaw to say that the peasants are putting up arches of birch branches across the roads and the children are throwing flowers, and that the militia cannot be everywhere, and that the picture is better attended than the May Day parade." Sztachelski's voice was quiet and even. "It is better attended than the May Day parade, Zofia. In a workers' state."

He came back to the desk and sat down.

"The list of bishops is a small problem. The Novena is the whole problem. If we let the Primate have his nine years, then in 1966 the Church will have the millennium and the state will have the anniversary of the manifesto, and the two will compete in the same week, in the same country, in front of the same people, and only one of them will be on the front pages. The Church is not a rival administration. It is a rival *nation*. It is the only rival nation Poland has ever had that did not need an army."

"And you want me to stop the Novena," said Zofia.

"I want you to blunt it." He opened the folder. "You cannot stop it. If you stop it, you make it. A picture in a church is a picture in a church. A picture stopped by the militia at a crossroads is a miracle. We learned that in 1953 with the Primate: we put him in a monastery and he became the most popular man in Poland, and we let him out and he became a politician. The Office does not stop things. The Office *administers* them."

"Then what is the administration?"

"Small things," said Sztachelski. "The Novena is a machine with a thousand wheels, and every wheel is a parish, and every parish needs a permit. A permit for the temporary altar. A permit for the procession on the road, because the road is a public road and the procession blocks traffic. A permit for the loudspeaker, because the loudspeaker is a public address. A permit for the night vigil, because the night vigil requires the church to be open after ten o'clock and the church's insurance does not cover it. A permit for the trees, for the birch branches across the roads, because the trees belong to the forestry administration." He turned the pages. "We have already begun. In the last three months the number of permits refused in the dioceses where the picture has passed is four hundred and eleven. Your colleague at the Presidium calls it the drip. The drip is the correct method. The drip does not make a martyr. The drip makes a nuisance. And a nuisance, in the end, is what a working man stays home from."

Zofia was silent.

"You disapprove," said Sztachelski.

"I think it will fail," said Zofia. "I think a permit refused is a story, and in a village the story is told in the evening, and the man who tells it is the priest, and the man who listens is the whole village. Four hundred stories in three months, Minister. You are not administering a nuisance. You are paying a printer."

Sztachelski smiled, and the smile was the most dangerous thing in the room.

"Then you will do it better than the Presidium," he said. "That is why you are here."

He took a single sheet of paper from the drawer and put it in front of her.

She read it. It was one page, typed, and it was a letter from a priest in the diocese of Lublin to the bishop, and it was a denunciation: the priest wrote that the vicar of his parish, a man of thirty-four named Father Antoni Rzepa, had spoken in the sacristy against the Primate, and had said that the Novena was a work of pride and the picture a piece of painted wood, and that in his judgment the Church should come to a settlement with the state.

"The letter is real," said Sztachelski. "It was written by a priest of the parish of Wąwolnica who is a member of the society of the priests of the Fatherland and an informer of the Ministry of Public Security, and it was sent to the bishop through the ordinary post, which is why I have a copy. What do you make of it?"

"I make of it that it is one priest's opinion, and that a vicar has the right to be wrong in the sacristy."

"The vicar does have that right. The bishop does not have to keep him in the parish." Sztachelski laid a second sheet beside the first. "The bishop will transfer him to a village in the north with a congregation of nine hundred and no house, and the vicar will refuse the transfer, and the bishop will suspend him, and the vicar will appeal to the Office — to us, Zofia — and the Office will find that his case is one of a group of priests whose consciences and rights deserve the protection of the state. We will give him a parish of his own, in a church we can control, with a vicar patriotic and a telegraph line. And in the press there will appear an article about a poor priest who was silenced by his bishop for preaching the love of his country."

"It will not work," said Zofia.

"It has worked eight times since 1955."

"It will not work on a priest," said Zofia, "unless he is already a fool or a drunk, and you do not need a report to find a fool. The report says this man is neither. The report says he is a sincere man who has lost his faith in the Primate's method and found another. Men like that do not become informers. They become sectaries. It is more dangerous."

Sztachelski looked at her for a long moment.

"That is what I said about the young bishop," he said. "You are the only person in this building who tells me the machine may not work. It is why I will keep you when the others are gone. But I want this done, and I want it done in the diocese of Lublin, because the bishop there is the one who signed the letter to the Prime Minister in November, and because the priests of Lublin read the Lublin newspapers."

He closed the folder.

"There is one more thing," he said. "Your rector of St. Anne's."

Zofia did not move.

"The young priest who preaches the Epiphany sermon about the state being afraid of a child," said Sztachelski. "I read your last report on the catechesis. You recommend no action. I am not going to tell you to punish a man for the size of his congregation. I am going to tell you something else, and I want you to hear it as an official and not as a woman, though I know you will not." He leaned back. "If Rome answers before the autumn, and the young one is made a bishop, then the Church will have a bishop who is a saint without a weakness, and we will have nothing on him. But if we have nothing on him, we will have to invent something, and inventions are the most expensive thing the Office makes, and the bill for a bad invention is paid by the next man who deserves a good one."

"Minister."

"I know," said Sztachelski. "You were in Świerczyna. It is in your file. I read everything about my officers, Zofia, it is the only way to survive in this building, and I read your file in 1956 and I put it down and said to myself: here is a woman who has buried a village and gone on working, and I will never mention it to her." He turned the fountain pen over in his fingers. "He is not going to be your friend. If he is a bishop, he will not be your friend. If he is a saint, he will carry you to the stake with a hymn. That is what a saint is, Zofia. A saint is a man who loves God more than he loves the person in front of him, and the person in front of him is always the one who burns."

"That is a strange thing for a minister to say."

"I was a doctor," said Sztachelski, "and I have watched men die of the two things that cannot be treated. The first is a hole in the lung. The second is faith. Go and do the Lublin thing. And Zofia —" He looked up. "If you should ever need to tell me something that is not in a memorandum, my door is open, and my window does not open, and that is the difference between me and the Ministry of Public Security. My office is a place where a thing can be said."

"Nothing can be said in this building," said Zofia. "But I will remember the window."

Work protocol, 14 February 1958. In the matter of the catechetical pavilion at the church of St. Anne in Warsaw. Agreed: 1) the question of the drainage of the site, to be settled by the Department of Municipal Economy; 2) scope of works — repairs to the roof and construction of the pavilion by 31 August of this year; 3) supervision on the part of the Office — p. director Z. Wierzbicka; supervision on the part of the parish — Fr. M. Żurawski; 4) matters of cost — to a separate protocol; 5) the parties undertake to consult in current matters. Drawn in two copies.

Five clauses. The first is the drainage. The fifth is the doorway.

They met on Fridays for four months, and the meetings were four hours long, and the work they did was not the work of enemies. It was not the work of friends either. It was the work of two people who had been trained by life to do a hard thing in a hard place, and who discovered, without ever saying so, that the other was the only person they had ever met who did the work in the same way.

She wrote the documents. He brought the numbers. She knew what a document had to say to survive the desk of a minister, and he knew what a parish could actually do, and between the two of them they built a thing: the hall at St. Anne's, with a stove, that would be finished in August, in time for the autumn term, with two hundred and thirty-one children in it and the roof repaired over the nave.

They did not speak of the village. They spoke of the cost of copper pipe. They spoke of the bishop's chancery, which had no money and would not admit it; of the Ministry of Education, which had a form for everything and no form for a stove; of the glazier, who had been in the Home Army and would not take a state contract and did the work in the evenings for vodka and a blessing. They spoke of the students, of the choir, of the professor of medicine who came to the six o'clock Mass in his operating clothes and left before the blessing because the operating theatre was waiting.

And once, in March, in the fourth hour, with the tea cold and the light failing, she said, without looking up from the paper: "There is a thing I have to tell you, and I will tell you once, and then we will not speak of it again."

He put down his pen.

"The Office has a file on the vicar of Wąwolnica. The provincial office has begun the Rzepa affair, and they will want the central Office to endorse the transfer and the pension and the parish they give him. I will be asked to sign it. If I sign it, the vicar becomes a state priest, and his bishop is humiliated, and the press writes an article about the freedom of conscience, and it is a small thing, and it will work, because the vicar is a sincere man who has been told by a colonel that God is on the side of the settlement and he believes it, and the believing is the tragedy, and there is nothing I can do."

"You can refuse to sign."

"If I refuse, another officer signs, and the other officer is a man named Klimek, who drinks and who writes a document like a man throwing a bucket of water, and the vicar will be ruined more thoroughly and the bishop humiliated more thoroughly and the article written by someone worse than me. There is no version of this in which the vicar is saved. There is only a version in which he is ruined by a competent person."

Michał looked at her across the table.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you will read it in the press in a week," said Zofia, "and because you will wonder whether the woman who wrote the protocol about the copper pipe knew about the vicar. And the answer is yes. And because —" She stopped.

"Because?"

"Because I am going to ask you for something, and I want you to know the price of the room you are standing in."

She put down her pen.

"The Office is going to move against the Novena in the summer. Not against the Church. Against the Novena. Permits, processions, vigils, the loudspeakers, the trees. It will be done through the provincial offices and it will be done badly, and the men who do it will be the worst men in the provinces, because the best men will find an excuse. I have read the plan. There are four hundred and eleven refusals in it, and by August there will be fourteen hundred, and the bishops will protest, and the protests will be filed, and the filing is the point. It will not stop the Novena and it will not break the Church. It will make a great deal of paperwork and a great deal of misery, and it will be a year in which the state and the Church do not speak to each other, and the man who pays for the year is the parish priest in the small town whose church has no roof."

"And what do you want from me?"

"I want the parish of St. Anne's to be the exception," said Zofia. "I want this parish to have its hall and its roof and its stove and its two hundred and thirty-one children, in the autumn, while the permits are refused in fourteen hundred other places, and I want it known in the Office that the exception exists because the rector of St. Anne's is a man the Office can do business with. And in exchange, when they come for the Novena in the summer, you will not organize the procession on the Krakowskie Przedmieście. You will not put the picture in the street in front of the Palace of Culture. You will keep your two hundred and thirty-one children in a hall with a stove, and you will not give the Ministry a photograph of the militia at a crossroads with the Virgin in a van."

The room was silent. Somewhere in the building a door closed.

"You are asking me to be your tame priest," said Michał.

"I am asking you to be a priest in a room with a stove," said Zofia, "and to let them have the crossroads."

"This is the offer," said Michał slowly, "that the Office makes to every priest in Poland. First the roof. Then the stove. Then the school. Then the photograph, in the newspaper, of the priest with the official at the opening of the school, and the priest has his picture taken, and the parish has its stove, and the bishop has a file, and the state has one more man it can point to when it says that the Church is not persecuted. I have seen what happens to the priests who take the roof. I have seen their faces at the deanery conferences. They are the saddest faces in Poland, pani director, because they took the roof and they were right to take the roof, and there is nothing more terrible than a man who was right to take the roof."

There was a long silence. Then Zofia said, very quietly, in a voice he had not heard from her since 1938:

"You think I don't know that."

And she picked up her pen and went back to the document, and they finished the protocol about the copper pipe, and he signed it, and she stamped it, and they did not speak of the vicar again.

But on the way out, at the door, with the corridor behind him and Tosia's typewriter clicking, he stopped.

"The vicar," he said. "Rzepa. When you sign the paper. Will you write his name in the memorandum?"

Zofia looked up.

"Everyone writes the name now," she said. "It is the fashion. A memorandum with a name in it is a memorandum that a man can be proud of. It does not save the vicar. It saves the officer's conscience, which is a different thing, and I have no conscience left to save, so I will write *a certain priest of the Lublin diocese*, and the file will not have his name in it in my hand, and in twenty years, if anyone looks, they will not find it in my writing."

"That is the strangest kindness I have ever heard of," said Michał.

"It is not a kindness. It is the arithmetic," said Zofia. "Good night, Father."

* * *

He was arrested on the nineteenth of April, at six in the morning, in his cell in the cloister, on a charge of having celebrated a Mass for the students of the University in the church of St. Anne without the permission of the Office.

The charge was true. The permission had been applied for in January and had not been granted and had not been refused, and Michał had said the Mass anyway on the eighteenth of April, the anniversary of the roundup at Mława, and had said it for the dead of his village, and one of the dead was a boy from the tower and one of the dead was a girl of six who had died of the influenza in 1929, and eleven of the dead were men taken from a market square.

He was held for three days, in the cells on Rakowiecka Street, in a room with a bench and a bucket and a bulb that was never turned off.

On the first day the officer was a young man who did not know the file and asked him the same question for six hours. On the second day the officer was an older man with a cold, who read the file and asked him a different question, which was whether he had ever met the woman Wierzbicka from the Office.

"I know the name," said Michał.

"You know the person."

"I know the name."

"You were seen with her," said the older man. "Four times this year. You were seen coming out of the Office on Krucza on the fourteenth of February at four in the afternoon. You were seen in the Bristol on the eighth of March with a woman in a grey coat. Do you know what the Bristol costs? My wife has never been in the Bristol." He sneezed. "Father, I am a simple man. I do not care about the Mass. I care about the woman in the grey coat. What is a priest doing in the Bristol with an official of the Office?"

"We were discussing the drainage," said Michał.

And the officer laughed, and it was a real laugh, an old-fashioned laugh of a man who has heard something silly and human in a long day, and he wrote in his notebook, and he let Michał go on the third day, at four in the afternoon, into the rain, without a charge.

He walked to the church. The doors were open and the nave was dark and the sanctuary lamp was burning in front of the tabernacle. He went up the side aisle to the chapel of Our Lady, and there was a woman in a grey coat, with her hat in her hand, standing under the silver lamp of the side altar.

"You got him out," she said.

"I got myself out." He stood beside her. "The officer asked me what a priest was doing in the Bristol with an official of the Office, and I said we were discussing the drainage, and he laughed."

"Then he is a good man."

"He is a man who has never taken his wife to the Bristol," said Michał, "and I am a man who asked a woman in a grey coat to a café on the eighth of March, and did not tell her why."

Zofia looked at him.

"Why did you ask me?"

"Because they were following me, and I wanted them to follow me to the Bristol," said Michał. "Because a man in Warsaw who is followed every day learns to choose what the follower writes down. I wanted the file to say that the priest of St. Anne's meets the official of the Office in the Bristol on a Saturday afternoon. It is the most useful sentence in a file that a man can put there himself. It says: he is in the room. It says: he can be talked to. It says: he is not going to the barricade."

"And the drainage?"

"The drainage is real," said Michał. "The roof is real. The stove is real. And the four hundred and eleven refusals are real, and every one of them is a village where the church has no roof and the state has no shame, and I am going to spend the rest of my life in the room, and I wanted them to write it down." He turned to her. "I am going to tell you why I did not tell you before you came. Because I knew that you would understand it, and I did not want to be understood by you. It would have been —" He stopped.

"It would have been the loft," said Zofia.

"It would have been the loft."

The rain came down in the courtyard. The lamp above the side altar was silver, and it was the twin of the lamp on the shelf in her kitchen, and it had been made in the same workshop in Warsaw in 1802, and it had come through two wars and had not been melted, and the flame stood up straight in the cold air, and the woman in the grey coat stood under it and looked at the small gold leaf of the fire.

"I signed the paper about the vicar this morning," she said, at last. "The transfer of the vicar of Wąwolnica, and the parish, and the press article, and the line in the memorandum that says *a certain priest of the Lublin diocese.* I signed it at ten o'clock, and at eleven I had a meeting about the statues of the Sacred Heart for the month of June. And at three o'clock I came here, because I knew the date, and I have known the date for fifteen years, and I have never once come."

"You came."

"I came." She put on her hat. "Not for the Mass. I do not believe in the Mass. I came because a man I knew was in a cell, and because I could not do anything, and because the not doing anything is the thing I do now, and I have to stand somewhere while I do it." She pulled the brim down. "Nineteen April. There is no prayer in me for them, Michał. There is only the arithmetic. Eleven men from the market square, five hundred taken, one of them a boy from a mill. I have counted it for fifteen years, and the counting is the only prayer I have, and it is not enough, and it will never be enough, and I am not going to pretend that it is."

Michał took her hand. It was the first time since 1941, and they were in the side aisle of a church, and there was a woman praying at the back, and a man with a broom in the porch, and the rain on the windows; and he held her hand with both of his, and his hands were cold.

"Zosia," he said. "I am going to be a bishop. Not because I want the palace, and not because the Primate is right about the year, although he is. Because if I am a bishop I will be in the room where the decision is made for twenty years, and if I am not a bishop I will be in a room in a parish with a stove, and the room will be smaller every year, and one day the room will be a cell, and I know what a cell is, and I will not go back into one."

"That is a reason to be afraid."

"It is a reason to be a bishop."

She took her hand back. She did it slowly, and the slowly was worse than a pulling away.

"Then we are the same," she said. "I am in the room because the room is the only place where I can do anything, and you are going into the room for the same reason, and the room has one door and it is guarded by men who keep files, and there is no window." She looked up at the silver lamp. "Do you remember what the curse was?"

"I remember."

"*May the bell of this parish ring for everyone you love before it rings for you.*" She looked at him. "She was a good woman and she took the milk to your house every Saturday for a year. But she was right. The bell is going to ring, Michał. It is going to ring for everyone in this room, and the men who keep the files are going to write down the hour, and the hour is going to be in a folder, and the folder is going to be read by a clerk who hates his job." She put her gloves on, one finger at a time. "I am not going to let the bell ring for you. That is the whole of what I have decided. Not God. Not the Party. Not the village. If the bell rings for you, I will be in the room where they write the hour, and I will be the one who writes it. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then we will not speak of this again."

"No."

She went down the side aisle and out into the rain, and he stood under the silver lamp with his hands at his sides, and the woman at the back of the church crossed herself and went out after a while, and the man with the broom finished sweeping the porch, and the door was shut.

* * *

That night Major Stolarczyk came to the Office with a paper and put it on Tosia's desk, because Zofia was not there, and the paper was a request for the transfer of the rector of St. Anne's to the parish of Nowa Huta, and the name of the new rector, a priest from Kielce who had been recommended by the curia and vetted by the Ministry.

Tosia read it. She had been a teacher before the war, and she had lost her husband and her son, and she had been in the Office for six years, and in six years she had learned exactly one piece of politics, which was that a paper put on a desk at night is a paper that gets signed in the morning.

She put it in a drawer, and she locked the drawer, and she telephoned the caretaker's wife at the building on Górnośląska Street, and the caretaker's wife knocked on Zofia's door at nine o'clock and said the party office had called, and would she come in early.

Zofia came in at six.

She read the paper. She read the file that came with it, which was four pages, and which said, in the neat clerk's hand of the Ministry of Public Security, that the rector of St. Anne's had been released from custody on the third day without a charge, and that in the course of the interrogation he had shown no nervousness, and that in the judgment of the interrogating officer he had been uncooperative but not evasive, and that the only matter that had caused him to show any emotion at all had been the mention of the woman Wierzbicka.

She put the paper in her bag and went to see the Minister before he had his coat off.

Sztachelski read it standing up, in the doorway, with his hat still in his hand.

"Who signed it?"

"Stolarczyk has the Ministry's copy. The provincial office wants the transfer in May."

"The provincial office is not the Office," said Sztachelski. "The provincial office is a man in a room in Kielce who wants to be promoted. The transfer of a priest from Warsaw to Nowa Huta is a matter for the central Office, and I am the central Office, and this paper has not been signed by me." He put it on the desk. "You want me to refuse it."

"I want you to refuse it," said Zofia. "The parish is in the middle of a building program. The hall is half built. The roof is half repaired. If you transfer him in May, the program stops, and the children are back in the sacristy, and there is a new rector who will sign anything, and the file will say that the Church took the roof and the state took the parish. If you leave him, the hall is finished in August, and there is a photograph in the paper of the children in a room with a stove, and the Office has a priest it can do business with, and the photograph is worth more than the transfer."

Sztachelski looked at her for a long time.

"All right," he said. "But you will do it as your decision, and you will write the memorandum, and if it goes wrong, the file will show that the decision was taken by the director of the Catholic section, and that the Minister approved it after consultation." He hung up his hat. "Zofia. The Ministry of Public Security does not send me a paper like this one for a parish priest unless someone has been talking. Who has been talking?"

"Colonel Wesoły," said Zofia. "He wants the woman in the grey coat in the Bristol. He has been building it since January."

"Then give him something," said Sztachelski. "A photograph of your face. A name in a memorandum. Something that is worth more to him than the transfer. That is how you survive in this building, Zofia: you pay the colonel before he takes the collection."

She went out and wrote the memorandum, and it was four pages long, and it was the best memorandum of her career: careful, unimpeachable, and entirely false in its reasoning. It said that the retention of the rector of St. Anne's in his post served the interests of the state, because it preserved the contact between the Office and the parish and demonstrated the Office's discrimination and restraint, and it quoted, without attribution, the correspondence of the Office itself, in a way that made it appear that the Minister had already decided.

She signed it. The Minister approved it. The transfer was refused. The rector of St. Anne's went back to his parish and his two hundred and thirty-one children and his half-built hall, and he never knew who had stopped the paper, and in the file of the Office, in the folder for April 1958, there is a document in the hand of Zofia Wierzbicka that is a lie, and the lie consists entirely of true sentences.

And on the last page, in the margin, in pencil, in the small private shorthand that an archivist would take for a filing mark:

*B-a-b-a.*

She was counting. She had been counting for fifteen years, and the counting had a name, and the name was not a name. It was the first syllable a child says.

# Chapter Six: Bread and Salt

*Świerczyna, July 1958*

The telegram came on a Tuesday in the last week of July, and it was eleven words long, and it was from Róża's son.

*MATKA BARDZO CHORA STOP PRZYJEŻDŻAJ STOP MŁAWA*

Mother very ill. Come. Mława.

Zofia had been in the Office since half past six. The telegram was brought up with the post at ten, and she read it standing in the middle of the room with the other letters in her hand, and Tosia said, from the typewriter, without looking up: "Bad news?"

"My sister is dying."

"Then go," said Tosia. "The Lord's work will keep."

Zofia looked at her.

"I mean the Office," said Tosia. "The Office will keep."

* * *

She had not been to Świerczyna in seven years.

She had gone in 1947 — no. Correct that. She had been at Świerczyna in 1947 twice: for the funeral of her grandmother in March, and in September for the sale of the family house. Then she had gone once more, in the spring of 1951, when her mother died in the hospital at Mława of a cancer of the stomach and was brought home to be buried, and Zofia had stood at the graveside in a black coat and gone back the same evening on the last train. And in 1946 Róża had written to her, the first letter in a year, six lines, to say that she was married to Wacek and expecting a child and living in the house of Wacek's mother on the Mława road, and Zofia had sent a parcel with a baby's blanket and a bank note and a letter that took four drafts and said, in the end, that she was well and overworked and thought of them all.

In 1947 Róża had written again, four lines, to say the child was a boy, Antek, and that she had been ill. And then nothing more, and Zofia had sent a parcel every Christmas, and had received, every Easter, a postcard with a hen on it, in Róża's round hand, and the postcards had stopped in 1957, and Zofia had told herself it was the post.

* * *

Róża had tuberculosis, and the left lung was gone, and the right lung was full of holes, and the doctors at the sanatorium in Otwock had sent her home in June because there was nothing more to be done and because the sanatorium needed the bed.

She was forty-one and looked fifty-nine. She lay in the good bed in the good room in the house on the Mława road, in a white gown of her mother's, and the window was open because she liked to hear the birds, and on the table beside her there was a photograph of her husband Wacek in his army uniform, Wacek who had been killed in 1952 by a tree, of all things, a tree that came down on him in the forest where he was working as a labourer for the state forestry, so that the state paid her a pension of two hundred and forty złoty a month and considered the matter closed.

Zofia came into the room at four in the afternoon of the Wednesday, in a hired car from Mława that cost her six hundred złoty and that she would not be able to explain in the Office, and Róża was awake.

"Well," said Róża. "The great lady."

"Rózia."

"Don't cry. I look like a devil and I know it, and crying makes you ugly, and then there will be two ugly women in the room and the boy will be frightened." Róża lifted a hand off the coverlet. "Come here and let me see if Warsaw has made you fat."

Zofia crossed the room and sat down on the chair by the bed and took her sister's hand, and the hand was a bird's hand, and the nails were painted, and Zofia had to look at the painted nails for a while before she could speak.

"Who did your nails?"

"Anka from the post office. She does them for the women in the village, two złoty, and she does a bad job, and I told her to do a bad job. A dying woman with a bad manicure is more respectable than a dying woman with a good one." Róża's breath was short, and she spoke in little runs and rested. "You look well. You look like the photograph in the paper."

"What photograph?"

"Your photograph. In *Trybuna Ludu*, when the bishops came to the Office in the spring. There was a photograph of the table and the backs of everybody's heads, and there was a woman at the end of the table in a grey dress, and Wacek's mother cut it out because she thought it was you, and it was you, and it is on the wall in the kitchen." Róża smiled. "It has been on the wall in the kitchen for four months, Zosia. Wacek's mother, who goes to the church twice on Sunday and once on the Saturday for the souls, has a photograph of a communist official on the wall of her kitchen, and when the priest comes on the first Friday she takes it down and puts it in a drawer, and on the Saturday she puts it back up. That is the whole of the last fifty years in this country, and it took a photograph to show it."

"You have not changed."

"I have changed. I have lost forty pounds and my left lung and my husband and my patience." Róża closed her eyes. "But I have not lost the tongue. Sit with me. Tell me about the great lady's life."

They talked for two hours. Zofia told her about the Office, and about the flat, and about the electric kettle that had been promised, and Róża laughed at the kettle and then coughed for a long time, and when the coughing was over she said, "You are going to tell me about the man," and Zofia said, "What man?" and Róża said, "The priest. From the village. Do you think a village forgets? The whole parish knows where he is. When he was made the rector in Warsaw in the year of the thaw, it was in the *Gość Niedzielny* under a photograph, and every woman in Świerczyna cut it out. He came to the parish in 1956, for the mission, and said the Mass in the field beyond the mill because the church is not built, and there were two thousand people, and my Antek was one of the boys who carried the banner, and I stood at the back with my hand on my side and wept like a fool." She opened her eyes. "Have you seen him?"

"Yes."

"Ah," said Róża. "There it is."

"It is not what you think."

"It is exactly what I think. I am dying, Zosia, and dying gives a woman a certain license, and I am going to say a thing to you that no one else in the world will say to you." Róża raised herself a little on the pillows. "You are a great lady with a photograph on the wall of a kitchen and a flat in Mokotów and a car with a driver, and you are the loneliest person I have ever met, and when you came into this room you looked at the door three times before you sat down. You are not afraid of the dying. You are afraid of the living. You have been afraid since the day you went east in 1944, and you have built a whole life out of being useful so that no one would notice."

"Róża."

"I am dying. You will be in a room with him, and the two of you will be very careful, and you will do good, and it will be the finest good, and it will be worth nothing, because the one thing a man like that can never be given is a woman who is careful." Róża put her hand on Zofia's wrist. "Take him back to the river. Not in the summer. In the winter, when the ice is on it. Take him back to the bend and lie down on the ice with him and listen to the bell, and let him be a boy for an hour, and then let him be a bishop, and you will have done more for him than the Office and the Primate and God put together."

Zofia sat with her sister's hand on her wrist.

"Do you know what he is?" she said. "Do you know what they will say? A bishop and a woman from the Office. In the same car. In the winter. It is a photograph, Rózia. It is the photograph in the file. It is the end of him."

"Good," said Róża. "Then let it be the end of him, and let him be a man for a night first. It would be a better end than the one they have planned for him, which is a long life in a palace with a stove."

* * *

The village had changed, and the village had not changed.

There were eighty houses now instead of sixty-one, because the state had built twelve in 1952 for the families of the men at the new dairy, and they were square and grey and identical and had slate roofs, and the people in them were called the *nowi*, the new ones, and their children went to a new school with a stove. The old school with no windows had been pulled down and made into a bus stop. The mill was still there and the wheel was still there and the mill was not a mill but a co-operative for the collection of milk, and Stanisław Żurawski had died in 1953 and Ania Żurawska still lived in the house beside the mill with a dog and a hen and a wall of photographs.

The church was not there. There was the wall, and the tower, and inside the walls a wooden platform with an altar on it and a roof of tarred boards and a sign painted on a board at the entrance with the name of the parish and the hour of the Masses. There had been an application for a building permit in 1949, and one in 1952, and one in 1955, and in 1956 the parish had been given permission to build a new church and had collected the money and the plans had gone to the province and the province had required the drainage study, and the drainage study had required the hydrology, and the hydrology was in the hands of an institute in Warsaw that had not responded, and the years had gone by.

Zofia went to see the ruins on the Thursday morning, alone, in her grey coat, with no driver in the car.

She stood in the nave of the church that was not there. The grass grew between the flags. The tower still held its bell, and there was a rope hanging from it, and a swallow's nest under the belfry, and the bell was cracked, and it had been cracked for three hundred years, and they rang it for the Mass and for the dead and for the fire.

She was standing there with her hands at her sides when the priest came out of the sacristy of the temporary church with a bucket.

He was in his shirtsleeves, with his collar and an apron, and he was carrying a bucket and a mop, and he stopped when he saw her, and then he set the bucket down on the flags, and came across the grass.

"Pani director," he said.

"Father."

"The Office does not usually inspect parishes in July."

"The Office is not inspecting the parish." She looked up at the tower. "My sister is dying in the village. I have come to see my sister."

"I know," said Michał. "I am saying the Mass for her on Friday. She has asked for the old rite with the St. Michael prayer, and I have told her that the bishop will not like it, and she has told me that the bishop can come and sing it himself."

Zofia laughed. It was an ugly little laugh, and it went out across the ruins and came back.

"She is the last of us," she said.

"She is the last of the village that was." Michał picked up the bucket. "There is a place where the roof caved in behind the sacristy in 1945 and the wall went with it, and the wall has never been repaired, and the children go through it. The provincial office has said the wall is a monument and may not be repaired without the consent of the conservator. It is a ruin of a church that the Germans burned, and it is a monument, and the conservator has been in Mława since 1952 and has not been to the parish once." He shrugged. "So the children go through the hole, and I have put a board on the other side so they can come through it without the wall falling on them, and the board is a violation of the conservation order, and I have written to the conservator to say so. He has not answered."

"How long have you been here?"

"Since Monday. I came for Róża." He looked at the bucket. "The rector of Mława has the whole deanery and a heart condition, and there is no vicar in Świerczyna since 1956, and I am the only priest within nine miles who can hear a confession in the Pomorze dialect and get it right. I came on Monday and I will stay until she dies, and the bishop has given me the fortnight, and the parish of St. Anne's is being looked after by a professor of philosophy from the seminary who is a very good man and cannot find the sacristy."

They stood in the ruins.

"It has been seven years," said Zofia.

"Seven years."

"There was a letter," she said. "In 1946. It came back to me. I never opened it."

"I know," said Michał. "I wrote it in the camp, in the infirmary, in July 1944, and I sent it to the curia at Mława, and the curate read it to my parents in the kitchen of the mill, and my mother put it in the Bible at the page of the births, and the Bible is in the house by the mill, under the stone in the floor, where it has been since 1944." He set down the bucket. "I did not write again. I had told you not to wait. I had told you in writing, and the writing was the best thing I had ever done, and I thought that a man who has told a woman not to wait must keep his word, because if he breaks it he is not a man who has been generous, he is a man who has been playing."

"You were nineteen."

"I was twenty-four. I was twenty-four and I was in the infirmary and I was weighing forty-one kilos and I had a fever of forty degrees, and I wrote it because I was afraid I would die and leave you with nothing, and because I was too proud to write the other thing, which is: wait for me, I am coming back, and I am afraid that you will not be there." He looked up at the tower. "I have had fourteen years to think of what I should have written. And I did not write it, and it is not your doing, and it is not mine, and the years are gone, and the two of us are standing in the ruins of a church in July 1958, and there is a bucket."

"There is a bucket," said Zofia.

"Will you come to the Mass on Friday?"

"No."

"Then come to the house on Saturday," said Michał, "because she has asked for you, and because there is a thing she wants to say and she will not say it to me, and because my mother will be there, and my mother has asked about you every Christmas for twelve years, and I have had nothing to tell her."

* * *

Ania Żurawska was seventy-two years old and had the shoulders of a woman who had carried two children and a mill and a war, and she was in the kitchen of the house by the mill when Zofia came down the lane on Saturday, and she came to the door and looked at her for a long moment.

"You are thin," she said. "All my life I have been the woman who tells people they are thin. It is the only thing I can still say."

"Pani Aniu."

"Come in. There is a cake."

She had baked a cake. In July 1958, in a village, for a woman of the Office, Ania Żurawska had baked a cake, and she put it on the table with the tea and the sugar and a little plate of butter, and she sat down opposite Zofia and folded her hands and looked at her.

"I want to tell you a thing," she said, "and then we will eat the cake."

"Yes."

"When they took my son in 1943, I went to the church in Mława every day for a year. Every day, in the dark, before the curate was up, I went to the church and I stood in front of the altar of the Mother of God and I said: give him back. Not *let him live.* Give him back. I did not say it as a prayer. I said it as a demand, because I was a woman whose son had been taken in a market square by men who had no right, and I did not have anything else to say to God, and I had been saying it for a year when the curate came to me in the March and said that the note had come through the Red Cross and that the note had his number on it and that the number meant he was in a place where they gave numbers." She lifted her chin. "And I put the number in the Bible and I wrote under it *żyje* — he lives — and I went home and I baked bread. Not because I believed it. Because it was the only thing in the world I could do that day."

"I know," said Zofia.

"You know what it is to have a thing in your hands that is the only thing." Ania nodded. "Then you know why I am going to speak to you as I would speak to a woman in my own kitchen and not to a great lady from Warsaw."

"Pani Aniu, I am the great lady from Warsaw."

"You are the girl who was sick on the ice in 1938," said Ania Żurawska. "I know what you are. I have known what you are since my son came home from the camp in 1945 with his eyes gone, and the first thing he asked for — before the soup, before his father, before the fire — was the chronicle. He asked for the chronicle, and I had it, and I gave it to him, and he sat in the kitchen until the middle of the night with the chronicle in his hands and did not open it. And then he said, *she carried my letters.* Just that. *She carried my letters.* And he went to the seminary in the spring." She put her hands flat on the table. "So I am going to say the thing I have been saying in my heart every Christmas when the parcel came from Warsaw with the blanket and the tea, and the parcel never had a name in it, only the name of the shop. The parcel never said *from Zosia.*"

"It said *from Z. Wierzbicka, Office for Religious Denominations,*" said Zofia. "It was the only paper I had."

"And now you are on the wall of Wacek's mother's kitchen in a photograph, and my son is a rector in Warsaw, and the two of you are in the same city and the same file and the same photograph in the newspaper, and the women of this parish cut it out and say: look, the boy from the mill. And nobody says: look, the girl from the school." Ania stood up. "Do you know why nobody says it?"

"No."

"Because the girl from the school has made herself into a thing that cannot be said," said Ania Żurawska. "You took the thing that was in you and you gave it to the state, and the state put a photograph of its table on the wall, and there is no place in the world for a woman who is a photograph in the newspaper of the state, and I am telling you this in my kitchen in July, and if you do not hear it now you will hear it when the bell rings." She put a hand on Zofia's shoulder. "Eat the cake. It has the last of the good flour from before the war."

* * *

The Mass on Friday was for Róża, and she died on the Saturday morning at ten minutes past five, with the window open and her son asleep on the chair beside her, and the whole village knew before six o'clock because the bell rang.

It rang for a long time. Zofia was in the yard, and the sound came out over the roofs and the mill and the river and the bend, and it went through her chest the way it had when she was six years old and could not have said what it was, and she stood with her hands at her sides and her face lifted and let it ring.

The funeral was on the Tuesday, and it was the largest funeral the village had had in years, because Róża Wierzbicka had been a woman whom everyone in the parish owed something: she had nursed two of the village women in 1947 when the doctor was in Mława and there was no petrol, she had carried food to the old ones in 1946, and in 1953 she had stood up in a meeting in the co-operative and told a man from the party committee that the price of the milk was a sin, and the man had gone back to Mława and written her name in a book, and in the village the book was called the book of the women who cannot be given a job.

Michał said the Mass in the field beyond the mill, because the temporary church held eighty and there were five hundred. There was a platform of boards and a table with the altar, and four tall candles, and the men of the parish had rigged a canvas against the sun, and the bishop of Płock had given permission for the Mass outside the church on the ground that the church was a ruin and the parish had no building that could hold the people, which was true and which had the useful property of being a decision the Office could not appeal, because there was no document.

Zofia stood at the back. There was a woman from the Office at a funeral Mass in the field beyond the mill, and the village knew exactly who she was, and the village had decided, without a meeting, to be polite and nothing more; and the women nodded to her, and the men touched their caps, and the children looked at her the way children look at a woman who has a hired car and a grey coat and no husband.

She had not heard him preach in the open air since 1939.

He spoke about Róża. She had been at the church on Sunday for fifty weeks of the year, and the other two weeks she had been ill; she had sung alto in the choir that was now four women and a boy with a wheeze; she had been the woman who came at six in the morning to light the stove in the sacristy because the sacristan was old and his hands were bad. He said: "She had a temper that was famous in this parish, and she had a tongue that could cut a man's tie without untying it, and she was afraid of nothing except the dark, and she made her son leave the lamp on in the corridor until he was nine years old, and she would not have wanted me to say so."

The village laughed, all at once, and the laugh went across the field like a wind through wheat.

Then he said: "And she was a woman who carried a thing to the end. That is the whole of the Christian life, and it is the whole of the life of this village, and it is the only thing that any of us will be able to say at the last. She carried it. We do not know what it was. Some of us carry a child, and some carry a grudge, and some carry a letter that was never opened, and some carry a photograph in the newspaper of a table in Warsaw. She carried it, and she carried it in this field and this lane and this ruin of a church, and she did not put it down, and now the carrying is over, and the thing she carried is not lost, because she carried it into this field and gave it to us. We are the ones who carry it now. It is a heavy thing. It is the only thing we have."

After the burial, the village came to the house on the Mława road with bread and salt.

That is the custom: the neighbours bring bread and salt to the house of the dead, because the dead have gone to the table where there is no hunger and the living are the ones who need the bread. Wacek's mother had baked six loaves in the night, and the women brought the salt in saucers, and the loaves were set out on the table in the good room with a knife, and the people came in and cut a piece and dipped it in the salt and ate it standing up, and said a word about Róża, and went out through the yard.

Zofia stood at the table all afternoon with the knife in her hand, cutting bread for the village.

She had not done it since she was a girl. It was the woman of the house who cut the bread, and she was the last of the Wierzbickis, and so it was her place, and the village accepted it without a word, and she stood there from noon until six in the black dress she had worn to her mother's funeral and cut the bread and dipped it in the salt and gave it to each of them, and the people came and took it and ate it and looked at her, and some of them said "thank you, pani Zofio" and some of them said nothing, and one old woman took her hand and said, "You are Jan's girl," and went out.

At six o'clock the priest came in.

He was in his cassock with the stole folded in his hand, and he had been at the graveside for two hours while the men filled it in, and the shoulders of the cassock were grey with dust.

He came to the table.

"Pani," he said.

"Father."

"Cut me a piece."

She cut the bread and dipped it in the salt and gave it to him, and he ate it standing, with his eyes on her face, and the room was full of the smell of bread and the sweat of the summer and the flowers that had been on the coffin.

"Bread and salt," he said. "You know what it means?"

"It means welcome."

"It means welcome, and it means the covenant, and it means a stranger who comes into a house and is fed is a stranger who cannot be harmed in it." He took the last of the bread from her hand. "In this parish, in 1943, when the men were taken, the women brought bread and salt to the house of every woman whose man had been taken, and they left it on the step, and they did not knock, because to knock would be to say that the man was dead. And in the morning my mother went out and there were eleven saucers of salt on the step, and a loaf on every saucer, and she brought them in and she put them on the table, and she said: *The village has made a covenant with us.*" He looked at Zofia. "There has been a covenant between the village and my family since the war. And there has been no covenant between the village and yours, because your father was taken and there was no house left for the bread, and your mother went to her mother's, and the village brought the bread to the wrong door."

"Father."

"I am going to the house by the mill now," said Michał, "and my mother will give me supper, and then I am going to sit in the kitchen with the chronicle, because it is the only thing in that house that has not been eaten by the state or the war or the years, and I am going to read the entries from 1937, and if you want to read them with me, the door is open, and there is a covenant."

He went out.

She cut the bread for another hour, and then the people stopped coming, and the room was empty, and Wacek's mother began to clear the table, and Zofia put the knife down and went out of the house and down the lane in the dusk.

* * *

The kitchen of the mill house had a lamp on the table, and the window was open to the river, and Ania Żurawska was asleep in the chair by the stove with her hands in her lap, and Michał was at the table with the chronicle open in front of him and the Bible beside it, and on the Bible was the letter from the camp, unfolded, and the card with the number on it.

Zofia stood in the doorway.

"Sit down," said Michał. "I am reading the entry for the nineteenth of December 1937."

She sat down.

"*Michał fell through the ice by the mill,*" he read, "*and I pulled him out and he would not let go of the bag with the flour in it and it is all ruined. He says he would have let go of the flour before he let go of me. I have written it down so that he can never take it back.*"

He turned the page.

"*Twenty-fifth of December 1938. Zosia and I lay on the ice at the bend and heard the bell. It was only the church bell. The story is a lie. This is a great disappointment to K. Wróbel. We are closing the chronicle for the winter. Nothing else will happen.*"

He turned the page.

"*Thirty-first of August 1939. They have moved the horses out of the mill to Mława. Father says we are to burn the grain in the loft if it comes to it, and Mother says things do not come to it. There is a moon like a knife. Zosia is at the church with the bandages. I am going to the church. I have a thing to say to her and I am going to say it tonight before anything else happens.*"

He closed the chronicle.

"There is nothing after it."

"The planes came at dawn," said Zofia.

"The planes came at dawn, and I did not say the thing, and I have had nineteen years to say it, and I have said it to myself every day in the camp and every day in the seminary and every day in the Office of the Primate and every day in the church of St. Anne, and I have never once said it to you." He put his hand flat on the chronicle. "I said it in a letter, and the letter is there, and the letter says *I am coming back.* It does not say *I love you.* A man of twenty-four in an infirmary does not write *I love you*, because *I love you* is a thing that a man writes when he has a house and a wage and a future, and I had a number and a fever. So I wrote it in the only language I had, which was the chronicle, and I said that in my chronicle you were on the ice at the bend, and that the bell was not the church bell, and I sent it to a girl in the village to read out loud in the kitchen." He looked at her. "And you kept it for fifteen years in a tin without opening it. And I have known that since April, because it is in the file. The Office's file says: *letter of 1946 returned to sender, undelivered, filed by addressee.* The file has your handwriting on the envelope, Zosia. The file has the date."

The lamp hissed. In the chair by the stove, Ania Żurawska slept on.

"Why did you not open it?" he said.

"Because you told me not to wait," said Zofia. "And I had obeyed you. I had obeyed you for twenty-seven years, and I was not going to open a letter that would tell me that the obedience had been right." She looked at the letter on the Bible. "I was a girl who was going to be somebody who decides what happens. And I decided. I decided in 1946, in a room in Warsaw, with a letter in my hand and a Party card in my bag, that the girl on the ice was dead, and that the woman in the room was alive, and that a dead girl does not read her letters. And then I put it in a tin and I kept it, because I was not quite able to put it in the stove." She looked up. "You want to know what I am, Michał. That is what I am. A woman who could not put a letter in a stove."

He reached across the table and took her hand, and this time she did not take it back.

"Tell me the thing," she said.

"The thing?"

"The thing you were going to say on the thirty-first of August. You have had nineteen years. Tell me now."

Michał looked at her across the chronicle.

"I was going to say: Zosia, marry me in the spring. And I was going to say: I have half a mill and a chronicle and forty-one entries in which I came when you were in trouble, and I am going to be a man they look at, but I would rather be a man you look at, and if you will have me I will be at the mill until I die, and if you will not have me I will go to America and be a miller in Chicago and never come back." He smiled, and it was the smile of the boy in the loft. "And I was going to be very dignified about it, and I had rehearsed it in the hayloft, and my hands were sweating."

"You were nineteen and your hands were sweating."

"I was nineteen and my hands were sweating, and the next morning the planes came over, and I never said it, and I have said it in this kitchen tonight in July 1958 to a woman in a black dress who is the deputy director of the Catholic section of the Office for Religious Denominations." He held her hand. "What do you say?"

Zofia sat very still.

"I say that we are in the kitchen of the mill with your mother asleep by the stove," she said, "and that I have a car at Róża's house that I must return to Mława by nine, and that tomorrow there is a boy of twelve who has lost his mother, and that you have the parish of St. Anne's and a bishop who is dying and a Primate who is a wall, and that I have a file with your name in it, page eighty-eight, and the file says that we are *contact material.*"

"I did not ask about the file."

"You did not have to. The file is in the room with us. It is in every room we have been in since January." She took her hand back, and it was the second time, and both times it was slow. "I have decided to be in the room, Michał. Not on the ice. The ice is gone. It went out in the spring of 1939 with the last of the winter and it has never come back, and the girl who lay on it with you is in a tin with a letter, and I am the woman who is left." She stood up. "You will be a bishop. Do you understand what that means? Not the palace. The photograph. If I am with you in any way that can be photographed, you will be the bishop who has a woman in Warsaw, and they will not need to write a single lie. They will take a photograph and put it in a newspaper and let the village draw its own conclusion, and it will be the right conclusion, and it will destroy you, and it will be the truth."

"Then let it be the truth."

"No," said Zofia. "Because if they destroy you with the truth, they destroy the children. The hall will not be built. The stove will not come. The two hundred and thirty-one children will be in the sacristy and the street. And you will be a saint in a photograph, and saints do not build halls." She put on her gloves. "I am going back to Warsaw in the morning, and in the autumn they will come for the Novena, and the only thing that stands between the village and the state is a man in a room with the door shut. I will keep the door shut. That is the covenant. Not bread and salt. The door shut."

She went to the door.

"Zosia."

She stopped.

"The curse," he said. "The widow's words. *May the bell ring for everyone you love before it rings for you.* I have thought about it for nineteen years, and I have decided that it was not a curse at all." He was standing now, with the lamp behind him. "She did not curse me. She told me the truth about my own life, which is that I would be a man who rings for other people. And that is what I have become. And the woman who said it took the milk to our house every Saturday for a year, and when she died, in the spring of this year, I said the Mass, and I asked her forgiveness at the grave, and I forgave her, and I did not hear a bell. Not once. Not in fourteen years." He came around the table. "The only time I have heard the bell was in 1938, on the ice, with you. And every year on the sixth of January, in the church of St. Anne, when the lamp is lit and the whole church is full, I hear it. I do not know where it comes from. I hear it, Zosia."

Zofia stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"Then it is the church bell," she said, "and the story is a lie."

And she went out into the dark.

# Chapter Seven: The Picture

*Warsaw and the villages, August–December 1958*

On the fourth of August the Minister of Education signed the circular, and the state began to take the crucifixes out of the schoolrooms.

It was done quietly, and it was done with a document, and the document was circulated to the school directors of the whole country in the week before the new term. The circular was four pages long and it was written in the language of the Office: the secular character of the school, the neutrality of the state toward the beliefs of its citizens, the necessity of removing the symbols that might suggest a religious character in an institution of public education. The crucifixes were to come down. The religious instruction was to be given only after the compulsory lessons, one hour a week, by teachers who were not members of religious orders. The emblems were to be removed from the walls of every classroom in Poland before the first of September.

In the Office on Krucza Street it was called the fourth of August, and the men who wrote the circular were in the next building, and nobody in the Office for Religious Denominations had been asked for an opinion, because the circular did not concern religion. It concerned the schools.

* * *

Michał had the hall by then.

It had been finished on the nineteenth of August, eleven days late, with the roof repaired over the nave and a stove in the corner that had been bought second-hand in the market at Kercelak by the parish council for two thousand nine hundred złoty, and the children's chairs and the long tables, and a cupboard for the catechism books that had been made by the old carpenter from the crate the altar came in, and the whole thing had cost more than the parish had, and the Office had paid a part of it through a fund for the restoration of historic buildings, because the wall of the hall was a party wall of the old churchyard and the conservator had ruled that a party wall of a historic churchyard could be restored from the fund, and the conservator had ruled it a week after Zofia Wierzbicka had gone to see him.

The opening was on a Sunday in September, and there was a ribbon across the door of the hall, and two hundred and thirty-one children, and the professor of philosophy from the seminary who had been looking after the parish in July said a prayer that was short enough for the children to follow, and the rector of St. Anne's cut the ribbon with a pair of scissors borrowed from the sacristy, and the parish council served tea and a cake that had been made in the presbytery kitchen.

The Office was not invited. The Office knew about the opening from a report, which said: *Uroczystość otwarcia pawilonu katechetycznego. Frekwencja ok. 300 osób. Obecni: ks. rektor, prof. T., przedstawiciele rady parafialnej. Przemówienia: krótkie. Nastroje: spokojne. Nie odnotowano wystąpień antypaństwowych.* Solemn opening of the catechetical pavilion. Attendance about 300. Present: the rector, Prof. T., members of the parish council. Speeches: brief. Mood: calm. No anti-state incidents noted.

At the bottom of the report, in Zofia's hand: *Przyjąć do wiadomości. Pawilon oddany do użytku zgodnie z protokołem z 14.02. Sprawę zamknąć.* Acknowledge. Pavilion delivered as per the protocol of 14.02. Close the matter.

She closed it. It was the only file she closed in 1958 with any pleasure.

* * *

The fourth of August happened in the villages.

In Warsaw it was a document and a meeting. In the villages and the small towns it was a man with a ladder and a school director with a letter. In a school in the parish of Radzymin the crucifix had hung above the blackboard since 1922 and had been carried there from the church in a procession with two hundred people, and the director took it down himself, in the evening, when the children were gone, and carried it to the church and gave it to the priest and said, "I have done what I was told, and I am ashamed, and I am not going to apologize to you, because I have a wife and three children and the alternative is the pavement." In a school in the parish of Wąwolnica the director took the crucifix down and put it in a cupboard and the children came to school the next day and saw the clean wall and one of them began to cry, and the teacher wept too, and the whole thing lasted ten minutes and there is no record of it anywhere except in the memory of a woman who was eight years old.

And in a village in the powiat of Mława, nine miles from Świerczyna, the school director was a man of fifty-five named Bolesław Kot, who had been a teacher before the war and had taught in secret through the occupation and had been in the camps and had come back to teach, and who on the first of September 1958 stood in front of his class with the letter in his hand and read it out loud, and then took the crucifix down off the wall and put it on the desk, and said to the children:

"Your parents will be told that the school is secular. I am telling you what the school is. This is a school in a village in Poland, and the state has told me to take the cross down, and I have taken it down, and I am not going to lie to you about why. It is because I have a pension and a wife and I am too old to be a hero and too honest to be a liar. The cross is on the desk. The cross will be here until the inspector comes, and then it will go into the drawer, and when the inspector has gone it will come out of the drawer. That is what the state has done to your teacher. It has made him a man with a cross in a drawer. I am sorry."

The children told their parents. The parents told the priest. The priest of that parish was a young man named Kaczorowski, and he wrote a letter to the bishop, and the letter found its way to the Primate's chancery, and pani Okońska read it and copied it and sent the copy to the Office, and on the top of the copy she wrote, in the neat hand of a secretary who had been doing this since 1931: *Do wiadomości Pani Dyrektor. B.K. — dobry człowiek.* For the information of the director. B.K. — a good man.

Zofia read the letter on the thirteenth of September, in the afternoon, and put it in the file, and then took it out again and read it twice more.

* * *

The Novena had a picture.

It was a copy of the icon of Częstochowa, painted in 1957 by an artist in Kraków at the Primate's request, in the size of the original, and blessed by the Pope in Rome in May 1957, and sent out from Warsaw in August 1957 to travel to every parish in Poland: nine years, a thousand parishes, the Mother of God carried from village to village in a van with a police escort that the state provided because it was afraid of the crowds and did not want an accident.

By the summer of 1958 the picture had been in the archdiocese of Warsaw and in the dioceses of Płock and Łomża and was on its way to the south, and in every parish where it had been, the report of the Office said the same thing: the crowds were enormous, the confessions were thousands, the young people came at night with candles, and the party committee in the district wrote to Warsaw to say that the militia could not guarantee public order at the crossings.

In the Office the Novena was not a religious event. It was a moving crowd, and a moving crowd was a political fact, and the political fact had to be administered.

The Minister had written his plan in the spring. The plan had been approved in June by a secretariat that did not put its name on paper, and the plan had four points: the permits, the processions, the press, and the schools. The permits were already being refused. The processions were being rerouted. The press was writing about the Church's wealth and the peasants' superstition. And the schools were being secularized, and the two things were the same thing, because the picture went into the villages where the state had just taken the cross out of the schoolroom.

Zofia had read the plan and had written her dissent in a memorandum that the Minister had read and returned to her with a note: *Nie zgadzam się z wnioskiem, ale doceniam odwagę. S.* I do not agree with the conclusion, but I value the courage. S.

Her dissent had said that the plan would not work, and her reason was the reason she had learned in 1943 in the forest, and she had written it in the language of an official: *Każde zdjęcie krzyża ze ściany tworzy w danej miejscowości jedno ognisko oporu, którego nie da się zlikwidować, dopóki żyje choć jedno dziecko tej szkoły.* Every removal of a cross from a wall creates, in that locality, a centre of resistance which cannot be liquidated while a single child of that school is alive.

* * *

The picture came to Świerczyna in October.

It was to come on a Thursday, and the parish had had three weeks' notice, and the village had spent the three weeks in a fever of preparation: the men had cleared the road from the Mława highway and filled the holes with gravel from the river, the women had made the birch arches with the last of the birch from the copse behind the mill, the girls had made a carpet of flower petals and rye and fir branches for the last two hundred yards, and the children had been rehearsed by the sacristan in the singing of the hymn that had been composed for the Novena, which they sang in a version that the village had made for itself out of two other hymns.

Permission for the procession on the road was refused on the Monday.

The refusal came from the presidium of the district council in Mława, and it gave as its reason the state of the road and the safety of the public, and it was signed by a man named Chudzik, who had been the secretary of the party committee in the district since 1954, and who had been at the school in Świerczyna in 1938 as a boy of nine, and whose mother was still in the village.

The priest came to Mława on the Tuesday. He was not the rector of St. Anne's; the rector of St. Anne's was in Warsaw and could not be spared twice in one month, and the parish of Świerczyna was being served by an old priest from Mława named Dąbrowski, who was sixty-eight and had a wooden leg from 1944 and had been the curate of the parish under Father Sokołowski before the war.

Old Father Dąbrowski went to Mława on the Tuesday and came back on the Wednesday and stood in the co-operative and told the village what had happened.

He had gone to the presidium. He had sat in the corridor for two hours. He had been received by a young official who had told him that the decision of the presidium was final. He had asked to see comrade Chudzik. He had been told that comrade Chudzik was in Warsaw. He had gone to the church and sat in the church for an hour, and then he had gone back to the presidium and sat in the corridor again, and at four o'clock a woman in the office had come out and given him a piece of paper, and the piece of paper said that permission was refused, and that the refusal could be appealed to the province.

"And what did you say?" said a man in the co-operative.

"I said what I have said for forty years," said Father Dąbrowski. "I said, *Bóg zapłać,* and I put my hat on, and I came home."

There was a silence in the co-operative. Then a woman in the corner said:

"Then there is no picture."

"There is no picture in the procession," said Father Dąbrowski. "There will be the Mass in the morning in the field, and the picture will come by the highway in the van, and it will come to the church by the road, and it will not come through the village, and the bishop has said that we are not to have a demonstration, and the bishop is right." He looked at them. "We are not going to have a demonstration. We are going to have a Mass."

And a man in the co-operative said: "Father, the birch arches are up."

"Then take them down," said Father Dąbrowski.

Nobody moved.

* * *

Ania Żurawska wrote to her son in Warsaw.

She wrote on the Saturday, in the hand she had learned in the village school in 1901, and she wrote four pages, and the letter arrived at the presbytery of St. Anne's on the Tuesday, and Michał read it standing up in the corridor, and then sat down on the stairs and read it again.

*My son. The picture did not come through the village. They had the arches up and the children had been rehearsed and the carpet was made of the last of the rye and the petals, and on the Thursday the van came by the highway and turned at the cross and went to the church by the road, and the whole village stood in the lanes with nothing to do with their hands. The children cried. I am ashamed to tell you that the old women cried too and I was one of them.*

*Father Dąbrowski said the Mass in the field and there were nine hundred people, and he preached for eleven minutes, and he said: we are not going to have a demonstration, we are going to have a Mass. And the village went home.*

*There is a thing I have to tell you. On the Friday, the day after, the school in the next parish (B.K. is the teacher, do you remember him, he was a boy from Grzybowo) — the school inspector came on the Friday and the cross was in the drawer, and B.K. took it out and gave it to the inspector, and the inspector put it in his bag and went away, and on the Monday B.K. came to school and there was no cross and he taught the children the multiplication tables.*

*On the Monday night somebody broke the windows of the school. Four windows, in the night, with a stone. And on the Tuesday the militia came from Mława and asked B.K. who had done it, and B.K. said he did not know, and the militia said that B.K. had done it himself for the sympathy, and they took him to Mława in the car, and he is there now, and his wife is in the school with the children and the broken windows and no glass.*

*My son, I am an old woman and I do not understand the world. But I understand this. The village is not the same village as the one you left. In 1943 we were afraid of the Germans and we knew who the enemy was. Now there is no enemy. There is the state, which is everybody's son and everybody's cousin, and there is the priest, and there is the party, and the man who breaks the window of the school in the night is a man from the village, and in the morning he goes to the co-operative and buys his bread with the rest of us.*

*I have not been to confession since the picture. I do not know why. Write to me, and do not write to me about the bishop, write to me about yourself, and tell me if you are eating.*

*Your mother.*

* * *

He went to see the woman from the Office.

It was the last week of October, and he went to the building on Krucza Street without an appointment at four in the afternoon, and Tosia looked at him over the typewriter and said, "She is in the Ministry until six."

"I will wait."

He waited for two hours in the corridor, in his black coat, with his hat on his knee, and the clerks went past him and looked at him and looked away, and at a quarter past six Zofia came up the stairs with her briefcase and a folder under her arm, and stopped on the landing.

"Father Żurawski."

"Pani director. I have come to ask you a question, and I am going to ask it in the corridor, because if I ask it in your office you will answer it as an official, and I have had enough official answers for one year."

Tosia got up and went into the other room and shut the door.

"Ask," said Zofia.

"The picture is going to the diocese of Kielce in November," said Michał. "It will be in the cathedral for three days, and there will be a Mass in the square on the third day, and the bishop of Kielce has asked me to preach it. It is not a procession. It is a Mass, in a square, in front of a picture, and the square is a public place, and the permit for the Mass has been granted, and the Office has granted it, and I have seen the paper." He looked at her. "Do you know about the paper?"

"I signed the paper," said Zofia.

"Then you know what it is. It is the one Mass in the whole of Poland this autumn where the picture and the people and the bishop are in a public square with the permission of the state. And the state has granted it because the bishop of Kielce is a man who has been ill for two years and has not made trouble, and because the province wants a demonstration that the Church is free." He took a step toward her. "And I am going to preach it. And I have a text, and the text is the one thing I have been saving for four years, and if I preach it, the Office will close the door of the room, and the hall and the stove and the two hundred and thirty-one children will be a photograph in a file, and I will be a bishop who made a speech in a square in Kielce, and everything we have built since February will be over."

Zofia looked at him.

"What is the text?"

"*Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and unto God the things that are God's.*" He said it quietly. "And I am going to say that the state has been rendering unto Caesar for ten years, and has taken the things of God and called them the things of Caesar, and that a schoolroom with a cross in a drawer is a room in which the state has decided that the multiplication tables belong to Caesar and the cross does not, and that the men who made that decision are not wicked, they are afraid, and that a Christian does not fight the afraid, a Christian gives them the thing that will make them unafraid, which is a school that teaches the multiplication tables *and* the cross, and a state that can allow a cross on a wall because it is not afraid of a piece of wood."

"That is not a sermon," said Zofia. "That is a program."

"It is a program. It is the program of a Church that is not going to the barricade and is not going to the drawer. It is the only program that can work in this country, and it has to be said out loud in a public square by a man who is going to be a bishop, or it is nothing." He stood with his hat in his hands. "And you are going to tell me not to say it. And I am going to ask you what the price is."

Zofia went to the window at the end of the corridor and stood looking down into the courtyard, where the lamps were coming on and the guard was locking the gate.

"The price is not the door of the room," she said. "The Minister will not close the door. He is a doctor, and a doctor does not close the door on a man who can talk, and he has been waiting for a priest like you since 1956. The price is the Ministry of Public Security, which has a file, and the file has a photograph in it now, and the photograph is of a woman in a grey coat, and the colonel has been building the file since January, and if you stand up in a square in Kielce and say what you have just said to me, the colonel will read the sermon and he will go to the Minister and he will say: the priest is in the room because of the woman, and the woman is in the room because of the priest, and the two of them are a single instrument, and the instrument is in the hands of the Primate. And the Minister will have to act, because a minister who is told that one of his officers is an instrument must act, and the act will not be against you. It will be against me."

"Then I will not preach it."

"Then you will not be a bishop."

"Then I will not be a bishop." He came down the corridor and stood in front of her. "Zosia. I have been in the room for two years. I know what the room is. I know what it costs and I know who pays. I have watched you pay for the paper about the vicar of Wąwolnica. I have watched you pay for the paper about the transfer in April. I have watched you carry the file on your shoulder like a woman carrying a sack of flour up a staircase, and I have not said a word, because the room was the only place where anything could be done." He put his hat on. "And now there is a square in Kielce, and a picture, and a thousand people, and a text that I have been saving for four years, and I am going to preach it, and you are going to be in the front row, in the grey coat, with the colonel behind you, and you are going to be the woman who signed the permit for the Mass. And when the colonel reads the sermon, he will see that the sermon is addressed to him. Not to the bishop. Not to the Party. To a man of forty-three with two daughters and a chess game on Sunday, who is afraid, and who has been told his whole life that the Church is the enemy of the people. And I am going to say: come into the room. There is a chair for you. There has always been a chair for you, and the Church has been too proud to put it out, and the state has been too afraid to sit in it."

The corridor was dark. The caretaker was locking the doors on the floor below, and the sound of the bolts came up the stairwell.

"You are a very dangerous man," said Zofia.

"I am a miller's son," said Michał. "We are the most dangerous people in Poland, because we have nothing to lose but the grain."

* * *

He preached the sermon at Kielce on the ninth of November 1958, in the square in front of the cathedral, in front of a picture of the Mother of God on a platform of boards, and there were eleven thousand people, and the bishop of Kielce was in his chair, and the sun came out for the first time in a week.

The sermon is in the archive of the diocese and in the file of the Office, and it is nine minutes long, and the passage that mattered is this, and I have translated it from the transcript that was made by a man from the Ministry of Public Security who was standing at the corner of the square with a microphone inside a leather case:

> *"There is a room in this city where the state and the Church sit at a table, and I have been in that room. I am not going to tell you what was said in it. I am going to tell you what I saw, which is that the men at that table are afraid, and the fear is not a metaphor and not an insult. It is a fact. They are afraid of the Church, and they are right to be afraid, because the Church in this country has been for a thousand years the one institution that a man could belong to without permission. And we are afraid of them, and we are right to be afraid, because they have the militia and the prisons and the schools. Two frightened institutions at a table, in a room with the door shut, for ten years. And I am going to say in this square, in front of this picture, that the door should be opened, and that the state should come out and see what a Church looks like when it is not afraid, and the Church should come out and see what a state looks like when it is not afraid, and that neither of them will be able to do it alone. They will have to do it together, in the same room, with the door open, and the people of this country watching. That is not a surrender. It is not a victory. It is the only thing that has ever worked in this country, which is a settlement. We are a nation of settlements. We have never won a war and we have never lost a peace."*

The crowd laughed. The bishop of Kielce, who was sixty-four and had been ill for two years and had not laughed in public since 1955, was seen to put his hand over his mouth.

The man from the Ministry of Public Security wrote in his notebook: *Laughter in the square. The bishop laughed. The sermon was not hostile. Recommend no action. Note: the priest should be watched.*

At the back of the square, in a grey coat, with the collar turned up, stood Colonel Wesoły, and beside him, in a better grey coat, with her hat in her hand, stood the deputy director of the Catholic section of the Office for Religious Denominations, and she listened to the whole nine minutes without moving, and when it was over she said, without turning her head:

"Well, Colonel. What are you going to write?"

"I am going to write what I heard," said Wesoły. "And what I saw. And I am going to write the name of the woman who signed the permit, because the paper in the file will have her name on it, and in twenty years, when they open the file, they will see that the woman who signed the permit stood at the back of the square and listened to a sermon about opening the door." He lit a cigarette. "And I will write one more thing, pani director, and then I will not trouble you again tonight. I will write that the sermon was not addressed to the crowd. It was addressed to me."

"I know," said Zofia.

"How do you know?"

"Because I have known him since he was six years old," said Zofia, "and he has never once in his life been able to resist an audience of one."

* * *

The consequence came in December, and it came not from the Ministry of Public Security but from the Primate.

The sermon had been reported in Rome by the bishop of Kielce, in a letter, and the letter was read by a man in the Secretariat of State who had known the Primate since 1946, and the man wrote to Warsaw. The letter said that the candidate for the episcopate whose name was on the list of December 1957 was, in the judgment of the Secretariat, a man of great promise and of uncertain judgment, and that the Secretariat would be grateful for the Primate's own estimate.

The Primate sent for Michał on the fifteenth of December and received him in the study with the lamp.

"You have made a speech in a square," said the Primate.

"Yes, Excellency."

"You have told eleven thousand people that the state and the Church are two frightened institutions at a table." The Primate took off his spectacles. "It is the best sermon I have read in ten years, and I have read it four times, and the fourth time I read it I was afraid, and I am not a man who is often afraid." He put the spectacles down. "Rome has asked me for my estimate. I am going to give it, and I am going to give it in writing, and you are going to hear it now, because you are going to have to live with it. I am going to tell Rome that you are the best preacher in Poland and the most dangerous priest in the room, and that both facts are the same fact, and that I am going to make you a bishop because of it and not in spite of it."

Michał said nothing.

"But not in Rome," said the Primate. "You will not go to Rome. A bishop who has made a speech in a square is a bishop who must be seen to be made quietly. You will be consecrated here, in Warsaw, in the cathedral, in January or February, with no foreign bishops, no journalists, and no photograph, and the Office will be invited to send an observer, and the observer will be the woman from the Office, and she will come in a grey coat and she will sit in the second row." The Primate looked up. "Do you understand what I am doing?"

"You are making it look like a concession."

"I am making it look like a concession," said the Primate, "and I am making it a fact. And there is one more thing, Father, and this is the thing you will not like." He took a folder from the drawer. "There is a place in this country where the settlement of which you spoke in Kielce is possible. It is not in Warsaw and it is not in Rome. It is in the diocese of Płock, in the person of a man who has been the bishop there since 1946 and who is the only bishop in Poland who has the confidence of both the Office and the Primate, and who is dying. And there is a parish in that diocese where the state has taken the school, and the parish has no church, and the village has been waiting for eleven years, and the man who can settle it is a priest who can be in a room with the colonel and the minister and the woman from the Office and come out with a document that both sides can sign."

"Świerczyna," said Michał.

"Świerczyna," said the Primate. "The church that burned in 1945 and has stood roofless ever since, and the parish that has been refused a permit four times. The Office has refused it because the village is the village of the priest who is going to be a bishop, and a church in that village would be a monument to you. And you are going to ask for it anyway, and you are going to be refused, and then you are going to ask again with a document that gives the state something it wants, and the something is not a bribe, it is a policy. It is the policy you preached in Kielce: the state allows the church, and the Church allows the school, and the two of them sign a paper in a room with the door open, and the paper is the first of its kind in Poland." The Primate closed the folder. "And you will have to decide what you are willing to put in the paper. And I cannot help you. And the woman from the Office can."

Michał stood by the window with his hands behind his back.

"Excellency," he said. "There is a thing I have to tell you, and I am going to tell it to you now because after the consecration I will not be able to tell it to anyone."

"Tell me."

"I have known the woman from the Office since 1926. We were children in the same village. We were —" He stopped. "There was a girl. And there was a mill. And there was a war. And when I came back, she had gone to the other side of the room, and I had gone to this one, and we have been in the room together since January, and we have built a hall and a stove and a permit for a Mass, and we have done it as two officials, with protocols and minutes and a file that says we are *contact material.*"

The Primate was very quiet.

"And is that all it is?" he said.

"No," said Michał.

"Then you cannot be a bishop," said the Primate.

"I know."

"Do you?" The Primate stood up. He was fifty-seven years old, and he had been in prison three times, and he had been in a monastery for three years, and he stood in front of the man who was going to be his bishop and he was angry, and the anger was the anger of a man who has spent his life in the same room with the same temptation. "A bishop is not a man without a heart. A bishop is a man whose heart is not his own. If you love her, you will be in every room with her, and the men in the room will see it, and they will use it, and they will use it against the Church and against her, and they will be right to use it, because it will be true. The Church has survived for two thousand years on a single rule, and the rule is not that her priests do not love. The rule is that a priest does not put a woman in the place where God should be. And the place where God should be is the place where a man makes his decisions." He put his hand on the desk. "If you cannot put her down, tell me now, and I will send you to a parish in the mountains and you will be a good priest and I will have lost a bishop, and I will not hold it against you."

Michał looked at him.

"I have put her down every day since 1946," he said. "I have put her down in the camp and in the seminary and in the chancery and in the church of St. Anne. I have put her down in a kitchen in July, with her hand in mine, and she took her hand back and told me that the covenant was the door shut." He looked up. "I am not going to put her down. I am going to hold her at the distance of this room, which is the distance of a table, and I am going to do the work, and if the work fails I will go to the mountains and be a good priest, and if the work succeeds I will be a bishop who has a woman in Warsaw whom he does not see, and the men in the room will know it, and they will use it, and I will not be able to stop them, and I will still be a bishop, because a bishop is a man who does the work in the room where he is put."

The Primate looked at him for a long time.

"Then kneel down," he said.

"Excellency —"

"Kneel down," said the Primate, "and I will give you my blessing, and then you will go home and sleep, because tomorrow you are going to be in a room with a colonel and a minister and the woman from the Office, and you are going to ask them for a church in a village where the both of you were children, and you will need to be a man who has slept."

* * *

The paper was signed on the nineteenth of December 1958, in the Office for Religious Denominations, in a room with a window that did not open, by three men and a woman, and it was five pages long, and it was the first document of its kind in Poland.

It is called, in the file, the *Porozumienie w sprawie parafii Świerczyna* — the Agreement concerning the parish of Świerczyna — and it says, in the language of the Office, that the state recognizes the need of the Catholic population of the parish for a place of worship; that the parish will be granted the permit for the reconstruction of the church on the historic foundations, under the supervision of the conservator; that the parish will cede to the state, for the use of the school, the building of the old parish granary, which the parish had used as a catechetical room; that the religious instruction of the children of the parish will be given in the new church and in the presbytery, and not in the school; that the parish will not organize processions on the public roads without the permission of the presidium of the district council; and that the state and the Church will meet, in the person of the signatories, once a quarter, to consider the matters arising.

There is a sixth clause, and it is in the hand of the Minister, and it was added at the end of the meeting, after the typist had gone home, and it says:

*6. Strony oświadczają, że celem niniejszego porozumienia jest stworzenie warunków, w których życie religijne obywateli i zadania państwa mogą być realizowane bez konfliktu. Strony traktują niniejszy dokument jako próbę i zobowiązują się do wzajemnej informacji o trudnościach.*

*6. The parties declare that the purpose of this agreement is the creation of conditions in which the religious life of the citizens and the tasks of the state may be realized without conflict. The parties regard this document as an experiment and undertake to inform one another of difficulties.*

"An experiment," said Sztachelski, when he read it. "It is a beautiful word. It means we can stop at any time." He looked at Zofia across the desk. "Whose idea was clause six?"

"The priest's," said Zofia.

"Of course it was." He signed it with his fountain pen. "Tell me, pani Zofio. When you were in the village in July. The funeral. Did the two of you stand in the same church?"

"There is no church in the village, Minister. That is the point of the document."

"Did you stand in the same field?"

"Yes."

"And what did you feel?"

Zofia looked at him.

"I felt," she said, "that I was in the presence of a man who was going to cost the state a great deal of money, and that the state should be grateful to him."

Sztachelski laughed. He laughed for a long time, and when he had finished he capped his pen and looked at her with something in his face that she had not seen there before, and that she would think about for the rest of her life.

"You are a better liar than I am," he said. "And I have been lying to ministers since 1945."

He signed the document, and pushed it across the desk, and she signed it under him, and then the colonel signed it, and then the priest signed it, in his black cassock, with the number on his forearm under the buttoned cuff, and when it was done the Minister stood up and shook hands with the priest and said, "Father, you have just made the most dangerous agreement in Poland, and I hope you live long enough to regret it," and the priest said, "Minister, I have been in a place where the agreements were made without signatures, and I prefer this," and the Minister laughed again, and the meeting ended at seven in the evening.

And in the corridor, under the portraits of the men who had come and gone, with the guard at the stairs and the caretaker with the keys, the priest and the woman from the Office stood for a moment, and he said:

"The paper is signed."

"The paper is signed."

"There is a thing in the paper that is not in the paper," he said. "It is in clause six. The experiment. It means that if it fails, it fails in a document, and the document can be read by the men who come after us. There will be a man who comes after us, Zosia. He will be in a room with a window that does not open, and he will have a file, and the file will have this paper in it, and he will read clause six and he will know that there was a year when two people tried."

Zofia looked at him.

"That is not a covenant," she said. "That is a file."

"It is the only covenant we are allowed," said Michał, and put on his hat, and went down the stairs.

# Chapter Eight: The Commission

*Warsaw, September–December 1958*

The Pope died on the ninth of October, and the city did the thing it always did when a Pope died, which was to pretend that it had not noticed, and then to notice everything.

The Party's newspapers printed four lines on the second page, in the place where news of foreign religious events was printed, and the four lines said that Pius XII had died at Castel Gandolfo after a long illness, and that the Cardinal Camerlengo had assumed the government of the Church. The four lines were written by a man in the press agency who was a member of the Party and had a mother in the countryside who had a picture of Pius XII in her bedroom, and he had wept when he typed them, and he told me so in 1969, in a café on Marszałkowska, in the middle of a long conversation about the things men do for the Party and the things their mothers do in the country.

In the churches it was a Requiem. In the churches it was nine days of Masses and the *Dies Irae* and the bells rung at noon in every parish in Poland, and the state, which had learned in 1956 that the bells could not be stopped, let them ring, and the sound went across the city at noon for nine days, and the men in the Office stood at their windows and listened to it and said nothing to each other.

On the twenty-fifth of October the cardinals went into the conclave. On the twenty-eighth, in the evening, the smoke was white, and the Patriarch of Venice came out on the balcony as John XXIII, and the Poles in the churches of Warsaw heard the news in the sacristies and the presbyteries and the kitchens before it was in the newspapers, and the news went through the city in a night.

The new Pope was seventy-six years old. He had been a bishop in Bulgaria and Turkey and France and had spent the war saving Jews in Istanbul with a certificate that said he was a tradesman. He was fat and old and had a peasant's face and a laugh you could hear in Saint Peter's Square, and the men who had elected him had elected him because they thought he would be a caretaker.

He was not a caretaker. On the seventeenth of November, before he had been in the Chair of Peter for three weeks, he announced a consistory for the fifteenth of December: twenty-three new cardinals. The list went round the Office on the eighteenth, and there was no Pole on it, and a clerk in the corridor said that it was a good thing for Poland that the new Pope had shown his hand so early, and another clerk said that the Primate should have been on it, and the first clerk said that the Primate had been a cardinal since 1953, and the second clerk said, "Then why does the file say *nominacja — sprawa otwarta*?", and the third one, who was Zofia, said nothing, because she had learned at her father's table, in 1938, that a state can be defeated by a fact it has decided not to record.

* * *

The Commission of Three met for the first time on the nineteenth of September 1958, in a room in the building of the Council of Ministers, and it met because the Minister had decided that the Świerczyna agreement should not be the only one.

I know about the Commission because its secretary was a young man of thirty-one named Wacław Dąbek, who was a legal adviser of the Office and who kept two files: the file of the Commission, which went to the Minister, and a second file, which he kept at home in a drawer and brought to me in 1969 in a paper bag, because he had decided, at the age of forty-two, that the country needed to know. He had been in the Commission for four months. He had taken notes. He is the single most important source of this book, and he is dead now, and his widow gave me the paper bag again in 1970 to be sure that I had it, and I have it.

The Commission was the Minister's own idea, and it was designed to do the thing the Office could not do in the ordinary way: to find priests and parish councils and local officials in a dozen places in Poland who were willing to make, with the state, the kind of agreement that the rector of St. Anne's had made over the parish of Świerczyna — the church in exchange for the school, the permit in exchange for the quarterly meeting, the cross on the wall in exchange for the classification of the school as secular and the catechism out of the classroom and in the church.

The Minister did not call it a policy of settlement. He called it *the working out of the practical questions*, and he was careful, in the Commission, to say at the first meeting, in the minutes that Dąbek took: "We are not negotiating with the Church. There is nothing to negotiate. The Church is not a partner. But the parish is a fact, and the state has to administer the facts, and a fact can be administered well or badly."

"And the document?" said the man from the Ministry of Public Security.

"The document is a technical instrument," said the Minister. "It is not a concordat. It is a protocol. Protocols are the invention of the office, and offices are the invention of the state, and the state has never lost anything by a protocol."

* * *

They chose twelve parishes. They were chosen by the Office on the recommendation of the provincial departments, and they were chosen because in each of them there was a priest whom the local department had described, in the language of the Office, as *trudny, ale rozmowny*: difficult, but talkative. A man who will not talk is a wall and a wall is administratively simple. A man who talks is a door, and a door is a thing that can be opened in the direction you want, if you are patient, and if you have the key.

The Commission met every second Thursday, and in October Dąbek took notes at five meetings, and in November at three, and by December there were four parishes where a document had been signed.

They were small documents, and they were all different. In one parish the state allowed the repair of the roof of a chapel in exchange for the parish giving the state a room in the presbytery for the kindergarten. In one parish the state allowed the building of a new cemetery in exchange for the priest agreeing to read a pastoral letter from the bishop that the state had asked the bishop to write about the duty of the citizens to the harvest. In one parish in the west the state allowed the return of a confiscated organ, and the organ had been in a warehouse in Poznań since 1950, and the priest wept when he heard it, and the state's official in the room — a woman of forty from the department, who had been a teacher before the war — wept too, and there is no record of her name anywhere.

And in each of the twelve parishes there was a man who came to the Commission with the truth about what was happening, and the truth was that in four of the parishes the parish council would not sit with the state, and the priest signed the document alone, and the bishop heard about it afterward, and in one of the four the bishop forbade the priest to sign and the priest signed anyway and was suspended for a year.

* * *

The worst of the twelve was a parish in the diocese of Lublin whose priest was Father Antoni Rzepa.

The vicar of Wąwolnica had been transferred in May, and had refused to go, and had been suspended by the bishop, and had come to Warsaw to appeal to the Office, and the Office had given him a parish: a parish of eight hundred souls in a village in the Lublin uplands, in a church that had been built in 1931 and had been shelled in 1944 and repaired with the permission of the Office in 1956, with a presbytery, a barn, and a vicar who was the son of a party activist and who reported to the provincial department once a month.

Rzepa was thirty-four years old. He had been ordained in 1951, and he had been a good curate and a good preacher, and in 1955 he had begun to say in the sacristy that the Church was losing the country because it was fighting the wrong war. He said that the state was not going to fall, and that the Church had to live in it, and that the bishops were living in 1939. He said the Novena was the work of a man who had been in prison for three years and had come out believing that he was Moses. He said that the picture was a piece of painted wood and that the people's faith was in their hearts and not in a van.

He said these things in the sacristy of his parish to the other priests and to two laymen and to the woman who cleaned the church, and one of the laymen told the police, and the police told the bishop, and the bishop transferred him, and the state gave him a parish, and the state now wanted him to be one of the twelve.

Dąbek wrote the minutes of the meeting of the second of October, and the minutes record the voice of the man who sat across the table from the Minister, and the voice is the voice of Father Antoni Rzepa, and I am going to put the passage in, because it is the sound of a man who has chosen the wrong side and knows it and cannot go back:

> *"Ksiądz Rzepa: Chcę powiedzieć jasno, dlaczego tu jestem. Nie jestem tu dlatego, że wierzę w państwo. Nie wierzę w państwo. Państwo zabrało mi parafię, biskup mnie zawiesił, i jestem tu, bo ksiądz, który nie ma parafii, nie jest księdzem, a ja nie umiem być niczym innym. Więc powiem, co mam do powiedzenia, i będę to mówił do końca życia, choćbym miał je stracić: kościół w Polsce musi przestać być fortecą. Forteca upadnie, bo fortecy zawsze upadają, a jak upadnie, to pod gruzami będą dzieci."*

> *"Father Rzepa: I want to say clearly why I am here. I am not here because I believe in the state. I do not believe in the state. The state took my parish, the bishop suspended me, and I am here because a priest without a parish is not a priest, and I cannot be anything else. So I will say what I have to say, and I will go on saying it for the rest of my life, even if I lose it: the Church in Poland must stop being a fortress. A fortress falls, because fortresses always fall, and when it falls, the children will be under the rubble."*

The Minister, according to the minutes, was silent for a moment. Then he said: "Father, I am going to tell you the truth, because you have told me the truth and because I have learned in this office that the men who tell the truth are the only ones worth the trouble. The state does not want the Church to be a fortress. A fortress can be besieged. The state wants the Church to be a house. A house can be entered."

"And I want the house," said Rzepa.

"I know," said the Minister. "That is why I am going to sign your paper, and why I am going to give you the roof, and why, in three years, when the bishop in Lublin has died and the new bishop is a man we can talk to, I am going to send your name to Rome as a candidate for the episcopate, and I am going to do it with a clear conscience, because you are right about the fortress."

There was a silence in the minutes.

"And I am going to do one more thing," said the Minister, "and I am going to do it because you are the only man in the room who has been honest with me, and because I am a doctor and I have spent my life telling men the diagnosis they did not want. In three years you will be a bishop, and the priests in Lublin will not speak to you, and the people in your parishes will love you and not obey you, and the men in this room will own you, and you will know it, and you will not be able to say a word about it, because the moment you say it the roof falls in on the children. That is the price of the roof, Father. I am telling you what it is. You can still leave the room."

Rzepa did not leave the room.

He signed the document on the sixth of November, and the roof was repaired in the spring, and in the file there is a photograph of the priest standing in front of the church with the scaffolding, and behind him the village, and in the front row of the village a boy of nine with a cap, and the boy is looking at the camera as if it were a gun.

* * *

Zofia's illness began in the first week of October, and it began with a pain in the side, and she ignored it for three weeks because she had a Commission and a Minister and a file with two hundred and fourteen pages.

She had had the pain before. She had had it in the forest in 1944, when it had been the cold and the hunger and a week of lying in a ditch, and she had had it in 1949, when it had been an inflammation of the kidney and she had been in hospital for eleven days, and she had had it in 1955, when she had not gone to the doctor and it had gone away.

This time it did not go away.

She worked through it. She went to the Office at half past six and stayed until eight and came home and lay on the bed with a hot water bottle and a book, and in the morning the pain was there, in the right side, low down, a dull grinding like a millstone, and she took a powder and put on her coat.

On the twenty-eighth of October, the day of the white smoke, she was in the Office until eleven at night, and at eleven she stood up from the desk and the pain went through her like a wire, and she sat down on the floor of the office with her back against the desk and her hand pressed to her side and stayed there for a quarter of an hour.

Then she got up and put on her hat and went home and did not tell anyone.

On the second of November she collapsed in the corridor outside the Minister's office, and Tosia found her, and the Minister's assistant called an ambulance from the Ministry of Health, and she was taken to the hospital on Lindleya Street and examined by a surgeon who had been a student of the Minister's in Wilno in 1938, and the surgeon said: the gallbladder, and there is a stone, and it has been there for a year, and it must come out.

She refused the operation.

She refused it for four weeks, and she worked through the four weeks, and the reason she refused it is in a memorandum she wrote to herself on the eighth of November, a memorandum that was never sent and that was found in her desk in 1968, in the folder of the non-current materials, folded in half, with the corner of the page turned down:

> *If I go into the hospital in November, the Commission continues without me, and Klimek takes the chair, and Klimek will sign anything the Ministry of Public Security puts in front of him, and Rzepa will be a bishop in three years, and there will be twelve documents in the file of the Office, and every one of them will be a stone that a man can throw at the Church in 1966. The work is a chain. Each link is a man in a room. I am one of the links. A link does not go into the hospital in November.*

The surgeon at Lindleya Street had a different opinion. He telephoned the Minister on the fifteenth of November, and the Minister listened, and said, "Doctor, she is the best officer I have, and she will not go into the hospital because she believes that the work will fall without her, and I am going to tell you what I am going to do, which is nothing, because if I order her into the hospital she will resign and work from her flat and be worse, and if she resigns I will have Klimek as my Catholic section, and Klimek is a drunk."

"So what do I do?"

"You keep the bed," said the Minister, "and you wait, and you watch her, and when the pain is bad enough, she will come, because she is not a fool, and the pain is the only argument that a woman like that can hear."

The pain became bad enough on the twenty-fourth of November.

She was in the Office, at the desk, with the fourth draft of the Rzepa protocol in front of her — the Commission was to meet on Thursday and the Minister wanted the text by Wednesday — and the pain came, and did not stop, and at four in the afternoon Tosia came into the office with the tea and found her with her forehead on the desk and her hands flat on the wood.

"Right," said Tosia, "that is enough," and she took Zofia's coat off the hook and put it on her shoulders and took her by the arm and walked her down the stairs and out of the building and into a taxi, and the taxi driver took one look at the woman in the grey coat and drove to Lindleya Street without being told.

They operated at nine in the evening, on the twenty-fourth of November 1958: a cholecystectomy, four hours, and the surgeon came out at one in the morning and told Tosia that the gallbladder had been full of stones and one of the stones had been in the duct, and that she had been three weeks from the peritonitis and the mortuary, and that the woman was the toughest patient he had seen since the war, and that she would live.

She was in the hospital for eleven days.

She was back at her desk on the eighth of December, with a scar in her side, a bottle of pills in her bag, and a file that had not stopped for eleven days.

* * *

He came on the twenty-sixth of November, at the end of visiting hours, in his black coat, with a small parcel wrapped in brown paper, and the sister at the desk told him that the patient was asleep and that he could sit by the bed for ten minutes.

The ward had eight beds, and six of them were occupied, and the woman in the bed by the window was grey and thin and had a tube in her arm, and she was awake, and she looked at him for a while without moving her head.

"You came," she said.

"I came."

"How did you know?"

"Tosia Grabska telephoned the presbytery at six this morning. She said: *Father, the great lady is in the hospital and she will not send for you, so I am sending for you, and you will not tell her that I telephoned.*"

"The traitor," said Zofia.

"The best friend you have," said Michał, "and I am going to tell her so at Christmas."

He sat down on the chair by the bed, and put the parcel on the table, and took off his hat, and looked at her.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I have had an operation. There were stones. The surgeon says I am a tough patient. I have told him that a woman who has been in the forest in 1944 is not tough, she is simply too tired to die." She turned her head on the pillow. "The protocol. The fourth draft of the Rzepa text. Did Klimek take it to the Minister?"

"Klimek took it to the Minister on the twenty-fifth and the Minister read it and said it was written by a drunk, and sent it back, and Tosia has it in her drawer for you."

"The Rzepa paper was signed?"

"It was signed on the sixth of November," said Michał, "in a room in the Council of Ministers, and there was no photograph and no communiqué, and the roof of his church is being repaired this week by four men from his own village, and a photograph of it will be in the diocesan paper in the spring, and no one will know that a paper exists." He took her hand. "There is a thing that is going to happen in Rome on the fifteenth of December," said Michał, "and the Office will know it before the newspapers do, and it will be in the file, and the file will not be wrong. I am telling you because you are in a hospital bed and you are the only person in the Office who would not sell it to the Ministry of Public Security."

Zofia lay still for a long moment.

"Then it is done," she said. "They will make you a bishop."

"They will make me a bishop."

"And you will be consecrated in the cathedral in January, quietly, with no photograph, and I will be in the second row in a grey coat, and the colonel will be behind me, and the file will have a new page."

"The file will have a new page." He held her hand. "Zosia. There is a thing I have to say to you, and I am going to say it in a hospital ward with six other women in it and a nun at the door, because it is the only place in Warsaw where I can say it without it going into a file."

"Say it."

"The Primate has asked me to go to Świerczyna on the twenty-seventh of December. The church is to be rebuilt in the spring, and there is a thing that has to be done before the foundation is laid. In 1943, when the Germans began to requisition the bells of the district, the men of my parish took ours down in the night and sank it in the millrace below the wheel — the channel is deep there and the silt is thick — and they marked the place with a stone on the bank, and the Germans came in June and looked in an empty tower and beat the curate and went away. The bell has been under the water and the mud for fifteen years, and the whole parish knows where it is to a yard, and no one has been able to get it out. The bishop of Płock has given permission for it to be raised before the foundation, and the men of the parish are going to do it on the twenty-eighth, with a tripod and a winch and a raft, and they have asked me to be there, and the Primate has said that I may go." He looked at her. "I am asking you to come with me."

Zofia looked at him.

"You are asking me to come to Świerczyna in December," she said. "In a car. With you. In the snow. On the day after Christmas."

"I am asking you to come to Świerczyna in December in a car with me in the snow on the day after Christmas," said Michał, "and I am asking you as a man who has been in the room with you for eleven months and has not once been in a room with you, and who is going to be a bishop in January, and who will never again in his life be able to ask a woman to come anywhere."

"The file," said Zofia.

"The file will say that the director of the Catholic section of the Office went to Świerczyna on the twenty-seventh of December to inspect the site of the reconstruction of the parish church, in accordance with the agreement of the nineteenth of December, and was accompanied by the priest who is the signatory for the parish, because the supervision of the works is a matter for the two signatories, and the journey was made by car on a public road with a driver from the Office, and the report of the journey is in the file." He took his hand away and put it on his knee. "It is true. Every word of it is true. That is the whole of what we are, Zosia. Two people who cannot do anything that is not true."

Zofia turned her head and looked out of the window at the snow coming down on the roofs of Lindleya Street.

"There is a thing I have not told you," she said. "Róża told me in July, in the room, with her hand on my wrist. She said: take him back to the river, in the winter, when the ice is on it. Take him back to the bend and let him be a boy for an hour, and then let him be a bishop." She looked at him. "I told her that the ice had gone out in 1939 and never come back, and I was right, and I am going to come anyway, and I am going to be in the car, and when we get to the village I am going to stand at the back and watch the men bring the bell out of the river, and I am not going to lie down on any ice, and I am going to go back to Warsaw on the evening of the twenty-eighth in the car, and the report of the journey will be in the file in my own writing." She reached for his hand. "But I will be in the car, Michał. I will be in the car."

He sat with her until the sister came and said that the patient must sleep, and then he put on his hat and went out into the snow, and at the door of the ward he turned and said:

"There is one more thing, and it is the thing the Office will get wrong. The consistory is on the fifteenth. The newspapers will say that the Primate is to be made a cardinal. He was made a cardinal on the twelfth of January 1953, in the consistory of that day, and he could not go to Rome for it, because the state would not give him a passport, and the red hat waited in Rome for four years, and in May 1957 he went, at last, and Pius XII put it on his head with his own hands. The file of this Office says *nominacja kardynalska — sprawa otwarta*. The file does not know that the hat came." He smiled. "In January the Minister will send for the file on the hat, and there will be a protocol, and the protocol will be for a hat that is already on a shelf on Miodowa Street, and nobody will tell him, because nobody in this Office has ever seen a cardinal."

"The file will have a page for it," said Zofia.

"The file will have a page for it," said Michał, "and the page will say: *ecclesiastical headgear, one item, red* — and in the margin, in pencil, some clerk will write *sprawdzić, czy już nie wydano*, check whether one has already been issued."

The sister closed the door.

* * *

The Commission met for the last time on the twenty-third of December, in the room in the Council of Ministers, and there were nine men and a woman at the table, and the Minister came in at the end and said eleven words, and the eleven words are in the minutes:

> *"Panowie, kończymy na tym roku. W styczniu będzie nowy rok. Nie wiemy, co przyniesie. Życzę państwu spokojnych świąt."*

> *"Gentlemen, we are finishing for this year. In January there will be a new year. We do not know what it will bring. I wish you a quiet Christmas."*

The nine men and the woman went out of the room, and the Minister stayed, and Dąbek, who was collecting the papers, saw him standing at the window with his back to the room, and the window did not open, and the Minister was looking at the street.

"Minister?"

"Nothing," said Sztachelski. "Go home, Dąbek. Kiss your wife. Do not read the file tonight."

Dąbek went home. He read the file. He read it for thirty years, in the end, and he gave it to me in 1969 in a paper bag, and on the top of the file he had written, in the hand of a man of forty-two who had decided that the country needed to know:

*Akta Komisji — sprawa dwunastu parafii. Nie zniszczyć. W razie mojej śmierci przekazać do Archiwum Państwowego. Nie wcześniej niż w 1989 roku.*

*Files of the Commission — the matter of the twelve parishes. Do not destroy. In the event of my death, to be transferred to the State Archive. Not earlier than 1989.*

He was right about the year. I have thought about that a great deal: that a legal adviser of the Office for Religious Denominations, in December 1958, in a room with a window that did not open, wrote on a file the year in which the country would be free, and got it exactly right, and then went home and had his supper.

# Chapter Nine: The Bell in the River

*Świerczyna, 27–28 December 1958*

They left Warsaw at six in the morning of the twenty-seventh, in a black Warszawa with a driver from the Office, and the snow began at Modlin and did not stop.

Zofia had signed herself back on duty for two days. The paper is in the file: *Wyjazd służbowy: 27–28.12.1958, Świerczyna, pow. Mława. Cel: nadzór nad rozpoczęciem prac przy odbudowie kościoła parafialnego, zgodnie z porozumieniem z 19.12.1958. Środek transportu: samochód służbowy. Kierowca: K. Malinowski.* Official journey, 27–28 December 1958, Świerczyna, powiat Mława. Purpose: supervision of the commencement of works on the reconstruction of the parish church, in accordance with the agreement of 19 December 1958. Means of transport: official car. Driver: K. Malinowski.

There is a second line, in her hand, added at the bottom: *Uwaga: drogi zawiane. Możliwe opóźnienie powrotu.* Note: roads drifted. Possible delay of return.

The driver was Krzysiek Malinowski, who had driven her for four years, and who had a wife in Wola and a boy of nine and a cough he had had since the war. In the front seat beside him was the bishop-elect of Płock, in a black coat and a black hat, with a small suitcase at his feet and his breviary on his knee, and in the back seat, with the report book of the Office on her lap, was the director of the Catholic section.

They drove north on the road to Płońsk with the chains on, and the snow came across the road in long white lines, and the fields on either side were without shape, and the villages went by with their roofs invisible and their windows yellow. At a crossroads a man in a sheepskin was selling something out of a sack, and Krzysiek slowed and rolled down the window and asked the price, and the man said a word that was lost in the wind.

At Płońsk they stopped for petrol and hot water, and Krzysiek went into the office with a chit, and Zofia and Michał stood by the pump in the falling snow with their collars up.

"You are to talk to me today as though I were an official of the Office and you were a bishop," she said.

"I am not a bishop until the twenty-fifth of January."

"You are a bishop in the file. In the file you have been a bishop since the nineteenth of December, when the Minister read your name and said: *this one is going to be trouble.*" She looked at the snow. "The file is the only thing in this country that is always early."

"All right," he said. "Pani director, the question of the drainage of the site of the former church was resolved by the conservator in November. The works will begin with the clearing of the rubble, which is expected to take three weeks, and the foundation stone will be laid in April. The parish has undertaken to provide the labour. The state has undertaken to provide the cement. There is no cement."

"There is never any cement."

"There is no cement," agreed Michał, "and there is a paper in the Office that says there is cement, and the paper is what we have, and I have learned in the room that a paper is a machine for making cement out of nothing, if the paper is signed by the right woman."

Krzysiek came back with the chit and the water, and they drove on.

* * *

They came to Mława at noon. The snow had eased and the sky over the town was the colour of a knife, and they stopped at the presbytery of the parish to leave the bishop's suitcase; he was to sleep there, and the parish priest had been told that the bishop-elect was coming, and had lit the stove in the good room in the morning and had put a bottle of vodka on the table under a napkin.

The parish priest was sixty-eight. He had been the curate at Świerczyna in 1945, and he had read a letter out loud in the kitchen of the mill, and he had told a young woman in a good grey coat that a man in a camp was not issuing orders, he was saying goodbye, and the young woman had told him that he had a great deal to learn about women. He was the only living man besides the two of them who remembered that afternoon.

He looked at Zofia on the steps of the presbytery and his face did something complicated.

"Pani," he said. "It has been thirteen years."

"It has been thirteen years, Father."

"You told me once that I had a great deal to learn about women."

"You did."

"I have learned," said the parish priest of Mława. "I have learned that the woman who told me that was right, and that I was a young fool with a bicycle and a bag of altar linen, and that the letter I read out loud in that kitchen was the best and the worst thing I have ever done." He held the door. "Come in out of the cold. There is soup."

They ate soup in the refectory with the parish priest and his vicar and an old woman who did the cooking, and they talked about the roads and the cement and the bishop of Płock, who was ill, and the twelfth of January, when the schools would reopen without the crucifixes in the classrooms. The vicar — a boy of twenty-six with a country face and a cold — said, "Father, Bishop, is it true that in Warsaw the churches are full?"

"In Warsaw the churches are full," said Michał, "and in Warsaw the offices are full, and the two facts are not as far apart as the papers say."

The parish priest put down his spoon.

"I read the papers," he said. "I read them every day, and every day I am angry, and then I read the diocesan paper, and it is worse, because it is written by us. And I have come to think, in my old age, that the papers are not the point. The point is this table. There are four people at this table, and each of us is a scandal to somebody, and the soup is hot, and the Church is in the room, and the state is in the room, and nobody has written it down." He wiped his mouth. "I am going to say a thing and then I am going to say the blessing and we will go out. In 1945 I told that woman on the steps that a man who is obeyed by the only person he wanted to be wanted by is destroyed. I have thought about it for thirteen years and I have decided that I was right, and that she has been destroyed, and that she has done a great deal of good while being destroyed, and that God in His mercy will put the good in the ledger and leave the destruction to the men who caused it."

Zofia looked at him for a long moment.

"Father," she said, "you have got better."

"I have got older," said the parish priest. "It is the same thing, if a man is lucky."

At two o'clock the car went on to Świerczyna with the bishop-elect and Zofia and Krzysiek, and the parish priest stood on the steps of the presbytery and watched them go, and afterward he told me, in 1969, that he had stood there a long time with his hands in his sleeves and had said a prayer he had not said since 1945.

* * *

The road from Mława to Świerczyna was nine miles and took an hour. The snow had drifted, and twice Krzysiek had to get out with the shovel, and the second time Michał got out with him and the two men dug the car out of a drift in the dark of the afternoon, and the bishop's black coat was white to the shoulder.

They came into the village at three, in the last of the light.

The village was under snow and the smoke went up straight from the chimneys, and in the lanes the men had been out with shovels since morning, and the road to the mill was clear, and the ruts were frozen and rang under the wheels. The birch arches were still up along the lane — the ones that had been put up in October for the picture that came by the highway and did not come through the village — and they had been repaired and re-tied since, and somebody had hung paper stars on them for Christmas, and they stood over the road in the snow like the bones of a summer.

The car stopped in the yard of the mill.

Ania Żurawska was in the doorway with a shawl over her head.

She was seventy-two, and she had buried a husband in 1953 and a daughter in 1929 and a daughter-in-law in July, and she had a son in Warsaw in a black coat, and she stood in the doorway of the mill house and looked at the car and the driver and the woman in the grey coat and her son, and she said, in the voice of a woman who had been a bell-ringer's daughter:

"Well. There is the bishop."

"Mama," said Michał.

"Don't you *mama* me. You are a bishop and I am a miller's widow and I have known it since 1926, when you came down out of that tower and told me that the bell went right through your chest." She came down the step. "And there is the great lady, in her grey coat, in a car. Come in, both of you. The stove is on and there is barley soup and the water is hot, and the great lady is going to take off that coat and sit by the fire and tell me what Warsaw has done to her face."

They went in.

The mill house had two rooms and a kitchen, and the kitchen had the stove and the table and the bench and the wall of photographs, and the photographs had grown since the summer. There was Róża in her wedding dress, and Wacek in his uniform, and Antek at his first communion, and a photograph that had been cut out of a newspaper in 1957 and framed in a frame of cardboard covered with blue paper, and the photograph was of the church of St. Anne in Warsaw with a young priest coming down the steps, and it had been cut out and framed by Ania Żurawska and hung between her husband and her daughter.

Zofia stood in front of it for a long time.

"Take off the coat," said Ania, behind her. "You are not in Warsaw. In this house there is no one who reports, and the driver is in the barn with a glass, and the door is shut, and if the door is shut for an hour in the house of a miller in December it is because the soup is hot and not because there is a conspiracy." She put a bowl on the table. "Sit down and eat, and then we will talk about the bell."

They ate.

The soup was barley and there was a piece of sausage in it and a piece of bread, and the lamp was on the table and the window was black and the stove ticked, and Ania Żurawska ate with them and asked Zofia about her side — "the surgeon was a butcher, I can see it in the way you sit" — and about Warsaw, and about the price of a pair of boots, and about the electric kettle, which had still not come; and she laughed at the kettle and said that in 1938 in this kitchen there had been a kettle that sang.

And then she said: "Now. The bell."

Michał put down his spoon.

"In 1943, in the April," said Ania Żurawska, "when they took the men in Mława, the Germans had begun to requisition the bells. They had taken the bells of Dąbrowa and Grzybowo and the two bells of the parish of Radzymin, and they put them on the train and sent them to Hamburg to be melted for the guns. The old sacristan, Maciej, he was seventy-nine, he had been the sacristan since 1891, he came to my husband in the March and said: they will come for ours. And my husband said: what do we do? And Maciej said: we sink it."

She folded her hands on the table.

"So they sank it. In the millrace, below the wheel, where the channel is deep and the silt is thick. They did it in the night, with the cart and the ropes and eleven men, and they put it down in eight feet of water and mud, and they marked the place with a stone on the bank, and the stone is still there, and my son knows it. And when the Germans came in the June for the bell, the priest — Father Dąbrowski, he was the curate then, he is sixty-eight now and has a wooden leg — he told them that the bell had been taken in 1939 by the Russians, and the Germans looked in the tower and there was no bell, and they beat him and went away." She looked at Michał. "And in the night before they sank it, I took a chisel and a hammer, and I went down to the cart where the bell was lying on its side under a horse blanket, and I cut a number into the bronze."

Michał did not move.

"I cut it into the waist of the bell, where it is thick," said Ania Żurawska, "and I did it in the dark with a chisel, and it took me an hour, and the men held the lantern, and I could not see what I was doing, and Maciej said, *Ania, the letter is crooked,* and I said, *it is a number, it does not have to be straight.* And the number is the number of my son, and it went into the river with the bell, and it has been in the water for fifteen years, and tomorrow the men are going to bring the bell out, and the number is going to come out with it, and I am going to be standing on the bank." She looked up. "That is the thing I wanted to say in this kitchen, and I have said it, and now I am going to bed, because I am seventy-two and tomorrow I am going to stand on a bank in the cold, and a woman of seventy-two must sleep before she stands on a bank."

She got up and went to the door of the next room, and stopped.

"Great lady," she said. "You have a letter in a tin."

Zofia looked at her.

"You had it in your bag in July," said Ania. "I saw the corner of the tin when you put your bag on the bench, and I said nothing, because I am a miller's widow and I know when a thing is being carried. Open it tonight. Not tomorrow. Tomorrow there is a bell and a village and a bishop, and there will be no room in the day for a letter." She went into her room and shut the door.

* * *

The fire in the kitchen stove was banked for the night and the lamp was turned down, and Michał and Zofia sat at the table with the chronicle between them and the tin on the table, and Zofia's hands were in her lap.

"Open it," he said.

"I have kept it for twelve years."

"Open it."

She opened the tin.

Inside were three things: the letter of 1946, in its envelope, with the Office's stamp on the corner and the word *zwrot* — return — in a clerk's hand; a photograph of the village from 1938, which her mother had put in the tin in 1946 and which Zofia had not looked at in twelve years; and a small cardboard box with a lock of hair in it, dark, tied with a thread, which was Róża's, and had been cut on the morning of the funeral by Wacek's mother, because that was what was done.

She took out the letter and opened it.

The paper was thin and had been folded for twelve years, and the fold had worn through in one place, and the handwriting was her own at twenty-seven: firm, upright, and without any of the flourishes that the Office had taught her later.

*Michał.*

*I have heard that you are alive. I am in Warsaw. I have work and a room and a ration card and a good coat. I have been told to tell you that I am not waiting for you, and I have done it, and it is in writing now, and you may keep the paper.*

*But if you come to Warsaw, come to the Office on Krucza Street and ask for Wierzbicka, and I will come down the stairs.*

*Z.*

She read it twice. Then she put it on the table and looked at it.

"Twelve years," she said.

"Twelve years."

"It took me four drafts. The first draft was eight pages. The second was four. The third was two, and it was full of the Party and the future and the necessity of the historical process, and I read it in the morning and I burned it in the stove of my room, and then I wrote this." She touched the paper. "Eight lines. I have written perhaps four thousand documents in my life. This is the only one I have ever written that a man could have used."

"He did not get it."

"He did not get it," said Zofia, "and I have spent twelve years with a letter in a tin, and I have told myself that the not-getting was the tragedy, and it is not. The tragedy is that I wrote it, and that I had understood everything by 1946, and then I took the Party card and the room and the good coat and I put the letter in a tin, and I did the arithmetic. I sat in a room in Warsaw with the letter in my hand and the future in front of me, and I decided that the future was worth more than the letter, and the decision was correct, Michał. That is the thing I have never been able to say to anyone. The decision was correct. If I had come down the stairs in 1946, I would have been the wife of a priest in a parish, and there would have been no hall at St. Anne's, and no agreement, and no twelve parishes, and no school, and no church at Świerczyna, and the children would be in the sacristy and the street, and I would have been happy." She looked at him. "And I would have been no use to anyone."

Michał took her hand.

"Zosia. Tomorrow, at nine in the morning, the men of this parish are going to bring a bell out of a river. It has a number on it that my mother cut with a chisel in the dark in 1943, and the number is mine. And I am going to stand on the bank in a black coat, and there will be eleven men and a tripod and a net, and the whole village will be on the bank, and my mother will be there, and you will be there, in your grey coat, with the report book of the Office in your bag, and the village will see the two of us standing on the same bank." He held her hand. "And I have decided what I am going to do after that, and I am going to tell you now, and you are not going to argue with me, because I have been in the room for two years and I know the arithmetic better than you do."

"Tell me."

"On the twenty-fifth of January I am consecrated bishop. In February the bishop of Płock will die or retire — he has had two strokes and he wants to go — and I will be the ordinary of the diocese, and the Office will have to deal with me for twenty years. And the first thing the Office will do, in March, is open a file on the bishop of Płock and the woman from the Office, because Colonel Wesoły is not a fool and he has been building a file since the spring, and it will have a report of a journey to Świerczyna on the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth of December, and it will say what it says." He looked at her. "And I am going to beat him to it. On the twenty-seventh of January, two days after the consecration, I am going to write to the Minister and tell him that the bishop of Płock requests that the director of the Catholic section of his Office be transferred from Warsaw to the provincial department in Płock, as the officer responsible for the implementation of the agreement of the nineteenth of December."

Zofia looked at him.

"That is a joke," she said.

"It is not a joke. It is the only move on the board. If you stay in Warsaw, Wesoły makes the file and the Minister has to act and you are finished as an officer and I am finished as a bishop. If you go to Płock as the officer of the Office responsible for the agreement, you are the woman the bishop of Płock has to meet once a quarter in the presence of the starosta, and the file will say so, and the file will be true, and the file will be *boring*. A quarterly meeting in the presence of the starosta is the most boring thing in the world, and I have been in the room long enough to know that a boring file is the only kind of file a man can live in." He put his hands flat on the table. "You will be in Płock, and I will be in Płock, and we will meet four times a year, with the starosta and a secretary and a report, and we will not be alone in a room for twenty years, and we will see each other four times a year, in a room with a window, and the Church and the state will build a school and a church and a hospital together, and the children of the villages will learn the multiplication tables and the catechism, and at the end of twenty years I will be an old bishop and you will be an old woman, and we will have done the work, and it will have been worth it. That is the offer. It is the only offer I have. It is not a life, Zosia. It is a room."

Zofia stood up and went to the window and looked out at the yard and the mill and the snow.

"Płock is a small town," she said. "The Office has a provincial department in Płock with four officers and a typist. The officer responsible for the implementation of the agreement would be a woman of thirty-nine with a scar in her side and a reputation in Warsaw as the best Catholic officer in the service, and she would be sent to a provincial town because a bishop asked for her, and the whole service would know it. I would walk into that department on the first of March and there would be a typist and four officers and they would all know why I was there, and the file would say *boring*, and the file would be a lie, because it would not be boring at all." She turned around. "It would be the most interesting thing in Poland. Four times a year. In the presence of the starosta. For twenty years."

"Yes."

"And in the other twenty years? When you are retired and I am retired and the Office has forgotten us and there is nothing in the file and no starosta and no secretary and no quarterly meeting?"

Michał came and stood beside her at the window.

"Then we will be two old people in a small town," he said, "and I will say the Mass on Sunday, and you will have the garden, and in the winter we will go to the bend and listen to the ice, and the children of the village will come to the door with their exercise books, and there will be no starosta in the room, and I am not going to pretend that I know what that will be like, because I have been a priest for six years and a bishop for six days and I have never in my life been happy for longer than a night."

"And you think I would be happy in Płock?"

"I think you would be in the room," said Michał. "I think you have been in the room since 1944 and you will be in the room until you die, and the only question is which room, and whether there is anyone in it who knows your name."

She stood at the window with her hand on the frame for a long time.

"I am going to say a thing to you," she said at last. "And it is not a thing I have said to anyone, and it is in no memorandum, and you will not put it in a file."

"Say it."

"I do not believe in God. I have not believed since the cellar in 1944. And I am not going to believe. The world is a machine and it has no mercy, and the men who say it has are selling something, and you have been selling it for six years, and you are going to sell it for twenty more, and I have watched you do it and I have loved every minute of it and I have not believed one word." She turned. "And I am going to Płock."

He took her in his arms.

They stood in the kitchen of the mill, in the dark, with the fire banked and the lamp down and the snow on the window, and he held her the way a man holds a woman when he has been alone for seventeen years, and she held him the way a woman holds a man when she has decided, in one movement of the mind, to give up the only thing she has.

They did not kiss. That is the thing I have wondered about for three years, and I asked him in 1970, and he said: "No. We stood in the kitchen for a long time. And then she said, *there is the letter*, and I said, *leave it on the table*, and she left it on the table, and we sat down on the bench with our backs against the warm stove, and we fell asleep. And at half past four in the morning my mother came out of her room and found us, and she put a blanket over the two of us and went back to bed, and she never said a word about it to either of us, ever."

* * *

The men began at eight.

It was still dark, and there were eleven of them and a boy of fourteen, and they came up the lane from the village with the tripod and the winch and the ropes and a net of woven hazel and a boat that had been made in 1939 and had been in a barn since the war. The bank was cleared of snow and the ice was broken with an axe in a place the size of a kitchen table, and the water under the ice was black and slow, and the millrace ran under the ice the way it had always run, and the place was marked with a stone on the bank, and the stone had been there since 1943 and the men knew it.

The whole village came. They came at nine and they stood in the lane and on the mill bridge and on the bank behind the rope, and the children were lifted onto shoulders, and there were three hundred people, and Zofia stood at the back on the bank with the report book of the Office in her bag and did not take it out.

The bishop-elect stood at the edge of the ice in his black coat, with the men, and his mother stood beside him in a black coat and a shawl, and she was seventy-two and had said at breakfast that she was not going to cry and that if anyone saw her cry it was the wind.

It took four hours.

They put down the boat and the tripod over the hole, and the diver was a man from the village named Stefan Wilk, who had been a frogman in the navy in 1948 and had left the navy because of his uncle, and he went into the water in two sweaters and a rope round his waist, and came up three times blue and shaking, and the third time he came up and held up his hand and said, "I have it. I put the rope under the crown. It is lying on its side in the silt, and there is a wheel of a cart on top of it, and an iron hoop, and about a thousand bottles."

They hauled.

The winch was a thing of 1938 from the mill, and the rope was new, and the men turned it in shifts, and the ice creaked, and the whole village was silent the way a village is silent at a harvest when the load is too heavy, and the bishop-elect took his turn at the winch with the men, in his black coat, and his mother watched him with her hands folded.

At a quarter past twelve the bell came out of the water.

It came up through the hole in the ice with the water pouring off it, black and green and streaming, and the mud on it was the mud of fifteen years, and the men swung the tripod and laid it down on the boards on the bank, and the water ran out of it into the snow, and the bell was cracked, and it was beautiful, and it was three hundred years old, and it was the bell of the parish of Świerczyna.

And on the waist of it, in the place where it is thick, in figures a hand and a half high, cut with a chisel in the dark in 1943 by a woman of fifty-seven, there was a number.

*119278.*

The village saw it.

There was a sound from three hundred people at once that was not a cheer and not a cry, and in the language of the Office it was nothing to report: a low sound, and it went across the bank and the mill bridge and the lane and up into the bare birch arches with the paper stars on them, and then the village was silent again.

Ania Żurawska walked out onto the boards and knelt in the snow beside the bell and put her hand on the number.

She stayed there for a moment. Then she got up, with her hand on the shoulder of her son, and she turned to the village and said, in the voice of a woman who had been a bell-ringer's daughter and who at twelve had learned to pull the rope of that bell:

"*On jest nasz. Dzwonić.*"

It is ours. Ring it.

They hung it that afternoon on a beam between two ladders in the field beyond the mill, because the church was not built, and the rope was a rope from the well, and the man who rang it was the old sacristan's grandson, who was nineteen and had never in his life heard it. When he pulled the rope the bell swung, and the crack in it made a sound that was not a pure bell but a bell with a voice, a cracked voice, the voice of a thing that had been under the water for fifteen years, and it rang out across the snow and the village and the river and the fields to the bend and beyond the bend, and it rang twelve times, and then the village stood in the field with their hats off and the children quiet.

And the bishop-elect of Płock stood in the field with his mother on one side and, on the other side, a woman in a grey coat from the Office, and the village saw the two of them standing there, and the village decided, without a meeting, that it had seen what it had seen, and that it would not be written down, and that if it was ever written down it would be written down in the parish.

* * *

They left at four, in the dark, and the snow had begun again.

Krzysiek had the engine running, and the bishop's suitcase was in the boot, and Zofia stood in the yard of the mill with her bag and her report book and her hat in her hand, and the light from the kitchen window lay on the snow.

Ania Żurawska came out with a parcel.

"Bread and salt," she said. "For the road. And a piece of the cake from Christmas, and a jar of the jam. The bread is from the flour of the new mill and it is not as good as the old, and you will eat it in the car and you will not give it to the driver, because the driver is a man and he has a wife and she can bake him his own bread." She put the parcel in Zofia's hands. "Great lady. You are going to Płock."

"I am going to Płock."

"Then I will see you four times a year, at the quarterly meeting, and I will not be at the quarterly meeting, because I am a miller's widow and I do not go to the meetings, and I will see you in the church with the starosta, and I will look at the two of you and I will know." She took Zofia's face in her two hands, and her hands were hard and warm and smelled of bread. "You were a girl who could not put a letter in a stove. I have known it since 1938. It is the only thing in the world that matters, and it is the thing that the state cannot take out of a woman with a chisel." She kissed her on both cheeks. "Go on. The road is bad."

They drove out of the yard and down the lane under the birch arches, and at the turn by the co-operative Zofia turned in her seat and looked back, and the light of the kitchen window was still there, and in it, small and black against the snow, were the two of them: the old woman and the bishop, standing in the yard of the mill.

* * *

The car stopped at the bend.

He asked Krzysiek to stop, and Krzysiek stopped, and the bishop-elect got out and stood on the road in the snow, and after a moment Zofia got out too, and the two of them walked down through the snow to the place where the meadow began.

The river was not frozen. That was the thing. It was the mild winter of 1958, and the Vistula was running grey and open through Warsaw, and the Mławka at the bend was black and quick and steaming in the cold, and there was ice on the millpond and on the flooded meadows, but the river itself was open, and the water went past in the dark with the sound of water going past, and there was no ice to lie on.

They stood on the bank.

"There is no ice," said Zofia.

"There is no ice," said Michał.

"Róża said: take him back to the river in the winter and lie down on the ice with him. And there is no ice, and Róża is dead, and the bell is out of the water, and the number is on it, and in March I will be in Płock in the provincial department with a typist and four officers, and there is no ice."

"No."

She put her hand through his arm.

"There was never a bell in the river," she said. "That is the truth, and I have known it since I was six years old. The bell was in the millrace, and we knew where it was the whole time, and the story of the bell in the river was a story the village told to the children so that the children would believe that a thing that is lost can still ring." She looked at the water. "It is a lie, and it worked. It worked for three hundred years. The children believed it, and the boys who went to the war believed it, and the ones who did not come back believed it, and a woman in a burning church in 1945 took a lamp off a chain because she believed it, and a woman with a chisel in 1943 cut a number into a bell because she believed it, and today there is a bell hanging on two ladders in a field with a number on it, and it rang, Michał. It rang, and it was out of the water, and it rang for the whole village."

"It was the church bell," said Michał.

"It was the church bell," said Zofia. "And it was the bell in the river. And I have spent my whole life trying to decide which one it was, and I have decided, and the answer is: both. That is not a compromise. It is the arithmetic. The bell in the river is the thing that rings when it is not supposed to be able to ring. And a country that has been taken apart and put back together three times in a hundred and fifty years is a country that runs on the bell in the river, and I have been working for fifteen years in a room with a window that does not open, and I have been telling myself that I am the church bell, Michał. That I am the bell that rings on the hour, the reliable one, the one in the tower with the mechanism and the men who keep the mechanism. And I am not." She looked at him. "I am the other one. I have been under the water for fifteen years, and I have rung when I was not supposed to, and the state has a file on me, and the file says *contact material*, and it is the truest thing that has ever been written about me."

He turned and took her hands.

"Zosia. I am going to ask you a question, and it is not the question of 1938, and it is not the question of 1941, and it is not the question in the kitchen of the mill." He held her hands in both of his. "It is the question of a man of thirty-nine who is going to be a bishop in twenty-eight days. Will you come to Płock?"

"Yes."

"And will you be in the room with me for twenty years, four times a year, with the starosta and the secretary and the report?"

"Yes."

"And when you are old, and I am old, and there is no Office and no file and no starosta, will you be in the room with me then?"

"Yes."

"Then there is no curse," said Michał. "There is no curse, and there never was, and the widow at the grave was a woman in her grief, and she took the milk to our house every Saturday for a year, and the bell rang for everyone I loved before it rang for me, and the ones it rang for are dead, and I am alive, and you are alive, and the bell is hanging on two ladders in a field with a number on it, and the number is mine, and it did not ring for me. It rang *with* me. That is the thing I have understood today, on this bank, in a mild winter with no ice. It rang with me."

He kissed her.

They stood on the bank of the Mławka at the bend below the mill, in the snow, in the dark, in the winter of 1958, and they kissed for the first time since August 1941, and the river ran past them, open and black, with the sound of water going past.

* * *

The report of the journey was written in her hand on the evening of the twenty-ninth, in Warsaw, at the desk in her flat, with the lamp on the table beside it burning.

It is four pages long, and three and a half of them are what a report of a journey is: the state of the walls, the tower, the foundations, the rubble, the cement, the plan for the beginning of works in April. On the fourth page there is this:

*"W dniu 28.12 br. dokonano wydobycia dzwonu parafialnego, zatopionego w kanale młyńskim w 1943 r. Dzwon przekazano parafii do czasu zawieszenia w odbudowanym kościele. Obecność: ks. M. Żurawski (nominat), przedstawiciele parafii, ludność miejscowa (ok. 300 osób)."*

*"On 28 December this year the parish bell was recovered, sunk in the mill channel in 1943. The bell was handed to the parish pending its hanging in the rebuilt church. Present: Fr. M. Żurawski (bishop-designate), representatives of the parish, local population (about 300 persons)."*

And at the bottom of the page, under the stamp, in the space for remarks, in a hand that was tired and very small, she wrote one more sentence, and then crossed it out with two lines of the pen, not enough to make it unreadable, enough to make it a thing that a typist would have copied only if she had been careless:

*"Dzwon ma na sobie numer 119278, wybity w 1943 r. przez matkę nominata. Numer pozostawić."*

*"The bell bears the number 119278, cut in 1943 by the bishop-designate's mother. The number to be left as it is."*

The typist was careful. The sentence is in the file.

# Chapter Ten: The Red Hat

*Warsaw and Płock, 1959–1971*

There is a photograph of the consecration, and I have it, and it is not the one that was taken by the young man in the gallery of St. Anne's.

It was taken in the sacristy of the cathedral of St. John on the twenty-fifth of January 1959, at a quarter past two in the afternoon, by the diocesan photographer, a Mr. Sarnicki, who had been taking photographs of confirmations and first communions since 1934 and had lost one hand in the Uprising and had a prosthesis he had learned to work a shutter with. It is a photograph of the sacristy with the bishops in it, and the new bishop is in the middle in a white vestment with the mitre on his head, and beside him is the consecrating bishop, and on either side the co-consecrators, and behind them the canons and the professors and the family. In the front row, at the edge, slightly out of focus because Mr. Sarnicki had been hurrying, there is a woman in a grey coat and a black hat, standing a little apart from the diocesan officials, with her gloves in her hand and her hand pressed flat against her side, and she is not looking at the camera. She is looking at the bishop.

The photograph is not the reason I keep it.

The reason I keep it is that on the back of it, in pencil, in the hand of a careful person who had been given a ruler and a name, there is a circle drawn around the head of the woman in the grey coat, and beside the circle there is a number, and the number is 119278.

* * *

The year 1959 began with a red hat, and the hat was not in a hatbox.

The hat had been on the Primate's head since May 1957, when Pius XII had put it there in a room in the Vatican with the doors shut, because the Primate of Poland had been created a cardinal on the twelfth of January 1953 and had not been able to go to Rome to be given the hat, and had waited four years for it while the file said *sprawa otwarta.*

What came in January 1959 was a protocol.

It came from the Ministry of Foreign Trade, and it was four pages long, and it concerned the import of one cardinal's hat, and it had been drafted in December by a man in the customs administration who had been asked by a man in the Office who had been asked by a man in the Central Committee to establish, in writing, whether the state had at any time permitted the importation of an item of ecclesiastical headgear of a rank superior to a bishop's, and if so, on what tariff line, and at what rate of duty.

The hat had come in 1957 in the luggage of the Primate himself, on his own head, and no customs officer had asked him to take it off, and there was no record of it anywhere, and the protocol said so, in the flat language of a ministry that has discovered the limits of its own paperwork. It was circulated for comment in the last week of January. Zofia read it at her desk with her coat still on.

At the bottom of the fourth page, in the space for remarks, in her own hand, she wrote: *Brak podstaw do dalszych czynności. Sprawę zamknąć.* No grounds for further action. Close the matter.

And then she sat for a while with her pen in her hand, in the office on Krucza Street, and she thought about a clerk in a customs administration who had spent a month of his life establishing the duty on a hat, and about the enormous, patient, stupid machine that had spent four years deciding what to do about a hat that was already on a head, and about the fact that she was, at that desk, at that hour, the person who had been given the power to close the matter, and that she had used the power, and that it had taken eleven words, and that no one would ever know.

It was the last week of January. It had been a month since Świerczyna.

She had not written to him, and he had not written to her. They had agreed on it in the car, in the dark, on the road back from the bend, with Krzysiek driving and the snow in the headlights: no letters. They had agreed that a letter is a document, that a document is a thing that can be found, that the Office read the mail of its own officers twice a year as a matter of routine, and that a bishop-elect of Płock and a director of the Catholic section could not exchange a single line until the transfer was through.

"And after the transfer?" she had said.

"After the transfer we will have the quarterly meeting," he had said, "and the quarterly meeting will be minuted, and the minutes will be binding, and the minutes are the only letters we will ever be allowed to write to each other."

She had laughed, in the car, in the dark, and it had been the first time she had laughed since the funeral in July.

She went back to the table and finished her tea and put on her coat, and at the door she stopped and went back and cut the four lines out of the *Trybuna Ludu* with the kitchen scissors, and put the cutting in the tin, and put the tin in the drawer, and went out to the Office.

* * *

The Office in January 1959 was a building in which the ground had begun to move.

The Party had decided on the schools. The First Secretary was to speak at the Twelfth Plenum in the spring, and the speech was being written in a room in the Central Committee by four men and a woman, and the phrase that had been agreed was *a thousand schools for the millennium*, and it was already being painted on the hoardings of the new housing estates, in letters a metre high, before the speech had been made. In the Office the schools were a matter for the Department of Education and not for the Catholic section, but everything that had to do with a school had also to do with a church, because a school was a building and a church was a building and the country had a fixed quantity of cement.

And the agreement of the nineteenth of December was a problem.

It had been signed in a room with the door shut, and it had been classified as secret until the propaganda line was established, and the propaganda line had not been established, and in the meantime the bishop of Płock had asked for the permits in writing, and the provincial department had begun to implement them, and a journalist on the *Słowo Powszechne* had heard about the church at Świerczyna from a priest in Mława and had written an article, and the article had been killed by the censor, and the censor had telephoned the Office to ask what the line was, and the Office had said that the line was being prepared.

The line was being prepared in the Minister's office on the third floor, at the end of the corridor, in a room with a window that did not open.

"Zofia," said Sztachelski, on the eighth of January, with the file of the agreement on the desk in front of him. "Sit down. I am going to tell you something that is not in the file, and then I am going to ask you a question, and you are going to answer it as an officer."

"Yes, Minister."

"The Party is going to fight the millennium. Not the Church. The millennium. In 1966 there will be a thousand years of the Polish state and a thousand years of the Polish Church, and the Party has decided that the state's millennium will be the schools, and the Church's millennium will be the picture and the Novena and the crowds. And the Party has decided that the state's millennium will be in the newspapers and on the hoardings every day for seven years, and that the Church's millennium will be in the parishes and the villages and the hearts of the people, and that the Party will lose that fight." He put his hand flat on the file. "I have said this to two men in the Central Committee, in the strictest confidence, and one of them agreed with me and the other one wrote my name in a notebook. I am telling it to you because you are the best officer I have and because in March you are going to Płock, and because when you are in Płock you will be the only officer of this Office who is in a room with a bishop every quarter, and I want you to understand what you are doing there."

"I am implementing the agreement of the nineteenth of December."

"You are building the only bridge in Poland," said Sztachelski. "There are twelve parishes in the Commission and eleven of them are a fiction and a photograph and a roof. Świerczyna is the only one where two people who trust each other signed a paper, and the paper will hold, and in five years there will be other papers, and in ten years there will be a bishop in Płock who has been in the room for a decade, and the men in the Central Committee will hate him and they will deal with him, and in 1966, when the Party is putting up its thousand schools and the Church is putting up its thousand churches, there will be a man in Płock who has done both, and he will be the only man in Poland who can say a word that both sides will listen to." He looked up. "That is what I am sending you to Płock for. Not for him. For the room."

Zofia sat with her hands in her lap.

"Minister," she said, "there is a thing I have to tell you, and I am going to tell it to you now, in this room, before I go to Płock, because if I do not tell it to you now I will be telling it to a colonel in three months."

"I know," said Sztachelski.

"You do not know what I am going to say."

"I know what you are going to say," said Sztachelski, "because I read your file in 1956 and I read the file of the priest in 1957 and I have been in a room with the two of you since February, and I am not a fool, and I was a doctor for twenty years and a doctor learns to see a thing in a man's face before the man has said it. You are going to tell me that you have known the bishop since you were children in the same village, and that the two of you were in love, and that the paper of the nineteenth of December was signed by two people who were in love, and that you are afraid that the file, when it is opened, will say so."

Zofia looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

"Good," said Sztachelski. "Then I am going to tell you the thing that is not in the file, and after today it will never be spoken of between us again. I do not care. Do you understand? I am the Minister for Religious Denominations of the Polish People's Republic, and I do not care whether my best officer is in love with a bishop, because the officer is the best officer I have and the bishop is the only man in the Episcopate who can sign a paper that will hold, and I have spent three years looking for the two of them and I have found them in the same file. If I remove you, I get Klimek. If I remove him, I get a bishop who will preach at the barricade and be arrested in 1961. The state will survive the love affair. It will not survive the loss of the bridge."

"That is a very cold speech," said Zofia.

"It is the coldest speech I have ever made," said Sztachelski, "and I have made it to you because you are the only person in this building who will understand it and not despise me for it. But I am going to add one thing, and it is not cold, and it is not in any file. In 1941 I was a doctor in Wilno, and there was a woman in the ghetto, and I could not save her, and I have been in rooms with men who could have saved her ever since, and I have learned that a man does what he can do and not what he should do, and that a man who waits until he can do what he should do does nothing at all." He picked up his fountain pen. "Go to Płock. Build the bridge. And if the colonel opens the file in three years, I will be the Minister and I will close it, and if I am not the Minister, then the bridge will be there and the file will not matter. That is all I can do. It is not enough. It is what there is."

He signed the transfer that afternoon.

* * *

The consecration was on the twenty-fifth of January, and it was not quiet.

It was not quiet because the protocol on the hat had come back from the ministries with four signatures on it and had been leaked to a journalist in Kraków, and the journalist had written that the state had spent four years deciding what to do about a hat, and the article had been killed and copied and passed from hand to hand, and by the middle of January every priest in Poland had heard it and every priest in Poland was telling it in the sacristy, and because in the second week of January the Primate had announced, from the pulpit of St. John's, that the Church would build its own school in the parish of St. Anne's — not a parish hall, a school, a school for the children of the city who could no longer be taught the catechism in the classrooms of the state — and the announcement had been in every newspaper for two days, and the Office had issued a statement that the matter would be considered in accordance with the law, and the newspaper had printed the statement under a photograph of the Primate in the red hat.

And it was not quiet because the militia had a report, and the report was from a man at the back of the church of St. Anne's on the sixth of January, and the report said that the congregation on the Epiphany had been the largest in the history of the parish since 1945, and that among them had been officers of the Office for Religious Denominations, and that the director of the Catholic section had been seen to leave by the sacristy door after the sermon.

The report is in the file, and there is a pencil note on it in the hand of Colonel Wesoły, and the note says: *Oczywiście.* Of course.

The cathedral of St. John held two thousand people. There were more than three thousand in it. They stood in the aisles and in the porch and in the street, in the snow, and the militia had put a cordon at the end of Świętojańska Street and the cordon was not needed and was there anyway.

The Office had sent an observer. The observer was Zofia Wierzbicka, director of the Catholic section, and she came in a grey coat and a black hat, and she was shown to a place in the second row on the left, among the officials of the diocese and the professors of the seminary, and the colonel was three rows behind her and to the right, in a good coat, with his wife.

She had not seen Michał since the bank of the river, and she had never in her life seen him in a cope.

He came up the aisle at the head of the procession with his hands joined and his eyes down, and the choir sang the *Veni Creator*, and the whole cathedral stood, and the people in the aisles stood on tiptoe, and the people in the porch stood with their ears, and the people in the street stood in the snow and could hear nothing and stood anyway.

The ceremony of the consecration of a bishop is four hours long, and it consists of things that have been done in the same order since the fourth century: the examination, the imposition of hands, the anointing of the head and the hands, the giving of the book of the Gospels, the ring, the mitre. The bishop-elect lies face down on the floor of the sanctuary during the litany, and the whole cathedral prays over him, and it is the oldest and strangest moment in the ceremony, because the man who is about to become a bishop lies on the floor like a corpse, and the Church prays over him like a body.

Zofia watched him lie on the marble floor of St. John's with his arms spread in the form of a cross, and the choir sang the litany of the saints in Latin, and she did not believe any of it, and she was, for the length of the litany, unable to move.

*Sancte Michael, ora pro nobis. Sancte Gabriel, ora pro nobis. Sancte Raphael, ora pro nobis.*

She had not heard the litany since 1939, in the church at Świerczyna, at the funeral of a boy of seven, with the widow standing at the graveside and the whole village holding its breath.

*Omnes sancti Angeli et Archangeli, orate pro nobis.*

The choir sang the names, and the names were the names of the dead of the whole world, and Zofia stood in the second row of the cathedral of St. John in the grey coat and the black hat, and the thing that happened to her has no name in the language of the Office.

She was not converted. She has been very clear about that — in the memorandum to herself of the eighth of January, in the letter she wrote in the spring, and in the note she left with her papers — and she says the same thing each time: *I did not believe. I have never believed. There is no God and I am not afraid of the dark.* What she says is this: that during the litany she heard the names of the saints, and the names were the names of the village, and she understood — not with her mind, with her whole body, the way you understand cold — that she had been carrying the village on her back since 1944, and that the village had been carrying her, and that the carrying had a name, and the name was not God and not the Party and not the state. The name was the field, and the mill, and the bend, and the bell, and the boy on the floor of the cathedral with his arms spread out like a corpse.

And in the moment when they raised him up and put the mitre on his head, and the whole cathedral made a sound that was not a cheer and was not a hymn, she turned her head and looked at the colonel three rows back, and the colonel was looking at her, and his face was the face of a man at a funeral.

* * *

Afterward, in the sacristy, with the bishops and the monsignors and the professors and the family, Zofia stood at the back with her hat in her hand, and the new bishop came down the line of the people who had come to kiss his ring, and she was the last in the line, and she knelt, because the protocol said that the observer of the Office would kneel, and she kissed the ring on his hand, and he said, in the voice of the ceremony, so that everyone could hear:

"*Bóg zapłać*, pani director."

And then, in the press of the people who had come to congratulate him, with a hundred people in the room and a colonel at the door, the new bishop of Płock leaned down and said, in a voice only she could hear:

"From the fourth of March there is a meeting. The starosta, the secretary, the report. I will see that the Office names you."

"From the fourth of March," said Zofia.

"And in the weeks before the fourth of March," said the bishop, "you will not write to me, and I will not write to you, and the file will be quiet, and on the fourth of March you will come into the room in Płock with the report book, and I will be at the other side of the table, and we will talk about the cement."

"The cement," said Zofia.

"The cement," said the bishop of Płock, and straightened up, and blessed the room.

* * *

Her transfer came through in the last week of February 1959, and she went north on the fourth of March with two suitcases and a crate of books, in a second-class carriage that stopped forty minutes at Nasielsk for a freight, and the flat was two rooms on the second floor of a building on the old square, with a window that opened, and the provincial department had a typist named Basia and four officers, one of whom was a man of sixty named Nowak who had been in the Home Army and who recognized her at once, because her name had been in a file in 1945 and he had been the man who wrote the file.

The first quarterly meeting of the new arrangement was held on the seventh of April 1959, in the room of the provincial department with the window that opened, and there were five people in it: the starosta, a man named Kęsik who had been a peasant's son and a partisan and a party man and who was in 1959 a tired and decent official with a bad liver; a secretary with a shorthand notebook; the bishop of Płock; the officer of the Office responsible for the implementation of the agreement; and the old parish priest of Mława, who had come because the bishop had asked him, and who sat at the end of the table with his hands folded and did not say a word until the end.

The minutes are three pages long, and they are in the file, and they are the most boring minutes I have ever read. They concern the cement, which had arrived in March; the drainage of the site, which had been completed in April; the contract with the glazier, who had died in February and whose son had taken over the workshop and wanted a new price; the question of the boundary wall, which the conservator had declared a monument; and the question of the bell, which the conservator had also declared a monument, and which the parish wished to hang in the new tower in the autumn.

At the end of the meeting, the starosta said: "Is there anything else, Father? Anything not in the minutes?"

The bishop of Płock looked at the officer of the Office across the table.

"No," he said. "There is nothing that is not in the minutes."

The parish priest of Mława said, "There is one thing, and I am an old man and I will say it, and then I will go home. The number on the bell. The conservator wants to take it off and put it in a museum, because it is a document. The parish wants to leave it on the bell. I have been a priest for forty-one years and I am going to say something that is not in the minutes, and the secretary may write it down if he likes. The number on the bell is a name. It was a number in a camp and it is a name in a parish, and if you take it off the bell and put it behind glass in a museum, you have turned a name back into a number. Leave it on the bell."

There was a silence in the room.

"The Office agrees," said Zofia.

"The state agrees," said the starosta, after a moment, and the secretary wrote it down.

And the bishop of Płock said, "Then we have finished, and I am grateful to you all, and the next meeting will be in July." He stood up. "Pani director, I am told that you are to be congratulated on the report of the journey of December. The Ministry has circulated it as a model. It is the finest report of a journey I have ever read."

"Thank you, Bishop."

"It is four pages long," said the bishop, "and three and a half of them are the cement."

* * *

There were six quarterly meetings at Płock while she was alive — the first in April 1959, the last in July 1960 — and a seventh on the ninth of November 1960, three weeks after the funeral, and after the personnel file had already been closed with one line in a clerk's hand. I have read the minutes of all seven, because they are in the file, and the file is in the State Archive in Płock, and I sat in the reading room of that archive for eleven days in the spring of 1969 with a pencil and a notebook, and the archivist, who was a young woman with a bad cold, brought me the file on the second day and after that let me have it without asking.

The minutes are dull. The minutes are the minutes of a quarterly meeting on the implementation of an agreement concerning a parish church, and they concern the cement, the boundary wall, the bell, the drainage, the contract for the painting of the interior, the bus route that the starosta wanted to run past the church and the bishop did not, and the question of whether the parish could have a second Mass on Sunday in the summer months.

But there is a thing in them that I did not see until the ninth day, and it is this: in the minutes of the seventh meeting, which was held on the ninth of November 1960, in the room with the window that opened, there is a paragraph that concerns the cemetery, and it says:

*"W związku z pogrzebem ob. Z. Wierzbickiej na cmentarzu parafialnym w Świerczynie, strona kościelna zwraca się z prośbą o wyrażenie zgody na stałe utrzymanie grobu przez parafię. Strona państwowa nie wnosi zastrzeżeń."*

*"In connection with the funeral of com. Z. Wierzbicka in the parish cemetery at Świerczyna, the Church side requests consent for the perpetual maintenance of the grave by the parish. The state side raises no objection."*

"Com. Z. Wierzbicka." Comrade. In the minutes of a meeting of the Polish People's Republic, in November 1960, the woman who had been the officer of the Office was "comrade," because the secretary wrote what the secretary was told to write, and the bishop of Płock had said, in the room, *the late director Wierzbicka*, and the secretary, who had been trained in 1951, had written *com.*

I do not know what the bishop said when he read the minutes. There is a fair copy in the file with the corrections in his hand, and the corrections are all the same kind of correction: *cement, 40 tons, not 14; the glazier's son, not the glazier; the well, not the wall.* He did not correct *com.*

Perhaps he did not see it. Perhaps he saw it and let it stand, because a correction in his hand would have been a thing that a man in an office in 1980 would have read.

* * *

Ania Żurawska lived until 1964. She saw the church finished and the bell hung and the first Mass said in it by her son, and she sat in the front pew on the day of the consecration of the church in October 1960, in a black coat, and did not cry, and afterward she said to the parish priest of Mława, "It is a very fine church and the acoustics are bad and the bell is in the right place."

Zofia died at Otwock on the fourteenth of October 1960, of a cancer that had begun in the pancreas and was found too late, and she was forty-one years old.

She had been ill since the spring. She did not tell the Office and she did not tell the bishop, and she went to the quarterly meeting in April and in July, and at the July meeting she was grey and thin and the starosta asked her whether she was well and she said that she had been on a diet; and the bishop looked at her across the table and knew, and said nothing, because she had asked him in April, in the church at Świerczyna, with the bell above them in the tower, not to say anything to her about it until she said it to him.

She said it in a letter of four lines on the twenty-ninth of August, and the letter is in the file, because it was found in his desk and he put it in the file himself, in an envelope marked *sprawa osobista* — personal matter — which is the only document in four hundred pages that is not about the state.

> *Wielce Czcigodny Księże Biskupie,*
>
> *Mam nowotwór trzustki. Lekarze twierdzą, że nie ma operacji. Zostaje mi kilka miesięcy. Proszę nie przyjeżdżać do Płocka z tego powodu i proszę mi nie pisać listów pocieszenia, bo nie umiem ich czytać. Proszę przyjechać, kiedy będzie można mówić o cemencie.*
>
> *Z. Wierzbicka*

> *Right Reverend Bishop,*
>
> *I have a tumour of the pancreas. The doctors say there is no operation. I have a few months. Please do not come to Płock for this reason and please do not write me letters of consolation, because I cannot read them. Please come when it is possible to talk about the cement.*
>
> *Z. Wierzbicka*

He came to Otwock on the sixteenth of September and was with her for eleven days, and he came again on the second of October and stayed until it was over. The nun who was in the room told me in 1969 that the bishop sat by the bed for the whole of the last night with his hand on hers and did not pray out loud, and that in the morning, when it was over, he got up and went to the window that opened and stood there for a long time, and then he came back and closed her eyes and said the *De profundis* in Latin, very quietly, and then he sat down again and said, in Polish, to the nun, "Sister, she did not believe. She never believed. Remember that when you pray for her, and do not pray for her as though she were a child. She was the most intelligent woman I have ever known, and she did not believe, and she was right about everything except the last thing."

She was buried at Świerczyna on the eighteenth of October, in the parish cemetery, on the far side of the path from the graves of the Wierzbickis, because the parish council discussed it for an hour and decided, in the end, that a woman of the Office who had signed the agreement of the nineteenth of December could be buried in the ground of the parish but not in the family plot, and the bishop did not overrule them. He said, "It is the village's decision, and the village has made it, and I will not take the village's dead from it."

The stone says:

*ZOFIA WIERZBICKA*

*1919 – 1960*

And nothing else. There is no cross on it. That was her instruction, in a letter of four lines that he put in the file in an envelope marked *sprawa osobista*, and the bishop obeyed it; and in 1961 a woman of the parish put a small silver lamp on the grave, and it was stolen in 1963, and another was put there in 1964 by Ania Żurawska, and it is there now.

* * *

The file of Zofia Wierzbicka, personnel section, Office for Religious Denominations, was closed with a single line, in a clerk's hand, on the eighteenth of October 1960:

*Akta osobowe — zmarła 14.10.1960. Sprawę wykreślić z ewidencji.*

Personnel file — died 14.10.1960. Strike the matter from the register.

It was signed by the chief of the personnel section, and the chief of the personnel section was Klimek, and he signed it without reading the file, because it was a Friday, and because there was nothing in it that could be used.

* * *

I went back to the bishop in November 1971, six weeks after the parcel came and a fortnight after Świerczyna, with the photograph in my bag and a list of questions I had been writing since the spring.

He was living in the bishops' house at Płock, retired, in two rooms on the first floor with a balcony and a garden, and he was fifty-two years old and had been retired since 1970, and he walked with a stick, and he had a housekeeper named pani Basia who had been the typist of the provincial department in 1959 and had come to him when he retired and who guarded him like a dog.

He received me in the garden, on a bench, in the sun, with the stick across his knees.

"You are Inka Balcerzak," he said. "Zofia's goddaughter."

"Yes."

"You were baptized in 1943 in a kitchen in the Old Town with water from a kettle, and I was the one who did it, and there was a woman in the next room who was holding the door shut with her foot because the police were in the street." He looked at me. "Your godmother stood proxy for you that winter, in a churchyard at Świerczyna, and nobody told me for fifteen years, because in that parish nothing was ever written down that a German could read." He put his hand on the stick. "You have her eyebrows."

"I have been told."

"You have her way of looking at a man as though he were a document that had been sent to you by mistake." He put his hand on the stick. "What do you want?"

"I want to know what happened in December 1958."

The bishop was quiet for a long time. A bee went past us and into the lavender, and the sun was on the wall, and somewhere in the house pani Basia was doing something with a pot.

"Nothing happened in December 1958," he said. "There is a report of a journey in the file. It is four pages long and three and a half of them are about the cement."

"Bishop."

"There was no adultery," said the bishop. "You are going to write a book, and you are going to want there to have been an adultery, because an adultery is a thing that a reader can hold. There was no adultery. There was a woman in a grey coat on a bank in the snow with the report book of the Office in her bag, and there was a man in a black coat on the ice with the men and the winch, and there was a bell with a number on it, and afterwards there was a car on the road and a river with no ice on it, and a promise made in a kitchen to a dying woman, and seven meetings about cement." He looked at me. "The rest is in the file."

"The file has a sentence crossed out," I said. "In her hand. About the number on the bell."

The bishop turned his head.

"So it does," he said.

"The typist was careful," I said. "The sentence is in the file."

"The typist was a good woman," said the bishop. "She is in the house now, making a soup."

He looked out at the garden for a while.

"You want me to tell you that it was worth it," he said. "That is what the young want. You want me to say: we did the work, and the work held, and in 1966 the Church had its millennium and the state had its schools and the two of them stood in the same country and did not shoot each other, and a boy from Świerczyna who would have been a miller in 1938 became a bishop and a girl who would have been a schoolteacher in 1938 became the only officer of the state who could be trusted with a paper. And it was worth it. But that is not the whole of it, and you are a translator, and a translator knows that a sentence can be true and incomplete at the same time."

"Then tell me the whole of it."

The bishop of Płock looked at me for a long time.

"The whole of it," he said, "is that I was in a room with her for eighteen months and I never once in my life took her to the bend on the ice in the winter, because the ice was not there, and the river is not a story, and a man cannot make a story happen by wanting it. We had eleven days in Otwock. Eleven days, in a room with a nun and a window that opened, and she was dying, and she was in pain, and she held my hand and we talked about the cement. That is the whole of it. The rest was the room."

He stood up, with the stick.

"I am going to say the blessing," he said, "and then you will go, and you will write your book, and you will get it wrong, and it will be a good book, and she would have corrected your Polish." He put his hand on my head. "Benedictio Dei omnipotentis, Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti, descendat super te et maneat semper. Amen."

* * *

I went from Płock to Świerczyna in a hired car, in the rain, in October.

The village is bigger now. There is a school with the number 29 and a bus that goes to Mława four times a day, and the co-operative has a new roof, and the mill is a milk-collection point with a metal tank and a noticeboard, and the millrace has been filled in since 1963 and it is a meadow with a path across it, and the place where the bell came out of the water is a patch of grass with a stone in it and the stone has a brass plate that says:

*TU WYDOBYTO DZWON PARAFIALNY 28 XII 1958*

Here, on 28 December 1958, the parish bell was recovered.

The letters are cut in capitals and one of them — the *Y* in *WYDOBYTO* — is a small letter, because the man who cut it was given the text on a piece of paper and did not look up.

The church is there. It is a good church, of brick, with a tower that is not leaning, and the bell is in the tower and it rings for the Mass and for the dead and for the fire, and on the wall of the porch there is a list of the priests of the parish since 1642, and at the bottom of the list, in a different and newer stone, there is:

*KS. MICHAŁ ŻURAWSKI*

*RODZIC PARAFII — BISKUP PŁOCKI*

*1919 —*

The *1919 —* is the only unfinished thing in the church.

I stood in the porch for a long time with the rain coming down on the road, and then I went to the cemetery.

Her grave is on the far side of the path from the Wierzbickis, and there is a silver lamp on it, and the stone says *ZOFIA WIERZBICKA / 1919–1960* and nothing else, and there is no cross, and the rain had made dark lines on the stone that looked like tears and were not.

I stood at the grave of my godmother, in the rain, in October 1971, with the parcel in my bag — the photograph, the tin, the pouch, the lamp, and the sealed envelope — and I did the thing that I had come to do, which was nothing at all. I did not read the letter. I did not open the envelope. I stood in the rain and I put my hand on the stone, and the stone was cold, and I said, out loud, in Polish:

"Ciociu. I have got it wrong."

And then I went back to the car and drove to Warsaw, and the rain came down, and at Mława I stopped at a shop and bought a newspaper, and the newspaper had a photograph of the First Secretary on the front page, and inside, on the fourth page, there was a small notice that a bishop had died in Kraków, and I read it in the car with the rain on the windscreen, and then I put the newspaper down and drove on.

* * *

The sealed envelope is on my desk, and it is addressed in her hand to *ks. Michał Żurawski, biskup, Płock*, and the handwriting of the address is the handwriting of the letters in the tin.

I have opened it. I opened it on the night the parcel came, with a knife, at my kitchen table, at two in the morning, and I am not sorry, and I am not going to print it.

I am not going to print it because it is not mine, and because the woman who wrote it wrote it to a man and not to a reader, and because in the course of three years I have taken everything else of hers — the report, the minutes, the memorandum, the letter that was returned to sender, the sentence crossed out at the bottom of a page — and I have decided, in the end, to leave her one thing. It is a small thing. It is the only thing she ever kept that was not a document, and it has been in a drawer and then in a parcel and then on a desk for eleven years, and I am going to put it back in the tin, and put the tin in the drawer, and leave it to the archive with the rest, closed, with a note in my hand that says: *Nie otwierać przed rokiem 2000. I. Balcerzak.* Do not open before the year 2000.

I will tell you one thing about it, because it belongs to the story and not to the woman. There is no address at the top of the letter and no signature at the bottom. There are four sentences. The first sentence is a date: *Otwock, 4 października 1960.* The second sentence is: *Jestem sama i nie boję się.* I am alone and I am not afraid. The third sentence is: *Dzwon jest w wieży, a ja jestem w wodzie, i to jest to samo.* The bell is in the tower, and I am in the water, and it is the same thing. And the fourth sentence is four words long, and it is the shortest document she ever wrote, and it is the only one in the whole of her file that has a word in it that appears nowhere else in four hundred pages.

It is not a word I am going to give you. Some things a translator keeps.

* * *

The photograph is on my desk. It is the one from the gallery of St. Anne's, with the pencil circle around the head of the woman in the grey coat, and the number 119278 written beside it in the hand of a careful person.

Above it, in a frame, is the other photograph, the one Mr. Sarnicki took in the sacristy of St. John's on the twenty-fifth of January 1959, with the bishops in the front row and the woman at the edge, slightly out of focus, looking at the bishop.

And beside that, in the saucer, is the silver lamp.

It was in the parcel, wrapped in the horse blanket that my godmother's mother had carried it out of a burning church in, and it had not been lit since the autumn of 1960. I have cleaned it. I have bought a bottle of lamp oil. I have not lit it.

I am going to light it on the sixth of January, and I am going to let it burn all night, and in the morning there will be a black ring on the ceiling of my flat, and the year after there will be another one, and the year after that another, and I will be, in the end, a woman of forty with a ceiling full of rings and a drawer full of files, and I will have paid something, and the payment will not be specified, and no one will ever tell me what it was for.

That is what she did. It is not a faith. It is a method.

I have written this book because I am a translator and because there was a document in a folder that no one was going to read, and because a woman in a grey coat stood at the back of a church on the sixth of January 1958 and decided, in the space of a sermon about the Magi, to go home by another road. She did not take it. She never took it. She stayed in the room for the rest of her life, and the room had a window that did not open, and she built a school and a church and a bell out of it.

The bell is in the tower at Świerczyna. It rings on the hour, and it is cracked, and the crack makes it a voice and not a bell. If you stand in the porch of that church on a Sunday morning, with the door open and the light coming in and the people going up the aisle with their hats in their hands, you can hear it, and it is not a pure sound.

It is the sound of a thing that was under the water for fifteen years and was brought up out of the mud by eleven men and a boy with a winch and a net of woven hazel, and it has a number on it, and the number is a name, and the village left it there.

# Historical Note

This novel takes place in a country that no longer exists, and it uses that
country's institutions as they were, not as they are remembered. These pages
separate what the record establishes from what this book has invented.

**The Office.** The Office for Religious Denominations (*Urząd do Spraw Wyznań*)
was created on 19 April 1950, attached directly to the Presidium of the
Government, and dissolved in 1989. It licensed church buildings, approved or
refused the appointment of parish priests, kept files on the clergy, ran the
"patriotic priest" movement, and was in the ordinary course of business the
interlocutor of every bishop in Poland. From 1956 to 1961 it was headed by
**Jerzy Sztachelski**, a physician by training and a minister without portfolio,
who appears in this book as a historical figure. The scenes in which he appears
are invented; the position he argues — that the state could not abolish the
Church and would have to administer it — is the position the record attributes to
him, and the reader may judge how well he argued it.

**The Great Novena.** On 3 May 1957 the Episcopate, under **Stefan Wyszyński**,
Primate of Poland, opened the Great Novena (*Wielka Nowenna*): nine years of
prayer, confession and parish missions preparing for the millennium of the
baptism of Poland in 1966. Its engine was a consecrated copy of the icon of Our
Lady of Częstochowa that travelled from parish to parish; by the end of 1958 it
had been met by crowds the Party could not explain and could not stop. The state's
counter-campaign of 1958 — the refusal of building permits, the pressure on local
officials, the July letter of the Party Secretariat to the voivodeship
committees, the raid on Jasna Góra on 21 July — is the weather of this book, and
the picture's detour around Świerczyna is the kind of thing that
happened in hundreds of parishes that year.

**The fourth of August.** On 4 August 1958 the Minister of Education, Władysław
Bieńkowski, issued a circular on the secular school: crucifixes and religious
emblems were to be removed from classrooms, prayer was forbidden at the start and
end of lessons, and religious instruction was to be taught only after the
compulsory timetable, by catechists who were not members of religious orders. In
the villages it was called *the night of the fourth of August*, and the memory of
it outlasted the circular; teachers who removed the cross from the wall and
teachers who refused were both, in different parishes, made to answer for it. One
chapter takes the liberty of putting a cross in a drawer and a broken window in a
village school — an event of the kind that occurred, not a documented event at a
documented place.

On 24 September 1958, at the Twelfth Plenum of the Central Committee, Władysław
Gomułka announced the campaign of *a thousand schools for the thousand years*, the
state's secular answer to the Church's millennium. The phrase was on the hoardings
before the speech was made, exactly as the novel says.

**Rome, 1958.** Pius XII died on 9 October 1958. The conclave of 25–28 October
elected Angelo Giuseppe Roncalli, who took the name John XXIII and was crowned on
4 November. The consistory he announced on 17 November, and held on 15 December
1958, created twenty-three cardinals. It did not create Stefan Wyszyński: the
Primate had been made a cardinal by Pius XII on 12 January 1953, had been unable
to travel to Rome to receive the red hat, and had finally received it from the
Pope's own hands in May 1957. The Office's file on the matter, in this book, did
not know that. The novel's red hat is therefore not a hat but a protocol about a
hat — which is, in the view of this book, the more accurate comedy.

**The roundup and the number.** The roundups of Polish civilians in the spring of
1943, in reprisal for the killing of German gendarmes, are documented; the number
this novel gives its central character, 119278, is invented, and so is the miller's
family that carries it. The tattooing of numbers at Auschwitz-Birkenau from 1943 is
not. The letter from the camp is invented, but it is built out of
the forms such letters had to be written on, the censors they passed, and the
things men in them were permitted to say and were not.

**Two liberties.** The parish of Świerczyna, its mill, its river, its bell and its
people are invented, though their like stood in every second parish in Mazowsze. And
the arrangement at the centre of the book — a bishop who agrees to sit in a room
with the Office every quarter so that a church can be built, and an officer who
agrees to be the person in the room — is a fiction of motive and not of fact. No
such bargain is recorded. But the men and women who negotiated the real ones are
dead, and the files they left are open, and the files do not agree with each other.
