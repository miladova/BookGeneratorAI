# Chapter Six: Bread and Salt

*Świerczyna, July 1958*

The telegram came on a Tuesday in the last week of July, and it was eleven words long, and it was from Róża's son.

*MATKA BARDZO CHORA STOP PRZYJEŻDŻAJ STOP MŁAWA*

Mother very ill. Come. Mława.

Zofia had been in the Office since half past six. The telegram was brought up with the post at ten, and she read it standing in the middle of the room with the other letters in her hand, and Tosia said, from the typewriter, without looking up: "Bad news?"

"My sister is dying."

"Then go," said Tosia. "The Lord's work will keep."

Zofia looked at her.

"I mean the Office," said Tosia. "The Office will keep."

* * *

She had not been to Świerczyna in seven years.

She had gone in 1947 — no. Correct that. She had been at Świerczyna in 1947 twice: for the funeral of her grandmother in March, and in September for the sale of the family house. Then she had gone once more, in the spring of 1951, when her mother died in the hospital at Mława of a cancer of the stomach and was brought home to be buried, and Zofia had stood at the graveside in a black coat and gone back the same evening on the last train. And in 1946 Róża had written to her, the first letter in a year, six lines, to say that she was married to Wacek and expecting a child and living in the house of Wacek's mother on the Mława road, and Zofia had sent a parcel with a baby's blanket and a bank note and a letter that took four drafts and said, in the end, that she was well and overworked and thought of them all.

In 1947 Róża had written again, four lines, to say the child was a boy, Antek, and that she had been ill. And then nothing more, and Zofia had sent a parcel every Christmas, and had received, every Easter, a postcard with a hen on it, in Róża's round hand, and the postcards had stopped in 1957, and Zofia had told herself it was the post.

* * *

Róża had tuberculosis, and the left lung was gone, and the right lung was full of holes, and the doctors at the sanatorium in Otwock had sent her home in June because there was nothing more to be done and because the sanatorium needed the bed.

She was forty-one and looked fifty-nine. She lay in the good bed in the good room in the house on the Mława road, in a white gown of her mother's, and the window was open because she liked to hear the birds, and on the table beside her there was a photograph of her husband Wacek in his army uniform, Wacek who had been killed in 1952 by a tree, of all things, a tree that came down on him in the forest where he was working as a labourer for the state forestry, so that the state paid her a pension of two hundred and forty złoty a month and considered the matter closed.

Zofia came into the room at four in the afternoon of the Wednesday, in a hired car from Mława that cost her six hundred złoty and that she would not be able to explain in the Office, and Róża was awake.

"Well," said Róża. "The great lady."

"Rózia."

"Don't cry. I look like a devil and I know it, and crying makes you ugly, and then there will be two ugly women in the room and the boy will be frightened." Róża lifted a hand off the coverlet. "Come here and let me see if Warsaw has made you fat."

Zofia crossed the room and sat down on the chair by the bed and took her sister's hand, and the hand was a bird's hand, and the nails were painted, and Zofia had to look at the painted nails for a while before she could speak.

"Who did your nails?"

"Anka from the post office. She does them for the women in the village, two złoty, and she does a bad job, and I told her to do a bad job. A dying woman with a bad manicure is more respectable than a dying woman with a good one." Róża's breath was short, and she spoke in little runs and rested. "You look well. You look like the photograph in the paper."

"What photograph?"

"Your photograph. In *Trybuna Ludu*, when the bishops came to the Office in the spring. There was a photograph of the table and the backs of everybody's heads, and there was a woman at the end of the table in a grey dress, and Wacek's mother cut it out because she thought it was you, and it was you, and it is on the wall in the kitchen." Róża smiled. "It has been on the wall in the kitchen for four months, Zosia. Wacek's mother, who goes to the church twice on Sunday and once on the Saturday for the souls, has a photograph of a communist official on the wall of her kitchen, and when the priest comes on the first Friday she takes it down and puts it in a drawer, and on the Saturday she puts it back up. That is the whole of the last fifty years in this country, and it took a photograph to show it."

"You have not changed."

"I have changed. I have lost forty pounds and my left lung and my husband and my patience." Róża closed her eyes. "But I have not lost the tongue. Sit with me. Tell me about the great lady's life."

They talked for two hours. Zofia told her about the Office, and about the flat, and about the electric kettle that had been promised, and Róża laughed at the kettle and then coughed for a long time, and when the coughing was over she said, "You are going to tell me about the man," and Zofia said, "What man?" and Róża said, "The priest. From the village. Do you think a village forgets? The whole parish knows where he is. When he was made the rector in Warsaw in the year of the thaw, it was in the *Gość Niedzielny* under a photograph, and every woman in Świerczyna cut it out. He came to the parish in 1956, for the mission, and said the Mass in the field beyond the mill because the church is not built, and there were two thousand people, and my Antek was one of the boys who carried the banner, and I stood at the back with my hand on my side and wept like a fool." She opened her eyes. "Have you seen him?"

"Yes."

"Ah," said Róża. "There it is."

"It is not what you think."

"It is exactly what I think. I am dying, Zosia, and dying gives a woman a certain license, and I am going to say a thing to you that no one else in the world will say to you." Róża raised herself a little on the pillows. "You are a great lady with a photograph on the wall of a kitchen and a flat in Mokotów and a car with a driver, and you are the loneliest person I have ever met, and when you came into this room you looked at the door three times before you sat down. You are not afraid of the dying. You are afraid of the living. You have been afraid since the day you went east in 1944, and you have built a whole life out of being useful so that no one would notice."

"Róża."

"I am dying. You will be in a room with him, and the two of you will be very careful, and you will do good, and it will be the finest good, and it will be worth nothing, because the one thing a man like that can never be given is a woman who is careful." Róża put her hand on Zofia's wrist. "Take him back to the river. Not in the summer. In the winter, when the ice is on it. Take him back to the bend and lie down on the ice with him and listen to the bell, and let him be a boy for an hour, and then let him be a bishop, and you will have done more for him than the Office and the Primate and God put together."

Zofia sat with her sister's hand on her wrist.

"Do you know what he is?" she said. "Do you know what they will say? A bishop and a woman from the Office. In the same car. In the winter. It is a photograph, Rózia. It is the photograph in the file. It is the end of him."

"Good," said Róża. "Then let it be the end of him, and let him be a man for a night first. It would be a better end than the one they have planned for him, which is a long life in a palace with a stove."

* * *

The village had changed, and the village had not changed.

There were eighty houses now instead of sixty-one, because the state had built twelve in 1952 for the families of the men at the new dairy, and they were square and grey and identical and had slate roofs, and the people in them were called the *nowi*, the new ones, and their children went to a new school with a stove. The old school with no windows had been pulled down and made into a bus stop. The mill was still there and the wheel was still there and the mill was not a mill but a co-operative for the collection of milk, and Stanisław Żurawski had died in 1953 and Ania Żurawska still lived in the house beside the mill with a dog and a hen and a wall of photographs.

The church was not there. There was the wall, and the tower, and inside the walls a wooden platform with an altar on it and a roof of tarred boards and a sign painted on a board at the entrance with the name of the parish and the hour of the Masses. There had been an application for a building permit in 1949, and one in 1952, and one in 1955, and in 1956 the parish had been given permission to build a new church and had collected the money and the plans had gone to the province and the province had required the drainage study, and the drainage study had required the hydrology, and the hydrology was in the hands of an institute in Warsaw that had not responded, and the years had gone by.

Zofia went to see the ruins on the Thursday morning, alone, in her grey coat, with no driver in the car.

She stood in the nave of the church that was not there. The grass grew between the flags. The tower still held its bell, and there was a rope hanging from it, and a swallow's nest under the belfry, and the bell was cracked, and it had been cracked for three hundred years, and they rang it for the Mass and for the dead and for the fire.

She was standing there with her hands at her sides when the priest came out of the sacristy of the temporary church with a bucket.

He was in his shirtsleeves, with his collar and an apron, and he was carrying a bucket and a mop, and he stopped when he saw her, and then he set the bucket down on the flags, and came across the grass.

"Pani director," he said.

"Father."

"The Office does not usually inspect parishes in July."

"The Office is not inspecting the parish." She looked up at the tower. "My sister is dying in the village. I have come to see my sister."

"I know," said Michał. "I am saying the Mass for her on Friday. She has asked for the old rite with the St. Michael prayer, and I have told her that the bishop will not like it, and she has told me that the bishop can come and sing it himself."

Zofia laughed. It was an ugly little laugh, and it went out across the ruins and came back.

"She is the last of us," she said.

"She is the last of the village that was." Michał picked up the bucket. "There is a place where the roof caved in behind the sacristy in 1945 and the wall went with it, and the wall has never been repaired, and the children go through it. The provincial office has said the wall is a monument and may not be repaired without the consent of the conservator. It is a ruin of a church that the Germans burned, and it is a monument, and the conservator has been in Mława since 1952 and has not been to the parish once." He shrugged. "So the children go through the hole, and I have put a board on the other side so they can come through it without the wall falling on them, and the board is a violation of the conservation order, and I have written to the conservator to say so. He has not answered."

"How long have you been here?"

"Since Monday. I came for Róża." He looked at the bucket. "The rector of Mława has the whole deanery and a heart condition, and there is no vicar in Świerczyna since 1956, and I am the only priest within nine miles who can hear a confession in the Pomorze dialect and get it right. I came on Monday and I will stay until she dies, and the bishop has given me the fortnight, and the parish of St. Anne's is being looked after by a professor of philosophy from the seminary who is a very good man and cannot find the sacristy."

They stood in the ruins.

"It has been seven years," said Zofia.

"Seven years."

"There was a letter," she said. "In 1946. It came back to me. I never opened it."

"I know," said Michał. "I wrote it in the camp, in the infirmary, in July 1944, and I sent it to the curia at Mława, and the curate read it to my parents in the kitchen of the mill, and my mother put it in the Bible at the page of the births, and the Bible is in the house by the mill, under the stone in the floor, where it has been since 1944." He set down the bucket. "I did not write again. I had told you not to wait. I had told you in writing, and the writing was the best thing I had ever done, and I thought that a man who has told a woman not to wait must keep his word, because if he breaks it he is not a man who has been generous, he is a man who has been playing."

"You were nineteen."

"I was twenty-four. I was twenty-four and I was in the infirmary and I was weighing forty-one kilos and I had a fever of forty degrees, and I wrote it because I was afraid I would die and leave you with nothing, and because I was too proud to write the other thing, which is: wait for me, I am coming back, and I am afraid that you will not be there." He looked up at the tower. "I have had fourteen years to think of what I should have written. And I did not write it, and it is not your doing, and it is not mine, and the years are gone, and the two of us are standing in the ruins of a church in July 1958, and there is a bucket."

"There is a bucket," said Zofia.

"Will you come to the Mass on Friday?"

"No."

"Then come to the house on Saturday," said Michał, "because she has asked for you, and because there is a thing she wants to say and she will not say it to me, and because my mother will be there, and my mother has asked about you every Christmas for twelve years, and I have had nothing to tell her."

* * *

Ania Żurawska was seventy-two years old and had the shoulders of a woman who had carried two children and a mill and a war, and she was in the kitchen of the house by the mill when Zofia came down the lane on Saturday, and she came to the door and looked at her for a long moment.

"You are thin," she said. "All my life I have been the woman who tells people they are thin. It is the only thing I can still say."

"Pani Aniu."

"Come in. There is a cake."

She had baked a cake. In July 1958, in a village, for a woman of the Office, Ania Żurawska had baked a cake, and she put it on the table with the tea and the sugar and a little plate of butter, and she sat down opposite Zofia and folded her hands and looked at her.

"I want to tell you a thing," she said, "and then we will eat the cake."

"Yes."

"When they took my son in 1943, I went to the church in Mława every day for a year. Every day, in the dark, before the curate was up, I went to the church and I stood in front of the altar of the Mother of God and I said: give him back. Not *let him live.* Give him back. I did not say it as a prayer. I said it as a demand, because I was a woman whose son had been taken in a market square by men who had no right, and I did not have anything else to say to God, and I had been saying it for a year when the curate came to me in the March and said that the note had come through the Red Cross and that the note had his number on it and that the number meant he was in a place where they gave numbers." She lifted her chin. "And I put the number in the Bible and I wrote under it *żyje* — he lives — and I went home and I baked bread. Not because I believed it. Because it was the only thing in the world I could do that day."

"I know," said Zofia.

"You know what it is to have a thing in your hands that is the only thing." Ania nodded. "Then you know why I am going to speak to you as I would speak to a woman in my own kitchen and not to a great lady from Warsaw."

"Pani Aniu, I am the great lady from Warsaw."

"You are the girl who was sick on the ice in 1938," said Ania Żurawska. "I know what you are. I have known what you are since my son came home from the camp in 1945 with his eyes gone, and the first thing he asked for — before the soup, before his father, before the fire — was the chronicle. He asked for the chronicle, and I had it, and I gave it to him, and he sat in the kitchen until the middle of the night with the chronicle in his hands and did not open it. And then he said, *she carried my letters.* Just that. *She carried my letters.* And he went to the seminary in the spring." She put her hands flat on the table. "So I am going to say the thing I have been saying in my heart every Christmas when the parcel came from Warsaw with the blanket and the tea, and the parcel never had a name in it, only the name of the shop. The parcel never said *from Zosia.*"

"It said *from Z. Wierzbicka, Office for Religious Denominations,*" said Zofia. "It was the only paper I had."

"And now you are on the wall of Wacek's mother's kitchen in a photograph, and my son is a rector in Warsaw, and the two of you are in the same city and the same file and the same photograph in the newspaper, and the women of this parish cut it out and say: look, the boy from the mill. And nobody says: look, the girl from the school." Ania stood up. "Do you know why nobody says it?"

"No."

"Because the girl from the school has made herself into a thing that cannot be said," said Ania Żurawska. "You took the thing that was in you and you gave it to the state, and the state put a photograph of its table on the wall, and there is no place in the world for a woman who is a photograph in the newspaper of the state, and I am telling you this in my kitchen in July, and if you do not hear it now you will hear it when the bell rings." She put a hand on Zofia's shoulder. "Eat the cake. It has the last of the good flour from before the war."

* * *

The Mass on Friday was for Róża, and she died on the Saturday morning at ten minutes past five, with the window open and her son asleep on the chair beside her, and the whole village knew before six o'clock because the bell rang.

It rang for a long time. Zofia was in the yard, and the sound came out over the roofs and the mill and the river and the bend, and it went through her chest the way it had when she was six years old and could not have said what it was, and she stood with her hands at her sides and her face lifted and let it ring.

The funeral was on the Tuesday, and it was the largest funeral the village had had in years, because Róża Wierzbicka had been a woman whom everyone in the parish owed something: she had nursed two of the village women in 1947 when the doctor was in Mława and there was no petrol, she had carried food to the old ones in 1946, and in 1953 she had stood up in a meeting in the co-operative and told a man from the party committee that the price of the milk was a sin, and the man had gone back to Mława and written her name in a book, and in the village the book was called the book of the women who cannot be given a job.

Michał said the Mass in the field beyond the mill, because the temporary church held eighty and there were five hundred. There was a platform of boards and a table with the altar, and four tall candles, and the men of the parish had rigged a canvas against the sun, and the bishop of Płock had given permission for the Mass outside the church on the ground that the church was a ruin and the parish had no building that could hold the people, which was true and which had the useful property of being a decision the Office could not appeal, because there was no document.

Zofia stood at the back. There was a woman from the Office at a funeral Mass in the field beyond the mill, and the village knew exactly who she was, and the village had decided, without a meeting, to be polite and nothing more; and the women nodded to her, and the men touched their caps, and the children looked at her the way children look at a woman who has a hired car and a grey coat and no husband.

She had not heard him preach in the open air since 1939.

He spoke about Róża. She had been at the church on Sunday for fifty weeks of the year, and the other two weeks she had been ill; she had sung alto in the choir that was now four women and a boy with a wheeze; she had been the woman who came at six in the morning to light the stove in the sacristy because the sacristan was old and his hands were bad. He said: "She had a temper that was famous in this parish, and she had a tongue that could cut a man's tie without untying it, and she was afraid of nothing except the dark, and she made her son leave the lamp on in the corridor until he was nine years old, and she would not have wanted me to say so."

The village laughed, all at once, and the laugh went across the field like a wind through wheat.

Then he said: "And she was a woman who carried a thing to the end. That is the whole of the Christian life, and it is the whole of the life of this village, and it is the only thing that any of us will be able to say at the last. She carried it. We do not know what it was. Some of us carry a child, and some carry a grudge, and some carry a letter that was never opened, and some carry a photograph in the newspaper of a table in Warsaw. She carried it, and she carried it in this field and this lane and this ruin of a church, and she did not put it down, and now the carrying is over, and the thing she carried is not lost, because she carried it into this field and gave it to us. We are the ones who carry it now. It is a heavy thing. It is the only thing we have."

After the burial, the village came to the house on the Mława road with bread and salt.

That is the custom: the neighbours bring bread and salt to the house of the dead, because the dead have gone to the table where there is no hunger and the living are the ones who need the bread. Wacek's mother had baked six loaves in the night, and the women brought the salt in saucers, and the loaves were set out on the table in the good room with a knife, and the people came in and cut a piece and dipped it in the salt and ate it standing up, and said a word about Róża, and went out through the yard.

Zofia stood at the table all afternoon with the knife in her hand, cutting bread for the village.

She had not done it since she was a girl. It was the woman of the house who cut the bread, and she was the last of the Wierzbickis, and so it was her place, and the village accepted it without a word, and she stood there from noon until six in the black dress she had worn to her mother's funeral and cut the bread and dipped it in the salt and gave it to each of them, and the people came and took it and ate it and looked at her, and some of them said "thank you, pani Zofio" and some of them said nothing, and one old woman took her hand and said, "You are Jan's girl," and went out.

At six o'clock the priest came in.

He was in his cassock with the stole folded in his hand, and he had been at the graveside for two hours while the men filled it in, and the shoulders of the cassock were grey with dust.

He came to the table.

"Pani," he said.

"Father."

"Cut me a piece."

She cut the bread and dipped it in the salt and gave it to him, and he ate it standing, with his eyes on her face, and the room was full of the smell of bread and the sweat of the summer and the flowers that had been on the coffin.

"Bread and salt," he said. "You know what it means?"

"It means welcome."

"It means welcome, and it means the covenant, and it means a stranger who comes into a house and is fed is a stranger who cannot be harmed in it." He took the last of the bread from her hand. "In this parish, in 1943, when the men were taken, the women brought bread and salt to the house of every woman whose man had been taken, and they left it on the step, and they did not knock, because to knock would be to say that the man was dead. And in the morning my mother went out and there were eleven saucers of salt on the step, and a loaf on every saucer, and she brought them in and she put them on the table, and she said: *The village has made a covenant with us.*" He looked at Zofia. "There has been a covenant between the village and my family since the war. And there has been no covenant between the village and yours, because your father was taken and there was no house left for the bread, and your mother went to her mother's, and the village brought the bread to the wrong door."

"Father."

"I am going to the house by the mill now," said Michał, "and my mother will give me supper, and then I am going to sit in the kitchen with the chronicle, because it is the only thing in that house that has not been eaten by the state or the war or the years, and I am going to read the entries from 1937, and if you want to read them with me, the door is open, and there is a covenant."

He went out.

She cut the bread for another hour, and then the people stopped coming, and the room was empty, and Wacek's mother began to clear the table, and Zofia put the knife down and went out of the house and down the lane in the dusk.

* * *

The kitchen of the mill house had a lamp on the table, and the window was open to the river, and Ania Żurawska was asleep in the chair by the stove with her hands in her lap, and Michał was at the table with the chronicle open in front of him and the Bible beside it, and on the Bible was the letter from the camp, unfolded, and the card with the number on it.

Zofia stood in the doorway.

"Sit down," said Michał. "I am reading the entry for the nineteenth of December 1937."

She sat down.

"*Michał fell through the ice by the mill,*" he read, "*and I pulled him out and he would not let go of the bag with the flour in it and it is all ruined. He says he would have let go of the flour before he let go of me. I have written it down so that he can never take it back.*"

He turned the page.

"*Twenty-fifth of December 1938. Zosia and I lay on the ice at the bend and heard the bell. It was only the church bell. The story is a lie. This is a great disappointment to K. Wróbel. We are closing the chronicle for the winter. Nothing else will happen.*"

He turned the page.

"*Thirty-first of August 1939. They have moved the horses out of the mill to Mława. Father says we are to burn the grain in the loft if it comes to it, and Mother says things do not come to it. There is a moon like a knife. Zosia is at the church with the bandages. I am going to the church. I have a thing to say to her and I am going to say it tonight before anything else happens.*"

He closed the chronicle.

"There is nothing after it."

"The planes came at dawn," said Zofia.

"The planes came at dawn, and I did not say the thing, and I have had nineteen years to say it, and I have said it to myself every day in the camp and every day in the seminary and every day in the Office of the Primate and every day in the church of St. Anne, and I have never once said it to you." He put his hand flat on the chronicle. "I said it in a letter, and the letter is there, and the letter says *I am coming back.* It does not say *I love you.* A man of twenty-four in an infirmary does not write *I love you*, because *I love you* is a thing that a man writes when he has a house and a wage and a future, and I had a number and a fever. So I wrote it in the only language I had, which was the chronicle, and I said that in my chronicle you were on the ice at the bend, and that the bell was not the church bell, and I sent it to a girl in the village to read out loud in the kitchen." He looked at her. "And you kept it for fifteen years in a tin without opening it. And I have known that since April, because it is in the file. The Office's file says: *letter of 1946 returned to sender, undelivered, filed by addressee.* The file has your handwriting on the envelope, Zosia. The file has the date."

The lamp hissed. In the chair by the stove, Ania Żurawska slept on.

"Why did you not open it?" he said.

"Because you told me not to wait," said Zofia. "And I had obeyed you. I had obeyed you for twenty-seven years, and I was not going to open a letter that would tell me that the obedience had been right." She looked at the letter on the Bible. "I was a girl who was going to be somebody who decides what happens. And I decided. I decided in 1946, in a room in Warsaw, with a letter in my hand and a Party card in my bag, that the girl on the ice was dead, and that the woman in the room was alive, and that a dead girl does not read her letters. And then I put it in a tin and I kept it, because I was not quite able to put it in the stove." She looked up. "You want to know what I am, Michał. That is what I am. A woman who could not put a letter in a stove."

He reached across the table and took her hand, and this time she did not take it back.

"Tell me the thing," she said.

"The thing?"

"The thing you were going to say on the thirty-first of August. You have had nineteen years. Tell me now."

Michał looked at her across the chronicle.

"I was going to say: Zosia, marry me in the spring. And I was going to say: I have half a mill and a chronicle and forty-one entries in which I came when you were in trouble, and I am going to be a man they look at, but I would rather be a man you look at, and if you will have me I will be at the mill until I die, and if you will not have me I will go to America and be a miller in Chicago and never come back." He smiled, and it was the smile of the boy in the loft. "And I was going to be very dignified about it, and I had rehearsed it in the hayloft, and my hands were sweating."

"You were nineteen and your hands were sweating."

"I was nineteen and my hands were sweating, and the next morning the planes came over, and I never said it, and I have said it in this kitchen tonight in July 1958 to a woman in a black dress who is the deputy director of the Catholic section of the Office for Religious Denominations." He held her hand. "What do you say?"

Zofia sat very still.

"I say that we are in the kitchen of the mill with your mother asleep by the stove," she said, "and that I have a car at Róża's house that I must return to Mława by nine, and that tomorrow there is a boy of twelve who has lost his mother, and that you have the parish of St. Anne's and a bishop who is dying and a Primate who is a wall, and that I have a file with your name in it, page eighty-eight, and the file says that we are *contact material.*"

"I did not ask about the file."

"You did not have to. The file is in the room with us. It is in every room we have been in since January." She took her hand back, and it was the second time, and both times it was slow. "I have decided to be in the room, Michał. Not on the ice. The ice is gone. It went out in the spring of 1939 with the last of the winter and it has never come back, and the girl who lay on it with you is in a tin with a letter, and I am the woman who is left." She stood up. "You will be a bishop. Do you understand what that means? Not the palace. The photograph. If I am with you in any way that can be photographed, you will be the bishop who has a woman in Warsaw, and they will not need to write a single lie. They will take a photograph and put it in a newspaper and let the village draw its own conclusion, and it will be the right conclusion, and it will destroy you, and it will be the truth."

"Then let it be the truth."

"No," said Zofia. "Because if they destroy you with the truth, they destroy the children. The hall will not be built. The stove will not come. The two hundred and thirty-one children will be in the sacristy and the street. And you will be a saint in a photograph, and saints do not build halls." She put on her gloves. "I am going back to Warsaw in the morning, and in the autumn they will come for the Novena, and the only thing that stands between the village and the state is a man in a room with the door shut. I will keep the door shut. That is the covenant. Not bread and salt. The door shut."

She went to the door.

"Zosia."

She stopped.

"The curse," he said. "The widow's words. *May the bell ring for everyone you love before it rings for you.* I have thought about it for nineteen years, and I have decided that it was not a curse at all." He was standing now, with the lamp behind him. "She did not curse me. She told me the truth about my own life, which is that I would be a man who rings for other people. And that is what I have become. And the woman who said it took the milk to our house every Saturday for a year, and when she died, in the spring of this year, I said the Mass, and I asked her forgiveness at the grave, and I forgave her, and I did not hear a bell. Not once. Not in fourteen years." He came around the table. "The only time I have heard the bell was in 1938, on the ice, with you. And every year on the sixth of January, in the church of St. Anne, when the lamp is lit and the whole church is full, I hear it. I do not know where it comes from. I hear it, Zosia."

Zofia stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"Then it is the church bell," she said, "and the story is a lie."

And she went out into the dark.
