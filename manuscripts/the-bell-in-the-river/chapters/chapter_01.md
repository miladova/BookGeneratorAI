# Prologue

*Warsaw, October 1971*

The parcel came in October, eleven years after the funeral of a woman I had not seen since the year I turned eighteen. It was wrapped in brown paper and tied with string knotted twice, the way people who lived through the war tie everything, as if string were still rationed. It came from a notary's office in Mława, a town ninety-odd miles north of Warsaw, about which I knew two things: that my mother was born in a village nearby, and that my godmother had asked to be buried there.

Inside were a photograph, a tin of letters, a leather pouch, a silver lamp wrapped in a horse blanket, and a sealed envelope.

The photograph is the one I keep on my desk. It was taken on the sixth of January, 1958, in the church of St. Anne on Krakowskie Przedmieście, without permission, by a young man in the gallery above the nave with a camera inside his coat. The film was taken off him three weeks later in a cell on Rakowiecka Street; the negative was developed in a laboratory there and filed in a folder with forty-one other photographs of the same morning. The folder is mine now. I will explain how that happened, and why the explanation is not a comfort.

In the photograph, a priest stands in a pulpit of pale stone, his hands on the rim, his face lifted. He is thirty-eight. Below him the nave is full: students in dark coats, a boy holding a violin case between his knees as if it were a dog. At the back, near the door, a woman in a grey coat and a red scarf. She came in late and will leave before the last hymn. She is thirty-eight, an official of the state, and she has spent the morning deciding what to do about the man in the pulpit.

Someone in the laboratory drew a pencil circle around her head. The circle is not crooked. It was drawn by a careful person, a person who had been given a ruler and a name.

Her name was Zofia Wierzbicka. She was my godmother, by proxy — she stood for me in a village churchyard in the powiat of Mława in 1943, because I could not be brought to her and she could not be brought to me, and the Church has allowed that since the missions. The man in the pulpit was Father Michał Żurawski, who baptized me that same year, in secret, in a kitchen in the Old Town, with water from a kettle, and who was ninety miles away from her that winter and did not know that she had done it.

* * *

I was fourteen when I understood that my godmother was someone. It was 1956, the year of the thaw, and she took me to the Bristol for hot chocolate, and in the street outside a man in a black overcoat followed us from Nowy Świat to Bracka. When I asked whether we were being followed she said: *Of course we are, Inka. It's Thursday.* Then she stopped in the snow, took off her glove, and straightened the collar of my coat, and said: *Never be afraid of people who follow you. Be afraid of people who agree with you.*

* * *

I am a translator. My trade is other people's sentences — French and Italian into Polish if the text is long and the money short, and sometimes the other way round for the state press agency, which is how I came to know men who keep folders. For three years I have been learning what my godmother was doing in that church on the sixth of January, 1958. I have the minutes she wrote with her own hand, the carbon of a document that was never filed, and one sentence, added in pencil at the bottom of a page, that has kept me from sleeping for a month.

The sealed envelope is addressed to *ks. Michał Żurawski, biskup, Płock*. It weighs nothing. It is the heaviest thing I own.

* * *

My mother was a nurse, and she had been a girl in a village near Mława who came to Warsaw in 1946 and married a man named Balcerzak and never once went back, and she told me once, drunk, on the anniversary of something she would not name, that in a village with a river and a mill and a wooden church with a leaning tower, a boy and a girl made a promise; then the war came and took them both, and when they met again nineteen years later they stood on opposite sides of a table covered with papers, and neither could put the papers down. When I asked who the boy was, she said: *The bell.*

That is not a name. But in our family, names were like the lights in windows during the blackout: you kept them covered.

* * *

I have the minutes. I have the photograph with the careful circle. What I do not have is the night of the twenty-seventh of December, 1958, when the snow came down on the Mława road and the two of them drove north in a car of the Office. That night I can reconstruct only from a report of a journey in a government file, a letter that was never opened, and the word of a bishop who is still alive and will not talk to me.

Where I have put words in their mouths, I have put only words the documents, or my mother, or my own memory of that decade will support. If my godmother had read this book, she would have corrected my Polish, and then she would have burned it, and then she would have made me a cup of tea.

# Chapter One: The Silver Lamp

*Warsaw, 6 January 1958*

The Church of St. Anne was cold enough to see the congregation breathe.

Zofia stood at the back by the holy water stoup, where the door let in a draught every time someone came in, and watched the nave fill. There were more people than the church was built for; the students stood three deep along the side aisles, stamping their feet quietly. The smell was the smell of every church of her childhood — cold stone, wet wool, candle wax, a ghost of incense in the plaster.

She was not here in her official capacity. She had said so to the driver, who was called Krzysiek, and he had nodded the way you nod at a child's story.

The priest came up into the pulpit after the Gospel. He was tall, and he climbed slowly, as if the stone might not hold him, and stood looking down at the crowd with his hands flat on the rim. His hair was grey at the temples, the way ash is grey on a morning when the fire has gone out.

Zofia's ears began to ring.

She had read the file — twice, at her desk, with the lamp angled so the shine of the paper would not give her away to the woman at the other desk. It said: *Żurawski, Michał, s. Stanisława i Anny, ur. 1919, Świerczyna pow. Mława. Święcenia: 1952. Rektor kościoła akademickiego św. Anny od 1956.* And further down, in the small typed letters of a report: *Czynnik opiniotwórczy w środowisku akademickim. Nastawienie: nieustępliwe w sprawach katechezy.*

She had read the file and she had not known him. The photographs in it were bad: a gaunt man in a black coat crossing a courtyard, his hat pulled down. There were three Żurawskis in the Warsaw telephone book. She had noticed nothing.

Now, in the pulpit, he began to preach, and she understood how an intelligent woman could hold a file in her hands and not know that the man in it was the boy who had put his coat around her shoulders in 1938.

His voice had not changed. The voice had aged the way floorboards age, but underneath it was the same — low, a little hoarse, with a Mazovian softness in the vowels he had tried to file down in the seminary and not entirely managed. He said: "They came from the East, and they asked where the child was born."

Epiphany. Of course. She had chosen the hour because the church would be full and she could be nobody in a crowd, and it had not occurred to her that the sermon would be about the Magi.

"They had a star," he said. "They had a learned tradition, and a caravan, and probably a map. But when they came into Jerusalem, into the city where the wise men were, the star was not enough. They had to ask. *Where is he who is born king?* They had to ask the people who did not want to tell them."

"Because the king they were looking for was not a king anyone in Jerusalem wanted," said the priest. "Herod heard about a child, and he was afraid. Not of the child. Of the child's existence. A single child, lying in the straw, and the whole machinery of the state was afraid of him." He paused. He looked out at the nave, and for a second — a second is a very long time, when a man is looking — his eyes came to the back of the church and stopped.

Zofia did not move. She had learned not to move in rooms where a man with a folder was watching.

"And then they were warned in sleep not to return to Herod, and they went back to their own country by another road. Not by the road they came. By another road. They had brought gold, and frankincense, and myrrh, and they went home the long way, through passes they did not know, because the road they knew belonged to a king who wanted to kill a child."

His voice dropped. "Every one of us has a road he came in by. Every one of us has a house he cannot go back to by the same way. That is not a tragedy. That is the Gospel. It is the only instruction the Holy Family ever gave to the wise: go home, but go home another way."

Zofia left before the offertory. She went down the steps into the cold, and the man who had been standing by the pillar in a grey cap fell in behind her, twenty yards back, and did not catch up.

There was a black Warszawa parked on the curb on the wrong side, where no car could legally park and any car could, in those days, park, if the man who got out of it looked like Krzysiek.

"The Minister wants you at eleven."

"I know what the Minister wants."

"Then get in, I'm freezing."

He drove badly and fast, as all the ministry drivers did. Through the windscreen the city went by in flat white light: the ruins along Marszałkowska, green with frost; the new frontages with their scaffolding; the Palace of Culture with its crown of ice; a queue outside a shop on Krucza that had formed, she knew, at five in the morning.

The Office was a stone building on a side street off Marszałkowska, with a courtyard where the cars were kept and a boarhound called Pastuch who had been a gift from the Ministry of Public Security in 1951 and had bitten a courier in 1952 and was thereafter kept chained to a wall. In the corridor the big portraits hung: Bierut at the top, and below him the men who had come after his portrait learned to hang itself in the spring of 1956. The lower portraits had been rehung so often that the wall behind them was a map of rectangular ghosts.

"Tosia," she said, coming into the outer office. "Has the post come?"

"Twelve letters, a summons from the prosecutor's office about the Silesian proselytes, and his Excellency the Minister's assistant telephoned twice." Tosia Grabska was sixty, had been a teacher before the war, had lost her husband and her son in 1944, and had worked for Zofia for six years, in the course of which she had learned to say *his Excellency the Minister* in a tone that could strip paint. "And the electric kettle has been promised."

"The kettle has been promised since November."

"He has promised it for December. He does not say which year."

Zofia drank the tea, which was made the way they made it in the Office, strong, with a slice of lemon that came from a jar and tasted of the jar, and opened the folder on her desk.

It was marked *Poufne — do wglądu w gabinecie* — confidential, to be read in the office — and it had come from the Ministry of Public Security through the liaison, a Major Stolarczyk, who coughed when he was afraid of a document. Inside were eleven pages. The first was a list.

The Primate had sent a list to Rome in the last week of December, and Rome had not answered and would not for months, because Rome in January 1958 was a dying man at Castel Gandolfo and a Secretariat of State afraid to move. The list had five names. Four were bishops, veterans of the German camps, whose weaknesses the Ministry had catalogued like the answers to a crossword puzzle. The fifth was not a bishop at all:

*k. Michał Żurawski.*

Under it, in pencil, a clerk had written: *najmłodszy od 1816? — sprawdzić.* Youngest since 1816 — check.

Zofia sat with the list in her hand for a long time.

Then she opened the second folder, the one on the parish of St. Anne, signed out of the archive in October for the building permit: a transcript of Father Żurawski's sermon of the first Sunday in Advent, and a carbon of a letter he had written to the Presidium about the catechetical rooms, in which he had used the words *nasze dzieci* — our children — four times.

At the bottom of the third page the clerk had written: *Mówi jak człowiek, nie boli go to, że go słuchają — patrz: ton. Ocena: trudny.* He talks like a man, and it does not hurt him that he is being listened to. Assessment: difficult.

She pressed the intercom.

"Tosia. The rector of St. Anne's has been asking for an appointment about the parish hall. Has he been given one?"

"The twelfth," said Tosia.

"Move him to today."

"Today." Tosia paused, and in the pause Zofia could hear everything Tosia had learned in four occupations and twenty-five years. "At what hour?"

"Three."

"It's half past eleven."

"Then he can come at three."

She spent until three reading the eleven pages twice, and at twenty to three she stood at the window of the corridor, which looked out over the courtyard where Pastuch lay in a wheel rut with his nose in his tail, and watched a man in a black coat come through the gate, stamp the snow off his boots, and touch his hat to the guard.

He came up the stairs slowly. She heard him ask the way at the landing, and heard the soldier of the Internal Security Corps who did the reception answer with the exaggerated politeness he used for priests, an irony that would have been serious in 1952 and was only stupidity in 1958.

She went into her office, shut the door, and sat behind her desk with her hands on the desk where he would see them.

He came in with his hat in his hand. And the thing she had not prepared for, the thing no file had told her, was that the years had not destroyed him; they had narrowed him. He was tall and thin, and the thinness was the thinness of the camp and had never entirely left. His face had been broken at the nose, long ago, and had healed in a way that made it grave rather than ugly. The collar was too big for his neck. His eyes went to her face and stayed there, quiet and unsurprised, and his mouth made the small involuntary movement of a man who has walked into a room expecting a stranger and found the only person in the world.

"Pani director," he said.

"Father." She indicated the chair. "Please."

They sat. The clock on the wall behind her was loud. She had owned it since 1949 and had never before heard it.

"It's about the hall," he said.

"It's about the hall."

"The parish hall at the back of the churchyard. We applied in September, and in October a commission came and said the drainage of the site was not provided for, and the engineer who provides the drainage says the plan of the street precludes the drainage, and the plan of the street was laid down by the Ministry of the Interior in 1952, and the Ministry of the Interior tells me to ask the Ministry of Education, and the Ministry of Education tells me to ask you. Where the chain of command ends, pani director, is where the Catholic Church begins." He set his hat on his knee. "I have two hundred and thirty-one children in catechesis. I have them in the church, in the sacristy, in a room where the parish organist used to live, and in the corridors when it is raining. It has been raining since 1945."

"Two hundred and thirty-one."

"Four came on Monday."

"From what schools?"

He looked at her. "Pani director knows the schools."

She did, better than he did. School No. 29 on Bednarska had been a convent school before the war and had a state pedagogy council that would not permit the use of its premises; the new school on the corner of Dobra had a director who permitted anything that could not be proven. The children came at four, after the shop windows were lit, with their exercise books under their arms, and some were the children of men who had been in the Party since 1944, because in this matter the Party's children were exactly like everyone else's.

"Why have you come to me?" she said. "The Office did not refuse you. The Office asked for the drainage."

"The Office asked three times, and each time for a different reason. The Office has not said yes and the Office has not said no. I have been a priest for six years, pani director. In six years I have learned the difference between a minister who says no and a minister who says *we will see.*"

The word he used was *zobaczymy.* They both heard it hang in the room.

"And what do you think I can do?"

"You can say yes."

"Can I?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's why I'm asking."

She took a sheet of paper and wrote on it, in her neat small hand, a name and a department and a number, and turned it so that it faced him.

"This is the clerk at the Municipal Board who signs for the drainage. Go to him on Monday at nine. The site will be inspected the following Thursday and the drainage will be found to be in order. Your hall will be built."

He looked at the paper. He did not touch it.

"In exchange for what?"

"Does it have to be something?"

"It has always been something."

"Then let me save us both a winter," said Zofia, "and tell you that no one in this building has asked me for anything in your matter. And when no one asks you for anything, Father, it usually means that the thing you want is a hole in the ground, and the question is not what it costs, but who is standing at the edge. I will tell you what it costs, since you insist. It costs me nothing yet. It will cost me later. Everything in this building is paid for later."

"That's a very good answer," he said. "Who taught you to talk like that?"

"Warsaw."

"Ah." He looked at the paper on the desk. "I knew a girl in Warsaw once. She would have answered the same."

Zofia heard the clock.

"Father Żurawski," she said. "You were born in Świerczyna, in the powiat of Mława, the son of Stanisław and Anna, and baptized in the parish church there by Father Ludwik Sokołowski, who died in the prison at Wronki in 1955 — I am telling you this so that you know what I already know, and so that we do not waste each other's time. You were a bell-ringer at that church. You were taken in the roundup at Mława in April 1943, and photographed, and sent to Oświęcim, and you have the number on your forearm, and you keep your left cuff buttoned even in the summer. You had a sister called Jadzia, who died in 1929, and a brother called Tomasz, who was taken to the Reich in 1939 and did not come home. Your father was Stanisław, a miller, who told four men from the Security Service in 1946 that he did not know where his son was, and was telling the truth."

The room was very quiet.

"And what else does the file say?" said Michał.

"It says that in 1938 there was a girl," said Zofia, "and that her name was Zofia Wierzbicka, and that she is sitting on the other side of this desk."

* * *

What happened next took eleven minutes by the clock, and the minutes do not exist, because Zofia did not make any.

He did not stand up. She had planned for him to stand up; she had lain awake the night before and planned for the shouting and for the terrible, necessary dignity of an old friend who walks out of an office. He sat with his hat on his knee and looked at her, and the look had in it neither accusation nor the tenderness she had been afraid of. It was the look of a man reading a document he had been sent at last, and finding it shorter than he had expected.

"Your file," he said. "The one in this building. Has it got a photograph?"

"A photograph from 1952."

"Not of me. Of you."

She did not answer.

"There is a photograph in your file," said Michał. "I have not seen it. But I know it exists, because there is a file on every man and woman in this building, and a file on the files. What does it look like?"

"You know what it looks like."

"I want you to tell me."

"It is a woman of thirty-three in a dark dress with a white collar, standing against a wall with a measuring scale on it," said Zofia. "It is not a flattering photograph. The scale is behind her head. It makes her look as though she has been arrested."

"That is what a photograph is," said Michał. "It is a thing that has been arrested." He turned his hat over. "Zosia. I am going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it as though you were answering it in the confessional, which is the only room in Warsaw where the answer does not go into a file."

"You have no right to ask me in the confessional."

"No," he agreed. "I have no right at all. I am asking as a man who was in the loft of a mill in the summer of 1941 with a girl who told him she was going to be somebody who decides what happens." He looked up. "Did you read my file before you read the list, or after?"

"After," she said.

"Ah."

"I read the list on the eighth of January. It had your name on it. I read the file the same evening, after the office was empty, until three in the morning." She looked at him. "I did not know it was you. That is not a lie and not a defense. I had read your file before, in 1952, at a meeting, and I had not known it was you. I read the name and did not see it."

"But now you have seen it."

"Yes."

"And what are you going to do?"

"That is not a question a man asks a woman in an office," said Zofia, and her voice was level and hard, "and if you ask it again I will have you taken out of the building. Not because you are a priest. Because you have asked me to be a girl in a loft, and I am the deputy director of the Catholic section of the Office for Religious Denominations, and there are eleven people in this building who would like my chair, and one of them is listening at the door."

Michał looked at the door. He did not look away from her face to do it; his eyes moved, and came back.

"Then let us talk about the drainage," he said.

And they did.

He told her about the roof, the tiles, the glazier, the inspector who had not come. She asked the cost of the tiles and he told her, in złoty, without hesitation, and she wrote it down. She asked why the numbers in catechesis had gone up, and he said, "Because in October 1956 I told the parents that anyone who sent a child to the state's ethics class in place of the catechism would answer to me, and the parents believed me, and the state did not."

"That is a threat," said Zofia.

"It is a promise," said Michał.

She wrote that down too.

"In the camp," he said, "the clerk who wrote the report was tired, and the officer who read it was afraid of his superior, and the superior was afraid of Berlin. Give a man one document he can put in front of his superior without being beaten and he will love you, and he will do the thing. That is not a theology. It is a miller's view of the world. The stone grinds whatever is put into it. You cannot stop the stone. You can choose the grain."

Zofia was silent.

"That is what you believe," she said.

"That is what I believe."

"Then we believe the same thing," she said, "and we have believed it for fifteen years, on opposite sides of a door, and neither of us has ever been able to say it out loud to anyone in this city who would not write it down."

He stood at the door with his hand on the handle.

"Friday," she said. "At ten. Bring the inventory."

"Pani director," said Michał, and the title was a wall, and he stood behind it, "the boy in the third row. His name is Piotr. He is fifteen. His father was killed in the war and his mother works at the telephone exchange. On Sunday he asked me whether it was a sin to be afraid."

Zofia did not answer, and he went out.

* * *

She watched him cross the courtyard in the snow. The dog Pastuch lifted his head and did not bark. The gate closed behind him, and the snow went on falling in the flat white light, filling his footprints the way it fills everything in this city, patiently, from above.

Then she opened the door, and Tosia was at the typewriter.

"Tosia. The rector of St. Anne's is coming on Friday. Enter it twice. Once for the drainage. Once for the roof." She stood in the doorway. "Did anyone come to this floor in the last hour?"

"The Minister's assistant, to say his Excellency will be late."

"Anyone else?"

Tosia looked at her over the typewriter with the flat black eyes of a woman who had been a teacher before the war and had lost her husband and her son and had decided, some time in 1945, to be useful in the only way left.

"There is a man at the end of the corridor," Tosia said. "He has been there since two. He has a briefcase, and he has not come in, and he is not from the Ministry, because the Ministry's men cough when they come up the stairs and this one does not."

Zofia went out. The man was at the end of the corridor, by the window, in a good grey coat with the collar turned up.

"Colonel Wesoły," she said.

"Pani director." He turned. He was forty-three, with a broad face and the mild eyes of a schoolmaster, and he had been in the Ministry of Public Security since 1944, and he had a wife and two daughters in Żoliborz, and he played chess on Sundays with a priest from the parish of St. Stanisław Kostka. "I was in the building on other business and I thought I would wait for you."

"You have a briefcase."

"I always have a briefcase."

"What is in it?"

"A photograph," said Wesoły, "which I think you have seen, and a question, which I will ask you on Monday at the Ministry. It is a small question. It is about a student who was in the church on the sixth of January with a camera inside his coat, and who is now in the cells on Rakowiecka, and who has told us a thing about the priest of St. Anne's that I would like to discuss with you before I write it down."

"If it is not written down, it is not evidence."

"Everything is evidence," said Colonel Wesoły. "The only question is who is holding it."

He touched his hat, and walked away down the corridor.

* * *

The flat was in Mokotów, two rooms on the third floor of a building built in 1936 for clerks of the Ministry of Posts and requisitioned in 1945 by the same ministry, which had given her the flat because she had asked in 1951, in writing, with three reasons, of which the third was *służba państwowa* — state service — and the first two had been true.

She had a kitchen table with a good lamp on it, a shelf of books, a photograph of her mother in a frame, and no telephone, because a telephone in the flat was a window that could not be curtained, and being careful had kept her alive.

On the shelf above the stove, on a saucer, stood the lamp.

It was silver, the length of a hand, with a round bowl and a wick and a ring at the top for a chain. It had hung in the parish church at Świerczyna for a hundred and forty years, before the side altar of the Mother of God, and in January 1945, when the retreating Germans fired the church and it burned with the village, her mother — who had been in the village that winter, because Warsaw was a ruin and a woman had to be somewhere — had gone into the burning church and taken the lamp down off its chain and carried it out wrapped in a horse blanket.

Her mother had given it to her in 1946, when Zofia came north for a week, and had said: *It is not ours. When there is a church again, take it back.* Zofia had carried it to Warsaw in a satchel and put it on a shelf, and in 1947 she had bought a bottle of lamp oil, and in 1948 she had lit it for the first time, on the sixth of January, the day of the Epiphany.

The sixth of January was the day the Germans had come to Świerczyna in 1940, and taken the parish silver and the registers and eleven men. It was also the day, in 1938, when the whole village had come to the Epiphany Mass in the cold, with the lamp burning before the side altar, and Zosia had stood at the back with Michał, and he had held her hand inside his coat pocket. She had been carrying that coincidence for thirteen years: the day of the light and the day of the taking, the same square on the calendar.

She lit the lamp.

She put it on the kitchen table and sat down opposite it with a glass of tea and her coat still on, and watched the small flame stand up straight in the cold air of the flat.

It was not a prayer. She had stopped praying in 1944, in the forest, and the stopping had gone on so long it had become a practice. The lamp was not a prayer. The lamp was a bill. Every year on the sixth of January she lit it and let it burn all night, and in the morning she looked at the black ring it left on the ceiling, and she paid something, and the payment was not specified, and no one had ever told her what it was for.

At nine the telephone in the corridor rang — the caretaker's telephone, which pani Kubiak answered and which was, in the building on Górnośląska Street, the village well — and pani Kubiak knocked at the door and said the Minister's office had called, and would she come at eight in the morning, not eleven.

"Thank you, pani Kubiak."

"Cold night."

"Cold night."

She went back to the table. The flame had not moved.

For a long time she sat and looked at it, and at some point after ten she said, out loud, in the empty kitchen, in the voice she used for the Office, the voice that could make a clerk put down his pen:

"No."

The flame burned.

"A bishop," she said. "They want to make him a bishop."

The flame burned, straight and small and steady, with the black ring of the year before still printed on the ceiling above it, and the year before that faintly visible through it, a smudge of smoke on white plaster, the record of a debt paid in the only currency she had left.

She did not turn it down. At midnight she got up and put a glass of water by the bed and checked the lock on the door, and in the morning the flame was still going, and there was a new ring on the ceiling, and she looked at it for a long moment before she put on her coat and went out to save what could be saved.
