# Chapter Nine: The Bell in the River

*Świerczyna, 27–28 December 1958*

They left Warsaw at six in the morning of the twenty-seventh, in a black Warszawa with a driver from the Office, and the snow began at Modlin and did not stop.

Zofia had signed herself back on duty for two days. The paper is in the file: *Wyjazd służbowy: 27–28.12.1958, Świerczyna, pow. Mława. Cel: nadzór nad rozpoczęciem prac przy odbudowie kościoła parafialnego, zgodnie z porozumieniem z 19.12.1958. Środek transportu: samochód służbowy. Kierowca: K. Malinowski.* Official journey, 27–28 December 1958, Świerczyna, powiat Mława. Purpose: supervision of the commencement of works on the reconstruction of the parish church, in accordance with the agreement of 19 December 1958. Means of transport: official car. Driver: K. Malinowski.

There is a second line, in her hand, added at the bottom: *Uwaga: drogi zawiane. Możliwe opóźnienie powrotu.* Note: roads drifted. Possible delay of return.

The driver was Krzysiek Malinowski, who had driven her for four years, and who had a wife in Wola and a boy of nine and a cough he had had since the war. In the front seat beside him was the bishop-elect of Płock, in a black coat and a black hat, with a small suitcase at his feet and his breviary on his knee, and in the back seat, with the report book of the Office on her lap, was the director of the Catholic section.

They drove north on the road to Płońsk with the chains on, and the snow came across the road in long white lines, and the fields on either side were without shape, and the villages went by with their roofs invisible and their windows yellow. At a crossroads a man in a sheepskin was selling something out of a sack, and Krzysiek slowed and rolled down the window and asked the price, and the man said a word that was lost in the wind.

At Płońsk they stopped for petrol and hot water, and Krzysiek went into the office with a chit, and Zofia and Michał stood by the pump in the falling snow with their collars up.

"You are to talk to me today as though I were an official of the Office and you were a bishop," she said.

"I am not a bishop until the twenty-fifth of January."

"You are a bishop in the file. In the file you have been a bishop since the nineteenth of December, when the Minister read your name and said: *this one is going to be trouble.*" She looked at the snow. "The file is the only thing in this country that is always early."

"All right," he said. "Pani director, the question of the drainage of the site of the former church was resolved by the conservator in November. The works will begin with the clearing of the rubble, which is expected to take three weeks, and the foundation stone will be laid in April. The parish has undertaken to provide the labour. The state has undertaken to provide the cement. There is no cement."

"There is never any cement."

"There is no cement," agreed Michał, "and there is a paper in the Office that says there is cement, and the paper is what we have, and I have learned in the room that a paper is a machine for making cement out of nothing, if the paper is signed by the right woman."

Krzysiek came back with the chit and the water, and they drove on.

* * *

They came to Mława at noon. The snow had eased and the sky over the town was the colour of a knife, and they stopped at the presbytery of the parish to leave the bishop's suitcase; he was to sleep there, and the parish priest had been told that the bishop-elect was coming, and had lit the stove in the good room in the morning and had put a bottle of vodka on the table under a napkin.

The parish priest was sixty-eight. He had been the curate at Świerczyna in 1945, and he had read a letter out loud in the kitchen of the mill, and he had told a young woman in a good grey coat that a man in a camp was not issuing orders, he was saying goodbye, and the young woman had told him that he had a great deal to learn about women. He was the only living man besides the two of them who remembered that afternoon.

He looked at Zofia on the steps of the presbytery and his face did something complicated.

"Pani," he said. "It has been thirteen years."

"It has been thirteen years, Father."

"You told me once that I had a great deal to learn about women."

"You did."

"I have learned," said the parish priest of Mława. "I have learned that the woman who told me that was right, and that I was a young fool with a bicycle and a bag of altar linen, and that the letter I read out loud in that kitchen was the best and the worst thing I have ever done." He held the door. "Come in out of the cold. There is soup."

They ate soup in the refectory with the parish priest and his vicar and an old woman who did the cooking, and they talked about the roads and the cement and the bishop of Płock, who was ill, and the twelfth of January, when the schools would reopen without the crucifixes in the classrooms. The vicar — a boy of twenty-six with a country face and a cold — said, "Father, Bishop, is it true that in Warsaw the churches are full?"

"In Warsaw the churches are full," said Michał, "and in Warsaw the offices are full, and the two facts are not as far apart as the papers say."

The parish priest put down his spoon.

"I read the papers," he said. "I read them every day, and every day I am angry, and then I read the diocesan paper, and it is worse, because it is written by us. And I have come to think, in my old age, that the papers are not the point. The point is this table. There are four people at this table, and each of us is a scandal to somebody, and the soup is hot, and the Church is in the room, and the state is in the room, and nobody has written it down." He wiped his mouth. "I am going to say a thing and then I am going to say the blessing and we will go out. In 1945 I told that woman on the steps that a man who is obeyed by the only person he wanted to be wanted by is destroyed. I have thought about it for thirteen years and I have decided that I was right, and that she has been destroyed, and that she has done a great deal of good while being destroyed, and that God in His mercy will put the good in the ledger and leave the destruction to the men who caused it."

Zofia looked at him for a long moment.

"Father," she said, "you have got better."

"I have got older," said the parish priest. "It is the same thing, if a man is lucky."

At two o'clock the car went on to Świerczyna with the bishop-elect and Zofia and Krzysiek, and the parish priest stood on the steps of the presbytery and watched them go, and afterward he told me, in 1969, that he had stood there a long time with his hands in his sleeves and had said a prayer he had not said since 1945.

* * *

The road from Mława to Świerczyna was nine miles and took an hour. The snow had drifted, and twice Krzysiek had to get out with the shovel, and the second time Michał got out with him and the two men dug the car out of a drift in the dark of the afternoon, and the bishop's black coat was white to the shoulder.

They came into the village at three, in the last of the light.

The village was under snow and the smoke went up straight from the chimneys, and in the lanes the men had been out with shovels since morning, and the road to the mill was clear, and the ruts were frozen and rang under the wheels. The birch arches were still up along the lane — the ones that had been put up in October for the picture that came by the highway and did not come through the village — and they had been repaired and re-tied since, and somebody had hung paper stars on them for Christmas, and they stood over the road in the snow like the bones of a summer.

The car stopped in the yard of the mill.

Ania Żurawska was in the doorway with a shawl over her head.

She was seventy-two, and she had buried a husband in 1953 and a daughter in 1929 and a daughter-in-law in July, and she had a son in Warsaw in a black coat, and she stood in the doorway of the mill house and looked at the car and the driver and the woman in the grey coat and her son, and she said, in the voice of a woman who had been a bell-ringer's daughter:

"Well. There is the bishop."

"Mama," said Michał.

"Don't you *mama* me. You are a bishop and I am a miller's widow and I have known it since 1926, when you came down out of that tower and told me that the bell went right through your chest." She came down the step. "And there is the great lady, in her grey coat, in a car. Come in, both of you. The stove is on and there is barley soup and the water is hot, and the great lady is going to take off that coat and sit by the fire and tell me what Warsaw has done to her face."

They went in.

The mill house had two rooms and a kitchen, and the kitchen had the stove and the table and the bench and the wall of photographs, and the photographs had grown since the summer. There was Róża in her wedding dress, and Wacek in his uniform, and Antek at his first communion, and a photograph that had been cut out of a newspaper in 1957 and framed in a frame of cardboard covered with blue paper, and the photograph was of the church of St. Anne in Warsaw with a young priest coming down the steps, and it had been cut out and framed by Ania Żurawska and hung between her husband and her daughter.

Zofia stood in front of it for a long time.

"Take off the coat," said Ania, behind her. "You are not in Warsaw. In this house there is no one who reports, and the driver is in the barn with a glass, and the door is shut, and if the door is shut for an hour in the house of a miller in December it is because the soup is hot and not because there is a conspiracy." She put a bowl on the table. "Sit down and eat, and then we will talk about the bell."

They ate.

The soup was barley and there was a piece of sausage in it and a piece of bread, and the lamp was on the table and the window was black and the stove ticked, and Ania Żurawska ate with them and asked Zofia about her side — "the surgeon was a butcher, I can see it in the way you sit" — and about Warsaw, and about the price of a pair of boots, and about the electric kettle, which had still not come; and she laughed at the kettle and said that in 1938 in this kitchen there had been a kettle that sang.

And then she said: "Now. The bell."

Michał put down his spoon.

"In 1943, in the April," said Ania Żurawska, "when they took the men in Mława, the Germans had begun to requisition the bells. They had taken the bells of Dąbrowa and Grzybowo and the two bells of the parish of Radzymin, and they put them on the train and sent them to Hamburg to be melted for the guns. The old sacristan, Maciej, he was seventy-nine, he had been the sacristan since 1891, he came to my husband in the March and said: they will come for ours. And my husband said: what do we do? And Maciej said: we sink it."

She folded her hands on the table.

"So they sank it. In the millrace, below the wheel, where the channel is deep and the silt is thick. They did it in the night, with the cart and the ropes and eleven men, and they put it down in eight feet of water and mud, and they marked the place with a stone on the bank, and the stone is still there, and my son knows it. And when the Germans came in the June for the bell, the priest — Father Dąbrowski, he was the curate then, he is sixty-eight now and has a wooden leg — he told them that the bell had been taken in 1939 by the Russians, and the Germans looked in the tower and there was no bell, and they beat him and went away." She looked at Michał. "And in the night before they sank it, I took a chisel and a hammer, and I went down to the cart where the bell was lying on its side under a horse blanket, and I cut a number into the bronze."

Michał did not move.

"I cut it into the waist of the bell, where it is thick," said Ania Żurawska, "and I did it in the dark with a chisel, and it took me an hour, and the men held the lantern, and I could not see what I was doing, and Maciej said, *Ania, the letter is crooked,* and I said, *it is a number, it does not have to be straight.* And the number is the number of my son, and it went into the river with the bell, and it has been in the water for fifteen years, and tomorrow the men are going to bring the bell out, and the number is going to come out with it, and I am going to be standing on the bank." She looked up. "That is the thing I wanted to say in this kitchen, and I have said it, and now I am going to bed, because I am seventy-two and tomorrow I am going to stand on a bank in the cold, and a woman of seventy-two must sleep before she stands on a bank."

She got up and went to the door of the next room, and stopped.

"Great lady," she said. "You have a letter in a tin."

Zofia looked at her.

"You had it in your bag in July," said Ania. "I saw the corner of the tin when you put your bag on the bench, and I said nothing, because I am a miller's widow and I know when a thing is being carried. Open it tonight. Not tomorrow. Tomorrow there is a bell and a village and a bishop, and there will be no room in the day for a letter." She went into her room and shut the door.

* * *

The fire in the kitchen stove was banked for the night and the lamp was turned down, and Michał and Zofia sat at the table with the chronicle between them and the tin on the table, and Zofia's hands were in her lap.

"Open it," he said.

"I have kept it for twelve years."

"Open it."

She opened the tin.

Inside were three things: the letter of 1946, in its envelope, with the Office's stamp on the corner and the word *zwrot* — return — in a clerk's hand; a photograph of the village from 1938, which her mother had put in the tin in 1946 and which Zofia had not looked at in twelve years; and a small cardboard box with a lock of hair in it, dark, tied with a thread, which was Róża's, and had been cut on the morning of the funeral by Wacek's mother, because that was what was done.

She took out the letter and opened it.

The paper was thin and had been folded for twelve years, and the fold had worn through in one place, and the handwriting was her own at twenty-seven: firm, upright, and without any of the flourishes that the Office had taught her later.

*Michał.*

*I have heard that you are alive. I am in Warsaw. I have work and a room and a ration card and a good coat. I have been told to tell you that I am not waiting for you, and I have done it, and it is in writing now, and you may keep the paper.*

*But if you come to Warsaw, come to the Office on Krucza Street and ask for Wierzbicka, and I will come down the stairs.*

*Z.*

She read it twice. Then she put it on the table and looked at it.

"Twelve years," she said.

"Twelve years."

"It took me four drafts. The first draft was eight pages. The second was four. The third was two, and it was full of the Party and the future and the necessity of the historical process, and I read it in the morning and I burned it in the stove of my room, and then I wrote this." She touched the paper. "Eight lines. I have written perhaps four thousand documents in my life. This is the only one I have ever written that a man could have used."

"He did not get it."

"He did not get it," said Zofia, "and I have spent twelve years with a letter in a tin, and I have told myself that the not-getting was the tragedy, and it is not. The tragedy is that I wrote it, and that I had understood everything by 1946, and then I took the Party card and the room and the good coat and I put the letter in a tin, and I did the arithmetic. I sat in a room in Warsaw with the letter in my hand and the future in front of me, and I decided that the future was worth more than the letter, and the decision was correct, Michał. That is the thing I have never been able to say to anyone. The decision was correct. If I had come down the stairs in 1946, I would have been the wife of a priest in a parish, and there would have been no hall at St. Anne's, and no agreement, and no twelve parishes, and no school, and no church at Świerczyna, and the children would be in the sacristy and the street, and I would have been happy." She looked at him. "And I would have been no use to anyone."

Michał took her hand.

"Zosia. Tomorrow, at nine in the morning, the men of this parish are going to bring a bell out of a river. It has a number on it that my mother cut with a chisel in the dark in 1943, and the number is mine. And I am going to stand on the bank in a black coat, and there will be eleven men and a tripod and a net, and the whole village will be on the bank, and my mother will be there, and you will be there, in your grey coat, with the report book of the Office in your bag, and the village will see the two of us standing on the same bank." He held her hand. "And I have decided what I am going to do after that, and I am going to tell you now, and you are not going to argue with me, because I have been in the room for two years and I know the arithmetic better than you do."

"Tell me."

"On the twenty-fifth of January I am consecrated bishop. In February the bishop of Płock will die or retire — he has had two strokes and he wants to go — and I will be the ordinary of the diocese, and the Office will have to deal with me for twenty years. And the first thing the Office will do, in March, is open a file on the bishop of Płock and the woman from the Office, because Colonel Wesoły is not a fool and he has been building a file since the spring, and it will have a report of a journey to Świerczyna on the twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth of December, and it will say what it says." He looked at her. "And I am going to beat him to it. On the twenty-seventh of January, two days after the consecration, I am going to write to the Minister and tell him that the bishop of Płock requests that the director of the Catholic section of his Office be transferred from Warsaw to the provincial department in Płock, as the officer responsible for the implementation of the agreement of the nineteenth of December."

Zofia looked at him.

"That is a joke," she said.

"It is not a joke. It is the only move on the board. If you stay in Warsaw, Wesoły makes the file and the Minister has to act and you are finished as an officer and I am finished as a bishop. If you go to Płock as the officer of the Office responsible for the agreement, you are the woman the bishop of Płock has to meet once a quarter in the presence of the starosta, and the file will say so, and the file will be true, and the file will be *boring*. A quarterly meeting in the presence of the starosta is the most boring thing in the world, and I have been in the room long enough to know that a boring file is the only kind of file a man can live in." He put his hands flat on the table. "You will be in Płock, and I will be in Płock, and we will meet four times a year, with the starosta and a secretary and a report, and we will not be alone in a room for twenty years, and we will see each other four times a year, in a room with a window, and the Church and the state will build a school and a church and a hospital together, and the children of the villages will learn the multiplication tables and the catechism, and at the end of twenty years I will be an old bishop and you will be an old woman, and we will have done the work, and it will have been worth it. That is the offer. It is the only offer I have. It is not a life, Zosia. It is a room."

Zofia stood up and went to the window and looked out at the yard and the mill and the snow.

"Płock is a small town," she said. "The Office has a provincial department in Płock with four officers and a typist. The officer responsible for the implementation of the agreement would be a woman of thirty-nine with a scar in her side and a reputation in Warsaw as the best Catholic officer in the service, and she would be sent to a provincial town because a bishop asked for her, and the whole service would know it. I would walk into that department on the first of March and there would be a typist and four officers and they would all know why I was there, and the file would say *boring*, and the file would be a lie, because it would not be boring at all." She turned around. "It would be the most interesting thing in Poland. Four times a year. In the presence of the starosta. For twenty years."

"Yes."

"And in the other twenty years? When you are retired and I am retired and the Office has forgotten us and there is nothing in the file and no starosta and no secretary and no quarterly meeting?"

Michał came and stood beside her at the window.

"Then we will be two old people in a small town," he said, "and I will say the Mass on Sunday, and you will have the garden, and in the winter we will go to the bend and listen to the ice, and the children of the village will come to the door with their exercise books, and there will be no starosta in the room, and I am not going to pretend that I know what that will be like, because I have been a priest for six years and a bishop for six days and I have never in my life been happy for longer than a night."

"And you think I would be happy in Płock?"

"I think you would be in the room," said Michał. "I think you have been in the room since 1944 and you will be in the room until you die, and the only question is which room, and whether there is anyone in it who knows your name."

She stood at the window with her hand on the frame for a long time.

"I am going to say a thing to you," she said at last. "And it is not a thing I have said to anyone, and it is in no memorandum, and you will not put it in a file."

"Say it."

"I do not believe in God. I have not believed since the cellar in 1944. And I am not going to believe. The world is a machine and it has no mercy, and the men who say it has are selling something, and you have been selling it for six years, and you are going to sell it for twenty more, and I have watched you do it and I have loved every minute of it and I have not believed one word." She turned. "And I am going to Płock."

He took her in his arms.

They stood in the kitchen of the mill, in the dark, with the fire banked and the lamp down and the snow on the window, and he held her the way a man holds a woman when he has been alone for seventeen years, and she held him the way a woman holds a man when she has decided, in one movement of the mind, to give up the only thing she has.

They did not kiss. That is the thing I have wondered about for three years, and I asked him in 1970, and he said: "No. We stood in the kitchen for a long time. And then she said, *there is the letter*, and I said, *leave it on the table*, and she left it on the table, and we sat down on the bench with our backs against the warm stove, and we fell asleep. And at half past four in the morning my mother came out of her room and found us, and she put a blanket over the two of us and went back to bed, and she never said a word about it to either of us, ever."

* * *

The men began at eight.

It was still dark, and there were eleven of them and a boy of fourteen, and they came up the lane from the village with the tripod and the winch and the ropes and a net of woven hazel and a boat that had been made in 1939 and had been in a barn since the war. The bank was cleared of snow and the ice was broken with an axe in a place the size of a kitchen table, and the water under the ice was black and slow, and the millrace ran under the ice the way it had always run, and the place was marked with a stone on the bank, and the stone had been there since 1943 and the men knew it.

The whole village came. They came at nine and they stood in the lane and on the mill bridge and on the bank behind the rope, and the children were lifted onto shoulders, and there were three hundred people, and Zofia stood at the back on the bank with the report book of the Office in her bag and did not take it out.

The bishop-elect stood at the edge of the ice in his black coat, with the men, and his mother stood beside him in a black coat and a shawl, and she was seventy-two and had said at breakfast that she was not going to cry and that if anyone saw her cry it was the wind.

It took four hours.

They put down the boat and the tripod over the hole, and the diver was a man from the village named Stefan Wilk, who had been a frogman in the navy in 1948 and had left the navy because of his uncle, and he went into the water in two sweaters and a rope round his waist, and came up three times blue and shaking, and the third time he came up and held up his hand and said, "I have it. I put the rope under the crown. It is lying on its side in the silt, and there is a wheel of a cart on top of it, and an iron hoop, and about a thousand bottles."

They hauled.

The winch was a thing of 1938 from the mill, and the rope was new, and the men turned it in shifts, and the ice creaked, and the whole village was silent the way a village is silent at a harvest when the load is too heavy, and the bishop-elect took his turn at the winch with the men, in his black coat, and his mother watched him with her hands folded.

At a quarter past twelve the bell came out of the water.

It came up through the hole in the ice with the water pouring off it, black and green and streaming, and the mud on it was the mud of fifteen years, and the men swung the tripod and laid it down on the boards on the bank, and the water ran out of it into the snow, and the bell was cracked, and it was beautiful, and it was three hundred years old, and it was the bell of the parish of Świerczyna.

And on the waist of it, in the place where it is thick, in figures a hand and a half high, cut with a chisel in the dark in 1943 by a woman of fifty-seven, there was a number.

*119278.*

The village saw it.

There was a sound from three hundred people at once that was not a cheer and not a cry, and in the language of the Office it was nothing to report: a low sound, and it went across the bank and the mill bridge and the lane and up into the bare birch arches with the paper stars on them, and then the village was silent again.

Ania Żurawska walked out onto the boards and knelt in the snow beside the bell and put her hand on the number.

She stayed there for a moment. Then she got up, with her hand on the shoulder of her son, and she turned to the village and said, in the voice of a woman who had been a bell-ringer's daughter and who at twelve had learned to pull the rope of that bell:

"*On jest nasz. Dzwonić.*"

It is ours. Ring it.

They hung it that afternoon on a beam between two ladders in the field beyond the mill, because the church was not built, and the rope was a rope from the well, and the man who rang it was the old sacristan's grandson, who was nineteen and had never in his life heard it. When he pulled the rope the bell swung, and the crack in it made a sound that was not a pure bell but a bell with a voice, a cracked voice, the voice of a thing that had been under the water for fifteen years, and it rang out across the snow and the village and the river and the fields to the bend and beyond the bend, and it rang twelve times, and then the village stood in the field with their hats off and the children quiet.

And the bishop-elect of Płock stood in the field with his mother on one side and, on the other side, a woman in a grey coat from the Office, and the village saw the two of them standing there, and the village decided, without a meeting, that it had seen what it had seen, and that it would not be written down, and that if it was ever written down it would be written down in the parish.

* * *

They left at four, in the dark, and the snow had begun again.

Krzysiek had the engine running, and the bishop's suitcase was in the boot, and Zofia stood in the yard of the mill with her bag and her report book and her hat in her hand, and the light from the kitchen window lay on the snow.

Ania Żurawska came out with a parcel.

"Bread and salt," she said. "For the road. And a piece of the cake from Christmas, and a jar of the jam. The bread is from the flour of the new mill and it is not as good as the old, and you will eat it in the car and you will not give it to the driver, because the driver is a man and he has a wife and she can bake him his own bread." She put the parcel in Zofia's hands. "Great lady. You are going to Płock."

"I am going to Płock."

"Then I will see you four times a year, at the quarterly meeting, and I will not be at the quarterly meeting, because I am a miller's widow and I do not go to the meetings, and I will see you in the church with the starosta, and I will look at the two of you and I will know." She took Zofia's face in her two hands, and her hands were hard and warm and smelled of bread. "You were a girl who could not put a letter in a stove. I have known it since 1938. It is the only thing in the world that matters, and it is the thing that the state cannot take out of a woman with a chisel." She kissed her on both cheeks. "Go on. The road is bad."

They drove out of the yard and down the lane under the birch arches, and at the turn by the co-operative Zofia turned in her seat and looked back, and the light of the kitchen window was still there, and in it, small and black against the snow, were the two of them: the old woman and the bishop, standing in the yard of the mill.

* * *

The car stopped at the bend.

He asked Krzysiek to stop, and Krzysiek stopped, and the bishop-elect got out and stood on the road in the snow, and after a moment Zofia got out too, and the two of them walked down through the snow to the place where the meadow began.

The river was not frozen. That was the thing. It was the mild winter of 1958, and the Vistula was running grey and open through Warsaw, and the Mławka at the bend was black and quick and steaming in the cold, and there was ice on the millpond and on the flooded meadows, but the river itself was open, and the water went past in the dark with the sound of water going past, and there was no ice to lie on.

They stood on the bank.

"There is no ice," said Zofia.

"There is no ice," said Michał.

"Róża said: take him back to the river in the winter and lie down on the ice with him. And there is no ice, and Róża is dead, and the bell is out of the water, and the number is on it, and in March I will be in Płock in the provincial department with a typist and four officers, and there is no ice."

"No."

She put her hand through his arm.

"There was never a bell in the river," she said. "That is the truth, and I have known it since I was six years old. The bell was in the millrace, and we knew where it was the whole time, and the story of the bell in the river was a story the village told to the children so that the children would believe that a thing that is lost can still ring." She looked at the water. "It is a lie, and it worked. It worked for three hundred years. The children believed it, and the boys who went to the war believed it, and the ones who did not come back believed it, and a woman in a burning church in 1945 took a lamp off a chain because she believed it, and a woman with a chisel in 1943 cut a number into a bell because she believed it, and today there is a bell hanging on two ladders in a field with a number on it, and it rang, Michał. It rang, and it was out of the water, and it rang for the whole village."

"It was the church bell," said Michał.

"It was the church bell," said Zofia. "And it was the bell in the river. And I have spent my whole life trying to decide which one it was, and I have decided, and the answer is: both. That is not a compromise. It is the arithmetic. The bell in the river is the thing that rings when it is not supposed to be able to ring. And a country that has been taken apart and put back together three times in a hundred and fifty years is a country that runs on the bell in the river, and I have been working for fifteen years in a room with a window that does not open, and I have been telling myself that I am the church bell, Michał. That I am the bell that rings on the hour, the reliable one, the one in the tower with the mechanism and the men who keep the mechanism. And I am not." She looked at him. "I am the other one. I have been under the water for fifteen years, and I have rung when I was not supposed to, and the state has a file on me, and the file says *contact material*, and it is the truest thing that has ever been written about me."

He turned and took her hands.

"Zosia. I am going to ask you a question, and it is not the question of 1938, and it is not the question of 1941, and it is not the question in the kitchen of the mill." He held her hands in both of his. "It is the question of a man of thirty-nine who is going to be a bishop in twenty-eight days. Will you come to Płock?"

"Yes."

"And will you be in the room with me for twenty years, four times a year, with the starosta and the secretary and the report?"

"Yes."

"And when you are old, and I am old, and there is no Office and no file and no starosta, will you be in the room with me then?"

"Yes."

"Then there is no curse," said Michał. "There is no curse, and there never was, and the widow at the grave was a woman in her grief, and she took the milk to our house every Saturday for a year, and the bell rang for everyone I loved before it rang for me, and the ones it rang for are dead, and I am alive, and you are alive, and the bell is hanging on two ladders in a field with a number on it, and the number is mine, and it did not ring for me. It rang *with* me. That is the thing I have understood today, on this bank, in a mild winter with no ice. It rang with me."

He kissed her.

They stood on the bank of the Mławka at the bend below the mill, in the snow, in the dark, in the winter of 1958, and they kissed for the first time since August 1941, and the river ran past them, open and black, with the sound of water going past.

* * *

The report of the journey was written in her hand on the evening of the twenty-ninth, in Warsaw, at the desk in her flat, with the lamp on the table beside it burning.

It is four pages long, and three and a half of them are what a report of a journey is: the state of the walls, the tower, the foundations, the rubble, the cement, the plan for the beginning of works in April. On the fourth page there is this:

*"W dniu 28.12 br. dokonano wydobycia dzwonu parafialnego, zatopionego w kanale młyńskim w 1943 r. Dzwon przekazano parafii do czasu zawieszenia w odbudowanym kościele. Obecność: ks. M. Żurawski (nominat), przedstawiciele parafii, ludność miejscowa (ok. 300 osób)."*

*"On 28 December this year the parish bell was recovered, sunk in the mill channel in 1943. The bell was handed to the parish pending its hanging in the rebuilt church. Present: Fr. M. Żurawski (bishop-designate), representatives of the parish, local population (about 300 persons)."*

And at the bottom of the page, under the stamp, in the space for remarks, in a hand that was tired and very small, she wrote one more sentence, and then crossed it out with two lines of the pen, not enough to make it unreadable, enough to make it a thing that a typist would have copied only if she had been careless:

*"Dzwon ma na sobie numer 119278, wybity w 1943 r. przez matkę nominata. Numer pozostawić."*

*"The bell bears the number 119278, cut in 1943 by the bishop-designate's mother. The number to be left as it is."*

The typist was careful. The sentence is in the file.
