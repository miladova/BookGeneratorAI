# Chapter Three: Two Resurrections

*Warsaw, 1946–1952*

In the spring of 1946 Warsaw was a city built out of its own rubble. There were streets where the houses had been taken away to the level of the cellar and the streets themselves were gone, and men crossed them on planks. And there were other streets where the façades had been put back up against the ruins behind them like scenery in a theatre, and you could stand on the pavement and look through a doorway and see, thirty feet in, the sky.

Zofia Wierzbicka came to Warsaw in February 1946 with a Party card, a leather satchel, and a letter of introduction from the provincial committee in Mława to a man named Kosiński in the Department of Education.

She was twenty-six. She had spent a year in Świerczyna after the liberation: buried her father, nursed her mother through the fever that followed, watched the village argue about the wheat. And in April 1945 she had gone to the rectory in Mława and asked the curate, who had been in the forest himself, whether he had heard anything of Michał Żurawski.

The curate had. The Red Cross lists were coming through; a prisoner named Żurawski, number 119278, had been in the infirmary in January, had been on the march west in the snow, and there was a paper that said he had been liberated by the Americans in May.

"Then he is alive."

"Then he is alive," said the curate. "But, pani Zofio, the men who come back from there are not the men who went. I have seen six of them. They come back, and they sit down, and they do not speak for a year. Some of them go to the seminary. Most of them go to the vodka."

She had stood in the rectory with her hat in her hand.

"When he comes back," she said, "tell him I asked."

And the curate said, "I will," and then, because he was a young man who had learned in the forest how to be brave at the wrong moment: "Pani Zofio, he asked you not to wait."

"I know what he asked."

"It was a good letter. But he wrote it in the infirmary of a camp, and men in the infirmary of a camp are not issuing orders, they are saying goodbye. If he comes back and finds that you obeyed him, he will be a man who was obeyed by the only person he wanted to be wanted by, and I do not think there is a word for what that will do to him."

Zofia had looked at him for a long moment.

"You are a very clever young priest," she said, "and you have a great deal to learn about women."

She had gone to Warsaw that spring, and she went back to Świerczyna twice in 1947, and once in 1951 for her mother's funeral, and after that not for a long time.

* * *

The Warsaw she came into was the Warsaw of the Three-Year Plan, of bricks carried by schoolchildren from the piles to the sites, of the slogans painted on the boards that hid the ruins: *CAŁY NARÓD BUDUJE SWOJĄ STOLICĘ* — the whole nation builds its capital. It was a city in which a man who had been in the Home Army learned to cross the street when he saw two men in leather coats, and in which the men in leather coats were also, sometimes, the men who had been in the Home Army.

The Party in 1946 was not yet the Party of 1952. There were arguments in the cells; the old Socialists were still in it, or being pushed out of it, or discovering week by week what they had joined. Zofia had joined in the forest in 1942, not for the ideology but for the rifles, and in 1946 she transferred her card to Warsaw and discovered, in the Department of Education, that a young woman who had been in the forest, who could write a clean Polish sentence, and who did not flinch when a colonel entered the room, was a woman for whom the state had uses.

Her first post was in the Department of Schools and Curricula, and her job was to remove from the textbooks the things that had been in them in 1938. It did not feel, then, like the work of a censor. It felt like the work of a builder. The old textbooks had a drawing of Marshal Piłsudski on the first page and a prayer at the end of the reader; a chapter on the Polish colonies in Africa that was a lie; a paragraph about the Jews that she read with a cold face and marked for deletion with a pencil so hard the point broke. She was not taking anything away from the children. She was taking away the lies. She was twenty-seven, she had been in the forest with a rifle, and she believed, with the whole of her considerable intelligence, that a country could be taught to be good, and that she was one of the teachers.

There was a photograph of her from that time. I have it. She is standing at a table in a room with three men and a map of the new school districts, and she is the only woman, and she is not smiling, and she is pointing at the map with a pencil, and the men are looking not at the map but at her.

* * *

Her office in 1946 was on the third floor of a building on Hoża Street, and it had a window that gave onto a courtyard with a chestnut tree and a pump. Zofia's desk faced the window. On the second day she moved it so that it faced the door.

Her superior was a man of fifty named Wiktor Kosiński, who had been a teacher in Lublin before the war and had joined the Party in 1944 with the Soviet-backed committee, and who was in 1946 a sincere and frightened man. He taught her the trade: that a memorandum should have three parts, that the third part should be the recommendation and should be one sentence, that a minister reads only the recommendation, and that a recommendation that is right and one that is wrong are read with the same eyes, and therefore the sentence must be written as though it had been decided by God.

He also taught her what he called *the arithmetic*. "Every decision has a bill," he said. "You will be told that this is cynicism. It is not cynicism. It is bookkeeping. The men who do not count the bill are not better men than us; they are men who have not read the file. Count the bill, write it in a memorandum, and then decide whether the country can pay. That is the whole of government."

"And if the bill is a man?"

"Then write the man's name in the memorandum," said Kosiński. "Never write *a number of persons*. If the man is worth the price, his name will make the argument stronger. If the name makes the argument weaker, then you have learned something about the price."

She learned it. She was a good clerk, and then a very good one, and then, in 1949, when the Office for Religious Denominations was being organized and someone in the Presidium asked for a woman who could write, who knew the provinces, and who had a Party card from the forest and not from the university, Kosiński sent her file across the river with a note in his own hand: *Intelligent. Reliable. No family. Recommend.*

She went.

* * *

The Office in 1949 had four rooms and eleven staff and a mandate that could be read in two ways. It was created to *regulate the relations between the state and the religious denominations* — which meant, in the language of the time, to see that the churches did what the state had decided they would do.

Her section was the Catholic section, the only one that mattered. The other denominations had their own files and own tragedies and came through the Office in a slow unhappy trickle. The Catholic Church had fifteen thousand priests, eight thousand churches, and the loyalty of most of the population, and it could not be administered. It could only be managed.

She learned to manage it. The geography of the episcopate: which bishop was ill, which was brave, which was brave on Sundays and careful on Mondays. The language of the concordat that did not exist, of the decree of 1945 that had never been repealed, of the *porozumienie* of 1950, which both sides had understood as a truce and neither had intended to keep. She learned that a permit for a church roof could be used to obtain a report on a priest's sermon, and a report on a priest's sermon could be used to obtain a permit for a roof, and that the machine ran on this exchange, and that a person could grease the machine and be crushed by it in the same afternoon, at the same desk.

* * *

The 1950s came down on the Church like a shutter.

In 1949 the state began the process the newspapers called the *normalization* and the priests called the *persecution*: the confiscation of the Caritas charities, the closing of the Catholic University's faculties, the show trials of priests, the creation of the so-called patriotic priests, the men who would sign anything and whom the Office kept in a building on Wilcza Street with a telephone and a car. In 1953 the Primate, Stefan Wyszyński, was arrested at his own desk in the middle of the night and taken to a monastery at Stoczek, and then to Komańcza, in the mountains, where he was held three years, and where he wrote the text of the Great Novena on a typewriter smuggled in to him inside a crate of books.

Zofia was not in the room when the Primate was arrested. She was twenty miles from it, in a building on Krucza Street, taking notes at a meeting about the arrest of the Primate. She wrote: *The Primate's attitude is uncompromising. Recommend the matter be handled at the level of the Office of the Council of Ministers. Recommend no publicity. The Church will respond with silence and the silence will be reported abroad.*

She read it over. It was correct. It was the memorandum of a competent official who understood the machine. She did not sleep that night, and in the morning she went to work.

* * *

What happened to her in those years is hard to write, and I am going to write it the way she wrote it, in a document I found in the archive of the Office in 1968. It is not a diary; she did not keep a diary. It is a single sheet of paper, typed, with no heading, in a folder marked *Materiały nieaktowe* — non-current materials — which is where a clerk puts the papers he cannot bear to destroy and cannot bear to file.

It says:

> *The question is not whether the Church is right. The question is whether I am the woman who can do this work.*
>
> *In 1944, in the forest, I believed that the future of Poland was a question of who would build the schools. I still believe it. The children of Świerczyna were taught in a room without windows by a man who was told to teach them that the Vistula was a German river. The state that tore down that room and built the school on Bednarska is my state, and I am not ashamed of it, and I will not pretend to be.*
>
> *What I did not know in 1944 is that a state which builds schools must also decide what is taught in them, and that the decision is made by men who have never been in a school. I did not know that the men would be afraid. I did not know that fear is the actual material of government. Every memorandum I write is a door, and behind every door is a colonel who is afraid of a bishop, and behind the colonel is a minister who is afraid of the Presidium, and behind the minister is a man in Moscow who is afraid of nothing and knows nothing.*
>
> *I did not know that the machine would run on the fear, and that I would learn to feed it, and that I would be good at it.*
>
> *There is no God. That is not a tragedy; it is a relief. If there were a God, I would have to explain to Him what I did in 1949 with the file of Father Sokołowski, who is in Wronki, and who is dying, and whose file I read and did not appeal, because the appeal would have cost me the section, and the section is the only place from which I can do anything at all.*
>
> *I can do something. That is the whole of my faith. I can do something. I can keep the men who are worse than me out of the rooms where the decisions are made, and I can make the decisions that remain less bad than they would have been. Some days I do. Most days I do not. I have not been to confession since 1942, and I have not been to Mass since 1945, and I have not said a prayer since the night my father was shot, and none of that is because I do not believe. It is because I do believe. There is a God, and He is not on this earth, and the men who say He is are selling tickets.*
>
> *M. is alive. He is in a seminary in Kraków, or he was in 1948, and he will be a priest, and he has not written to me, and I have not written to him, and there is nothing to write. If I met him now, he would ask me what I have done, and I would tell him, and he would not be able to answer, and the not answering would be the end of it. That is why I do not write. That is the only reason. All the other reasons are the reasons a woman gives a colonel.*

The sheet is unsigned. It was typed on an Office machine with a broken *l* that I have seen in a hundred other documents.

I have read it perhaps forty times. The thing I keep returning to is not the confession. It is the sentence: *I can do something.* It is the sentence of a woman who has decided to save what she can save, and who knows the decision is not a decision but a daily practice, and who will spend the rest of her life doing it, and will be made to pay for it in the end.

* * *

Michał Żurawski was liberated in Austria in May 1945, weighing forty-four kilos, with a document that said he was a Polish citizen and a number on his arm.

He was in the American zone. There were officers who came through the displaced persons camps with lists, and a Pole in a British uniform who asked whether he wanted to go to England, and a man from the Second Corps who asked whether he wanted to go to Italy, and in September an American officer with a Polish name and a kind face who asked whether he wanted to go to America.

"No," said Michał.

"You understand what you are going back to?"

"Yes."

"There is no Poland," said the captain, who had been born in Chicago and had a photograph in his wallet of a street in Poznań he had never seen. "There is a Soviet province with a Polish flag. They will put you in prison if you say what you think. You have been in one camp; do you want to be in another?"

Michał looked at him.

"I want to go home," he said, "and I want to be buried in the ground I was born on. Everything else is a detail."

He came back in November 1945, through Czechoslovakia, on a train that took eleven days and stopped for a day outside Katowice because a bridge had been rebuilt by hand. He walked up from the station at Mława in December, in the dark, because the cart that was to meet him had been commandeered for the milk, and came into the mill kitchen at eight o'clock at night with snow in his hair, and his mother was standing at the stove with a pot in her hand, and his father was at the table with the accounts book, and no one said anything for a long time.

Then his mother put down the pot, and said: "You are thin."

"I am thin."

"You are alive."

"I am alive."

And Stanisław Żurawski, who was a taciturn man and a miller, and who had spent the war carrying grain in a cart with a false bottom, stood up from the table and went out into the yard and stood in the snow for a quarter of an hour, and came back with his face washed and said, "Sit down. Your mother has made soup."

That winter was the worst in living memory. There was no grain and no coal and the wheel was frozen, and in January the family ate potatoes and a soup made from the bones of a pig given to them by a neighbour who had been given it by a cousin at the Ministry of Agriculture. Michał weighed forty-four kilos in November, fifty-one in January, fifty-four in March; he had the cough half the men of his generation had; in the evenings he sat by the lamp and did not speak.

His mother, who had been a bell-ringer's daughter, watched him for six weeks.

Then one evening in February she sat down opposite him with the mending, and said, "You are going to be a priest."

Michał did not look up from the fire.

"I have been thinking about it," he said.

"I know what you have been thinking about, because I have been watching you think about it, and you have the face of a man who is going to do a thing and is afraid to say it in case he is not allowed." She put the mending down. "You are going to be a priest. There is a seminary in Kraków that takes men from the camps. You wrote Father Leon's name in the Bible."

"Mother."

"I am not asking you. I am telling you what you are going to do. Because in this house we have had one son taken to the Reich and one son come back, and the son who came back is not the son who went. The son who went was a miller. The son who came back has been in a place where they took everything a man has, and he has come back with a thing they could not take, and the thing is not for the mill."

Michał looked at her for a long time.

"And what is the thing?" he said.

"God," said his mother, "and the habit of getting up in the morning."

He laughed. It was the first time he had laughed since 1943, and it was a terrible laugh, a laugh with something broken in it, and it lasted a long time, and when it was over he was crying, and his mother held him, and the fire went down, and in the morning he went to Mława to see the curate about the seminary.

* * *

He was ordained in Kraków in June 1952, in the cathedral on Wawel Hill, in a ceremony that began at six in the morning because the authorities had refused permission for a public ordination and the bishop had decided that a private one at dawn was better than nothing.

There were nine men ordained that morning. Eight of them were in their twenties, from the seminaries, and one was thirty-two, with grey in his hair and a number on his arm, and when the bishop laid his hands on that one's head, the bishop closed his eyes and held them there a moment longer than he held them on the others, and there was a sound in the congregation, a low sound, and it was not a cough.

His parents came. Stanisław wore a black suit that had belonged to the parish organist, and Ania wore a hat that her daughter-in-law had made her from a pattern in a magazine, and they sat in the fourth row and did not hold hands, because the village did not, and when it was over and the new priests came down the aisle the old miller stood up and said, in a voice that carried to the back of the cathedral:

"*Bóg zapłać.*"

God repay. The old peasant thanks. And the priests laughed and the bishop laughed and the bishop's secretary, a young man who had never in his life been in a village, was seen to wipe his eye.

* * *

Zofia was in Warsaw on the day of the ordination, in the Office on Krucza Street, in a meeting with the Ministry of Public Security about the seminary in Kraków.

The file lay on the table in front of her: a thin file with a photograph clipped to the inside cover, a man of thirty-two in a black suit with his head turned away, and a typed page that said *Żurawski, Michał*, with the date and place of birth, the seminary, the date of the ordination, and a note at the bottom: *Wg doniesienia: wyjątkowo oddany Prymasowi. Przeszłość obozowa — opinia środowiska wysoka. Rokowania: twardy. Klasyfikacja: B.*

Classification B was the classification for a priest who was not yet dangerous enough to arrest and too dangerous to leave alone.

The colonel asked her a question about the seminary's intake.

She gave the answer.

The meeting moved on to the next item, and she wrote the minutes in her small neat hand, and at the end she carried the file back to the registrar, who stamped it and filed it among four thousand other files, and for six years nobody looked at it again.

* * *

The two resurrections came within a month of each other: she was made chief of the Catholic section in May 1952, and he was ordained in June 1952. Neither of them knew. The letters he had written to her in 1946 had gone to the rectory at Świerczyna, where the curate put them in a drawer and then, in 1948, when the rectory was searched and the drawer opened, into the stove, to save her name. The letter she had written to him in 1946 had gone to the Red Cross in Kraków, had never found him, and was returned to sender in 1947, and she had never opened it.

It was returned to sender. She had never opened it. It is in the parcel on my desk, with the tin and the pouch and the sealed envelope, and it is a letter from a woman of twenty-seven to a man in a displaced persons camp, and it is eight lines long, and the eight lines have defeated me for six weeks.

I have opened it. I opened it in October, after the funeral, with a knife, at my kitchen table, in the middle of the night, and I am not sorry.

It says:

*Michał.*

*I have heard that you are alive. I am in Warsaw. I have work and a room and a ration card and a good coat. I have been told to tell you that I am not waiting for you, and I have done it, and it is in writing now, and you may keep the paper.*

*But if you come to Warsaw, come to the Office on Krucza Street and ask for Wierzbicka, and I will come down the stairs.*

*Z.*

He never came. She never knew whether he had received it.

He had not received it. It came back in 1947 to the Office, to the address on the envelope, and was put in the tray of undeliverable post; a clerk, seeing the name, sent it up to the section, and the section read the name and sent it back down, and it was in a folder in her desk when she cleared it in 1956, and she took it home in her bag, and it has been in the tin ever since.

That is the whole of the debt, and this is the arithmetic: two people, both alive, both in the same city since 1946, both in the same file since 1952, and neither knocked on the door, because each of them knew the bill.
