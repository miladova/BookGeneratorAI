# The Bell in the River — Working Plan

**Working title (locked):** *The Bell in the River*
**Author (byline):** M. L. Wrona
**Form:** Historical novel, prologue + 10 chapters. Target was ~4,000 words a chapter; the
drafted book runs ~62,000 words, which is the form it wants to be. Length is settled; no
further cutting.
**Setting:** Warsaw, January–December 1958, with the rural parish of Świerczyna (powiat Mława, Mazowsze) in memory and in a spring journey.
**Language:** English. Polish terms carry a one-line gloss, as reviewers do in translated fiction.

---

## The Spine

In 1939 two children from a Mazovian village, **Zofia (Zosia) Wierzbicka** and
**Michał (Miś) Żurawski**, love each other completely. The war and its aftermath put
them on opposite sides of everything: she becomes a high official of the communist
state (Urząd do Spraw Wyznań, the Office for Religious Denominations); he becomes a
priest and the youngest candidate for a bishopric in modern Polish history. The novel
tracks one year, 1958, approaching a decision that will end in a schism inside the
Church and disappear one of them forever.

The story is told by **Inka**, a woman (born 1942, of age in 1963) narrating in 1971.

---

## Characters

**Zofia Wierzbicka** — b. 1919, Świerczyna. Deputy director of the Department of
Religious Denominations (Wydział Wyznań), Urząd do Spraw Wyznań, Warsaw. Grew up
the devout altar girl of St. Bartholomew's; her father Jan was a church organist and
PPS (Polish Socialist Party) village teacher. In 1940 Jan was denounced and shot;
Zofia joined the Communist Party (PPR) in 1942, at nineteen, as an act of revenge
and famine; she believed she was fighting the men who killed him. Over fifteen years
she built a competence nobody can dismiss — she speaks five languages, drafts every
agreement herself — and she spends her moral capital on a single private sentence
hidden in official documents: *Chapter 5, Article 2 (b): for the cultural needs of the
parish.* She is among the few people the Church genuinely respects, which is her
greatest usefulness to the state. Her grief is not for God; it is for the girl who
believed.

**Michał Żurawski** — b. 1919, Świerczyna. "The boy who came down from the tower."
Belfry bell-ringer, then smuggler of children's letters, then a teenage courier of the
Home Army. In 1943, at twenty-three, he was taken in a village roundup and sent to
Auschwitz (no name to his past there, only a number). He survived the Death March and
was liberated; after the war he entered the seminary and was ordained in 1952. As
**Father Michał**, rector of St. Anne's (the academic church, Krakowskie Przedmieście)
since 1956, he has become the priest who negotiates with the government better than
any bishop — a man who has learned, in the camp, that the state cannot be defeated
from the outside, and who will soon decide it can be met from the inside, at a price.
He thinks he has always been the selfish one; every survivor's ledger, in his mind,
has him in the red.

**Stefan** — b. 1905, Warsaw. Cardinal-Primate. Not a saint but the novel's necessary
force: he holds the Church together through 1958. He names Michał his candidate, and
asks the impossible: that Michał become, in secret, the head of a negotiation the
government can never admit exists.

**Inka** — b. 1942, Warsaw (St. Anne's parish), narrates in 1971. Her mother **Jadwiga
Balcerzak**, b. 1916, raised Inka alone; a nurse. Inka's godmother, from her first
communion in 1949, was Zofia Wierzbicka. She remembers Zofia ("Ciocia") in 1949
smiling at a first-communion photograph in a way her mother called "crying without
crying." The novel is her attempt to find out who her godmother was, and what happened
to her in the winter of 1958.

**Róża** — b. 1917, Świerczyna; Zofia's elder sister by two years. Quick, funny, able to
haggle a trader into tears. Married Wacek (d. 1952, killed by a falling tree in the state
forest); her son **Antek** b. 1946. Ran the family house after the war. In 1958 she is
forty-one and looks fifty-nine: tuberculosis, a lung gone, dying in the good bed in the
good room on the Mława road. Died 26 July 1958; buried from the field on 29 July with
bread and salt and five hundred people. She is the ground where both of their loyalties
give way.

**Colonel Wesoły** — b. 1915. Ministry of Public Security, Department IV (the Church
file). Zofia's shadow for years; not a cartoon villain. He believes what he does is
necessary because he watched his own family be murdered by men who also prayed. He
will eventually be the one whose surveillance logs expose the whole thing.

**Jerzy Sztachelski** — historical figure: minister without portfolio and head of the
Urząd do Spraw Wyznań (1956–1961). Zofia's titular boss; pragmatic, capable of both
sociability and menace; scenes with him are invented but anchored in the record.

## Structure — ten chapters + prologue (as drafted)

- **Prologue (Warsaw, October 1971)** — Inka's frame: the parcel from a notary in Mława,
  the photograph with the pencil circle, the tin, the sealed envelope, the lamp.
- **I. The Silver Lamp** — 6 Jan 1958, Warsaw. Epiphany sermon at St. Anne's; the list of
  five names with *najmłodszy od 1816? — sprawdzić* in pencil; the drainage scene and the
  two hundred and thirty-one children; Wesoły in the corridor with his briefcase; the
  lamp lit on the anniversary of the taking, and the ring of smoke on the ceiling.
- **II. The Chronicle** — Świerczyna, 1926–1945. The tower, the chronicle, the village; the
  vigil of the Holy Family (Dec 1938); the vow on the ice at the bend (24 Dec 1938); the
  widow's words at a child's grave (Easter 1939); the war, the letters, the roundup at
  Mława (19 Apr 1943), number 119278; the camp letter and the curate who read it aloud.
- **III. Two Resurrections** — Warsaw, 1946–1952. Her career, Kosiński's arithmetic, the
  Primate's arrest; the sheet of paper in *Materiały nieaktowe*; his return and his
  mother's order ("God, and the habit of getting up in the morning"); the ordination of
  15 June 1952 and the meeting about the seminary on the same day; the returned letter.
- **IV. The Stone and the Rope** — Kraków, Nowa Huta, Warsaw, 1948–1957. The seminary at
  twenty-six; the Corpus Christi sermon and the Primate's message from Komańcza; Nowa
  Huta; the arrest of March 1954 (eleven days, Pomorska Street); the chancery under
  Okońska; the thaw; why he never looked for her.
- **V. What Sztachelski Wants** — Feb–Apr 1958. The window that did not open; the Novena
  as "the whole problem"; the Rzepa file and the protocol of 14 Feb; the Bristol; the
  arrest of 19 April and the memorandum that blocks the transfer; *B-a-b-a* in pencil.
- **VI. Bread and Salt** — Świerczyna, July 1958. Róża's death; the field funeral and the
  bread and salt; the chronicle read in the mill kitchen; "marry me in the spring, 1939";
  the covenant of the shut door; the Bible under the floor stone.
- **VII. The Picture** — Aug–Dec 1958. The fourth of August and the cross in the drawer;
  the picture that turned at the cross and went by the road; the field Mass of nine
  hundred; Kielce, 9 Nov ("two frightened institutions"); the Primate's rebuke (15 Dec);
  the Świerczyna agreement of 19 Dec and its clause six in his own hand.
- **VIII. The Commission** — Sept–Dec 1958. The Commission of Three and the twelve
  parishes; Rzepa signs; the death of Pius XII and the election of John XXIII; the
  illness moved to November (operation, 24 Nov); Klimek's three documents; the visit in
  the ward; the invitation to Świerczyna; the protocol on a cardinal's hat.
- **IX. The Bell in the River** — 27–28 Dec 1958. The drive north and the journey report;
  the presbytery at Mława; the tin opened in the mill kitchen ("the decision was
  correct… I would have been no use to anyone"); the offer of Płock ("It is a room");
  the bell raised at 12.15 by Stefan Wilk, the chiselled 119278, *On jest nasz. Dzwonić.*;
  the bend with no ice; "both. That is the arithmetic"; the report crossed out.
- **X. The Red Hat** — Jan 1959 – Oct 1971. The sacristy photograph with 119278 on the
  back; the protocol about a hat; Sztachelski's millennium speech and "I do not care…
  the state will not survive the loss of the bridge"; the consecration of 25 Jan, three
  thousand people and the litany; *Bóg zapłać, pani director*; "From the fourth of
  March…"; the transfer and the nineteen quarterly meetings at Płock; Zofia's death at
  Otwock (14 Oct 1960) and burial outside the family plot; Klimek closing the file
  ("Strike the matter from the register"); the bishop in 1971 — "There was no adultery…
  The rest was the room"; and Inka's coda, with the envelope opened at two in the
  morning and three of its four sentences printed.

## Historical anchors (verified)

- Urząd do Spraw Wyznań existed 1950–1989; Jerzy Sztachelski headed it 1956–1961 (as
  minister without portfolio). Its oversight of religious denominations is documented.
- The Wisła was not fully frozen in the 1958–59 winter; but 1958 autumn was wet and
  the river high — the river ice that matters in the book is in the bellfry/old
  millchannel, not the main channel.
- The Great Novena (Wielka Nowenna) formally opened 3 May 1957; the pilgrimage copy of
  the Black Madonna began touring parishes in 1957. In 1958 the regime moved against
  the Novena, with the July 1958 letter of the PZPR Secretariat to regional party
  committees and with the stopping of permits to build churches.
- 4 August 1958: the Minister of Education's circular on the secular school (removal of
  crucifixes and religious emblems, religion moved to the end of the day) — the
  "Night of the Fourth of August."
- 24 September 1958: Gomułka's "1,000 schools for the millennium" speech at the XII
  Plenum.
- 9 October 1958: Pius XII dies. Conclave 25–28 October; Roncalli is elected John XXIII
  (crowned 4 November). 17 November: a consistory is announced for 15 December
  (twenty-three cardinals) and held. Wyszyński is NOT among them: he was created
  cardinal by Pius XII on 12 January 1953, was refused a passport, and received the red
  hat from the Pope's own hands on his ad limina visit of May 1957. The Office's file,
  in this book, still says the matter is open — which is the joke of Chapter Ten.
- 30 November 1957: the bishops' letter on religious education read in churches.

## Craft constraints (self-imposed)

1. No framework clichés: no "a testament to," "tapestry," "shivers," "little did they
   know," no moralizing final paragraphs.
2. Every chapter ends on an image, not a lesson.
3. Dialogue carries the plot; Polish words always earn their place.
4. The 1971 narrator (Inka) has a reason to know what she knows; nothing is presented
   as verifiable fact she could not have.
5. The novel refuses to decide whether faith or the Party was right. It decides only
   who was loved, and what the bills were.

## Production

- each chapter written in `chapters/chapter_NN.md` (one H2 title + plain prose; `*  *  *`
  scene breaks; blockquotes reserved for found documents).
- `scripts/build_manuscript.py` compiles DOCX + EPUB with `generator/docx_compiler.py`
  and `tools/epub_compiler.py` and writes a word-count report.
- Front matter: title page, epigraph (Norwid, from a published source), the note on
  names and money, and a short historical note is placed at the back.
