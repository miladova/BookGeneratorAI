# The Door Between Houses

## Book I: The Last Summer of Empires

### Chapter One

Warsaw, 4 August 1915

The first thing the Russians took from the Radecki house was the clock.

Not the gilt French clock in the blue drawing room, which had been silent for seven years, nor the regulator in the servants’ hall, whose pendulum had acquired a limp. They took the station clock from the hall table: a square mahogany thing with a cracked glass face, given to Count Kazimierz by a railway director who had since been arrested, released, and arrested again. Two soldiers came for it shortly after noon. They wrapped it in a horse blanket as if it were a wounded child and carried it out between them.

“Is it government property?” Helena asked the officer in the doorway.

“Everything useful is government property now, miss.”

He had a narrow blond moustache and a silver tooth. He looked at her as if she had asked whether the sun belonged to the tsar.

Behind Helena, the clock’s empty square of dust showed on the table. Beyond the open door, Warsaw made a sound she had never heard before: not the usual braid of tram bells, cart wheels, vendors, and arguments, but a city trying to speak with its mouth full. A wagon rattled past with mattresses tied to its roof. Somewhere down Aleje Ujazdowskie a woman called a child’s name over and over, each time more quietly. The streetcars still ran, though their windows were crowded with faces turned toward the Vistula.

“My father has the receipt,” Helena said.

The officer’s smile came and went. “Then he should keep it safe. There will be many things to prove after the war.”

He went down the steps. The soldiers followed with the clock.

Helena watched them turn toward the avenue. When she looked back, her father was standing at the top of the staircase, one hand on the carved banister. Count Kazimierz had dressed for dinner at half past twelve: black jacket, stiff collar, a silver watch chain, though nobody had yet decided whether there would be dinner.

“Papa, they’ve taken the clock.”

“I heard.”

“You could have stopped them.”

“I could have made a scene.”

“Would that have been worse?”

He examined the bare patch on the table. “It would have been more expensive.”

He went back upstairs.

The answer irritated her because it was almost a joke and not quite. In the last year, her father had become an expert at the almost. Almost angry. Almost frightened. Almost willing to admit that the country they had spent three generations pretending to own had never belonged to them at all.

At the street door, their butler, Stefan, was arguing with a second soldier over the Radeckis’ carriage horses. Helena went down to help, although she knew her presence would only make Stefan more formal and the soldier more stubborn. Before she reached the landing, the bell rang.

The woman who stood outside wore a dove-grey travelling coat over a summer dress and held a black umbrella though the sky was clear. She was small, handsome in the way of a knife, and composed enough to make composure seem an accusation.

“Miss Berman,” Helena said.

“Miss Radecka.” Zofia Berman glanced past her into the hall. “I’m sorry. I would have sent a card, but our footman has disappeared with my mother’s hatbox and both our cards are in it.”

“Has he gone to Russia?”

“He says he has gone to fetch the hatbox. My mother says he has gone to Russia. My brother says he has gone to the German army. My father says he has gone to the devil, which is the only destination they agree upon.”

The old familiarity in the exchange was a trap. Helena felt herself stepping into it before she could decide whether she wanted to. Their mothers had arranged for the girls to share French lessons at twelve, then discovered that the girls were better at teaching each other bad habits. By sixteen, they had perfected the art of quarrelling without giving the governess a reason to separate them. At twenty-two, they no longer had a governess. They had acquired better reasons.

“What do you need?” Helena asked.

“Your garden gate.”

“Of course.”

“Only for a minute.”

“That is what you said last time.”

“Last time I needed a ladder.”

“You needed a ladder because you had climbed the wall and lost a shoe.”

“I had not lost it. I had thrown it at your brother.”

“He deserved it.”

“Most certainly.” Zofia’s eyes shifted to the second soldier at the foot of the stairs. “May I?”

Helena stepped aside. Zofia came in, trailing a breath of rainless air and some perfume Helena remembered from before the war. The memory was so intact it seemed indecent. For a moment, the house was not being emptied by men with orders; it was a July afternoon in the old garden, when Zofia had stolen a peach and Helena had insisted it was not ripe, and they had eaten it anyway over the wall.

“Papa is upstairs,” Helena said. “The officers are taking the horses.”

“I know. They came to us first.”

“And your father let them?”

“My father explained that a horse is a machine with feelings, and they explained that a machine with feelings is still a machine. Then he offered to sell them the stable and they asked whether he had a permit.”

A shout rose from the street. Stefan swore in a low voice. Zofia turned toward the window.

“There’s a man in our yard,” she said. “He came through the gate from your side. He’s carrying a parcel. My brother says it’s a dead cat. I think it’s a person.”

“Why do you think it’s a person?”

“Because it moved.”

They went through the pantry and out into the narrow passage between the two properties. A service door in the garden wall stood open, its iron latch polished bright by decades of use. The gate was not meant for families. It had been cut into the wall by a Radecki ancestor who wanted the gardener to wheel manure through without crossing the front drive. The Bermans had bought the neighbouring house twenty years later and, in the first of many small disputes, insisted the gate had been built on their side. Neither family had been able to prove it. Both had used it ever since.

On the Berman side, a figure lay beneath the grape trellis.

Zofia was right. It was a man. He had one arm tucked across his chest and the other stretched toward the garden path. His clothes were dusty, his face so pale that the dark streak along his temple looked painted on. A canvas parcel rested by his hip.

“Is he alive?” Helena asked.

The man opened his eyes. “Regrettably.”

Zofia crouched. “What happened?”

“An argument with a cart.”

“You lost?”

“On points.” He tried to sit up and hissed through his teeth.

Helena knew him: not well, but enough. Janek, a clerk from her father’s estate office. He had a shy smile, an unreliable tie, and the habit of bringing the count documents no one wanted to sign. For three years he had been learning to type at a speed that made the estate steward call him a machine. He was twenty or twenty-one, though he had the long, unfinished look of a boy.

“Janek. What are you doing here?”

He glanced at the parcel. “I was told to bring this to the house.”

“Which house?” Zofia asked.

He looked at the wall, then at her. “I wasn’t told.”

“Who told you?”

“A man at the station.”

“Which station?”

“Wiedeńska.”

“The station is full of men,” Helena said. “You’ll have to be more exact.”

“A man with a red scarf.”

Zofia’s mouth tightened. “Did he give you a name?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you what was in the parcel?”

“No.”

“Did you open it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it was tied.”

It was an answer so simple it became suspicious. Helena picked up the parcel. It was heavier than its size suggested. A strip of twine crossed the brown paper twice, knotted with a sailor’s care. She could feel a flat, hard edge through the wrapping.

“Don’t,” Janek said.

“Don’t what?”

“Don’t take it inside.”

Zofia looked at him. “You just said you were told to bring it to the house.”

“I was told to bring it to a house.”

“Which house?”

“I told you. I wasn’t told.”

“You have a remarkable gift for answering questions without answering them.”

“I have a remarkable headache.”

A whistle sounded beyond the wall. Not a train whistle. A sharp, short note, answered by another from the street. Helena’s shoulders stiffened before she could stop them. All morning the city had been full of whistles: policemen, railway men, soldiers. Each one seemed to announce a new rule, and each rule had been made to expire by the next one.

Zofia stood. “We need to get him off the path.”

“We should get a doctor.”

“My father’s doctor is at the factory. Your family doctor is in Łódź, according to the last letter.”

“He’s not our family doctor. He’s my mother’s cousin.”

“Then he may be more useful.”

“Not if he’s in Łódź.”

“Do you know anyone else?”

Helena did: a surgeon from the hospital near the Ujazdowski barracks, a woman who had once stitched her brother’s eyebrow after a riding accident and had called him an idiot while doing it. But the barracks were already being emptied, and the hospital corridors were crowded with men whose uniforms had been cut away. She had heard the surgeon was taking a cart east with the last of the wounded.

“Not quickly,” Helena said.

“Then we’ll have to manage.” Zofia bent and slid one arm behind Janek’s shoulders. “Come on. Your cart has won.”

Janek gave a breath that might have been laughter. “You’re not strong enough.”

“I am strong enough to resent you.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No, but it helps.”

Helena put the parcel under her arm and took his other side. They carried him through the gate into the Radecki kitchen, where the cook was packing copper pots in newspaper and Stefan was attempting to prevent the soldiers from taking a mare with a foal. The mare had already decided whose argument she preferred. She kicked the stall door hard enough to make the soldiers step back.

“Put him in the scullery,” Helena said.

“Not the scullery,” Zofia said. “There’s a better mattress in our morning room.”

“We are not carrying him back through the garden.”

“Then we’ll use the passage.”

“That’s what the passage is for.”

“Precisely.”

They took him into the Berman house through the servants’ entrance. The contrast between the two houses had always struck Helena as a kind of argument made in plaster. The Radecki townhouse was full of portraits whose eyes followed the family through rooms nobody used. The Berman house had been rebuilt in stages, and its rooms had the bright, practical openness of somewhere people were expected to work. There were books on the stair landing, a telephone on a small table, and a bronze horse that looked as if it had arrived from a shop rather than a family crypt.

At fourteen, Helena had once asked Zofia why there were no portraits of the Bermans’ ancestors. Zofia had said there were portraits, but they were in albums, where the people in them could be shut away when company came. Helena had laughed. Zofia had not. The next week she had brought Helena a photograph of her grandfather, a bearded man in a narrow black coat, and told her the story of how he had arrived in Warsaw with two shirts, a borrowed prayer book, and a mind that could calculate interest faster than the bank. Helena had asked if that was meant to impress her. Zofia had said no; it was meant to make her stop confusing a portrait with a history.

Helena had thought of that sentence more than once since. It had not improved her manners.

In the morning room, Róża Berman stood beside a trunk with a strip of linen tied around her sleeve. She had gray threaded through her dark hair and an expression that made it plain she had already dismissed three people and was considering a fourth.

“Zofia,” she said. Then she looked at Helena, at Janek, at the blood on his collar, and at the package. “You’ve brought me a puzzle.”

“We found him in the garden,” Zofia said.

“Which garden?”

“Yours.”

“Then he is your puzzle.”

“He came through our gate,” Helena said.

Róża’s gaze settled on the parcel. “That makes it mine.”

She spoke Polish with the careful precision of a woman who had learned that a room would listen for the wrong syllable. Her English was better than either girl’s. Her Russian was better than most officers’. She had once made a magistrate apologize to a seamstress in three languages and then charged him for the thread.

“Can we use the telephone?” Helena asked.

“If the line still works.” Róża took a small key from her waist and opened a cabinet. “Zofia, fetch water. Helena, hold the lamp. I’ll see to his head.”

“You know how?”

“I have lived with men.”

Janek stirred. “That sounds worse than the wound.”

Róża pressed a cloth to his temple. “It is.”

While Zofia went to the kitchen, Helena set the parcel on a chair. She could hear the distant thud of something heavy being moved in the street, then a rumble that travelled through the floorboards. A cart, she told herself. A city did not shake from one cart.

The telephone receiver gave a dead hush. Róża tried the hook twice, then spoke into it with the calm authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed by machinery. Nothing answered.

“Someone cut the wire,” Helena said.

“Or the exchange has gone.” Róża hung up. “There are two doctors within walking distance. One has gone to the station. The other is hiding from his landlord.”

“Is that a joke?”

“It is Warsaw.”

Zofia returned with water and a basin. The three women worked without discussing the parcel. Janek’s wound was not deep, though his face had taken on the stillness that comes after pain has used up its first supply of noise. Róża cleaned the blood from his hair. Zofia held the basin. Helena supported his shoulder. He did not thank them, which she liked better than if he had.

When he could sit, Róża gave him a spoonful of brandy. “Now tell us why you came.”

“To deliver the package.”

“To which house?”

“I don’t know.”

“You are either very honest or very foolish.”

“I’ve been told both.”

“By whom?”

Janek shut his eyes. “A man at the station.”

“The man with the red scarf?” Zofia asked.

He opened one eye. “Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“He said if I carried it to the house on the avenue, I would be paid.”

“Which avenue?”

“This one.”

“Which house on this avenue?”

“He said I’d know.”

Helena laughed once, without humour. “There are hundreds.”

“He said I’d know,” Janek repeated. “Then he told me not to let a soldier take it.”

Róża folded the cloth and laid it on the table. “That is a better answer.”

“Why?” Helena asked.

“Because someone who says ‘you will know’ is either trusting your judgment or testing your obedience.” Róża glanced at the parcel. “Neither is a reason to open it.”

A knock came from the front of the house. All four of them looked toward the door. It came again, more urgently.

Zofia went to the hall. Through the open doorway Helena saw Marek Berman crossing from the front room, his shirt sleeves rolled to the elbow and his collar unbuttoned. He was twenty-six, tall and dark, with a crease between his brows that had been there since the war began. The first time Helena had met him, he had been holding a glass of lemonade and pretending not to understand a joke about his father’s new motorcar. She had thought him stiff. Later she had thought him proud. Now she wondered if the two were merely what people called a man when they did not know what he was afraid of.

He listened at the door before opening it. A boy stood on the step, breathless, carrying a folded sheet of paper. Marek took it, read the first line, and closed the door.

“What is it?” Zofia asked.

“Father wants us at the factory.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

“Why?”

“Because the Russians have given the railway workshops until sunset to hand over the machinery they can move. The Germans may reach the city tomorrow. Father says we have to decide what to leave and what to break.”

Róża’s face did not change. “He should have decided last week.”

“He did. The foreman has changed his mind.”

“Then the foreman should have changed it last week.”

“Mother—”

“If a machine is going to be taken, let it be taken.”

“They’ll use it.”

“They’ll use it whether it works or not.”

“They won’t use it if it’s in pieces.”

“And how many men will be in the room when you break it?”

Marek did not answer. He looked at the bandage on Janek’s head, then at the parcel. His expression shifted by a degree. Helena saw it because she had once been good at reading him, or had believed she was.

“Where did that come from?” he asked.

“The garden,” said Zofia.

“Which garden?”

“Yours.”

“Then why is it in here?”

“Because Helena brought it.”

“Helena should put it down.”

“It is down,” she said. “You might look before you issue orders.”

His gaze met hers. “I’m sorry.”

The apology was so quick and unadorned that it caught her by surprise.

“Did you send for Janek?” she asked.

“No.”

“Do you know what’s in the package?”

“No.”

“Did you expect it?”

“No.”

“Is there a reason you’re looking at it as if it has just insulted you?”

“I’ve had a difficult morning.”

“You and everyone else.”

A second knock sounded at the door. This time, no one moved. The caller tried the handle.

Marek crossed the hall and put one hand against the door. He did not open it. “Who is there?”

“Police.”

“Which police?” Zofia whispered.

“Apparently we’re about to learn,” Helena said.

A man outside called in Russian, then in German. Marek answered in Polish that the house was occupied. The voice outside demanded entrance. Róża, still holding the bloodied cloth, looked at her son.

“Don’t be a hero,” she said.

“I’m not.”

“Good. Heroes are very expensive.”

He opened the door only as far as the chain allowed. On the step stood a Russian policeman, his cap askew, and a second man in a railway uniform. The policeman spoke quickly. Marek listened, then replied. The railway man tried to peer past his shoulder.

“Tell them we have a wounded clerk,” Róża said.

Marek did. The policeman’s eyes travelled into the hall. Helena saw the man notice her, then the open corridor, then the closed parlor door beyond. His gaze lingered on the house as if he were trying to remember the arrangement of its rooms.

He asked another question.

Marek’s face was blank. “He asks if we have seen a man carrying a parcel.”

Zofia’s fingers tightened around the basin. Janek had gone very still.

“What did you tell him?” Helena asked.

“That we have seen many men carrying parcels.”

The policeman spoke again, louder. His hand rested on the leather strap crossing his chest. The railway man said something that made him glance back toward the street. Beyond them a whistle sounded, followed by a burst of voices and the clatter of boots moving at a run.

For one suspended second, nobody in the house seemed to breathe.

Then the policeman pulled his hand from the strap, stepped backward, and shouted for the railway man to follow. They went down the steps. Marek closed the door and slid the bolt.

For a moment he kept his palm flat against the wood. The tendons stood out beneath his rolled sleeve. Helena remembered his hands as a boy’s: ink-stained, impatient, always turning some small object over as if it might reveal a second purpose. He had once dismantled her music box to find the tune inside it, then spent the rest of the summer trying to put the tune back. His father had made him apologize. His mother had asked Helena whether she wanted the box repaired or the boy punished. Helena had said both, and Róża had smiled as though she had passed an examination.

The house held the sounds of the city at a distance: wheels, feet, an order shouted and repeated. A moment later the kitchen door banged in the draft. Everyone flinched except Róża, who set down the bloodied cloth and smoothed the linen at her cuff.

“Is the factory still running?” Helena asked.

“Not in any sense that would satisfy an accountant,” Marek said. “The engines are running. The men are there. We have no idea whether there will be coal tomorrow, or whether the rail line will be open, or whose flag will be over the gate when the next shift arrives.”

“Your father thinks it can be saved?”

“My father thinks anything can be saved if a person is willing to pay the price.”

“And you?”

“I think people say ‘the price’ when they mean someone else must pay it.”

It was not what Helena expected from him. She had been prepared for an argument about his father, or a defence of the factory, or some polished declaration about Polish industry. Instead he looked tired enough to be honest.

“Does your father know you think that?”

“He is the one who taught me.”

Zofia made a small sound. “That is not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Róża glanced at the door. “Then keep it. Answers are expensive today.”

“Did he believe you?” Zofia asked.

“I don’t know.”

“What happens now?” Helena asked.

Marek looked toward the parcel. “We find out what the man at the station wanted.”

“No,” said Róża. “We find out whether it is safe to keep.”

Janek pushed himself upright. The room tilted around him; he caught the chair and stayed there, jaw clenched.

“I can take it back,” he said.

“To the station?” Helena asked. “It’s full of soldiers.”

“Then I can leave it somewhere.”

“You’ve already been hit by a cart,” Zofia said. “Let us not ask the cart to finish the conversation.”

“Maybe it was a carriage.”

“Do you remember?”

“No.”

“Then it could have been an elephant. You shouldn’t go near those either.”

A laugh escaped Helena before she could stop it. It sounded strange in the room, almost like a mistake. Marek looked at her, and for a moment the years between them seemed no wider than the passage in the garden wall.

Róża wrapped the parcel in a clean towel. “No one opens it until we know who sent it.”

“And how do we find that out?” Helena asked.

“We ask the person who delivered it.”

“He doesn’t know.”

“Then we ask the person who told him.”

“He wore a red scarf,” said Zofia.

Róża’s eyes lifted to her daughter. “That is not a name.”

“It is more than we had before.”

“Not much more.”

“It’s enough to begin.”

“Beginnings,” Róża said, “are what people call trouble before it has learned your address.”

From the street came the low groan of a departing tram. The building gave a small, deliberate shiver. In the hall, the telephone rang once—one bright, impossible note—then stopped.

No one moved toward it.

Helena looked from Zofia to Marek, from the towel-wrapped parcel to the open passage beyond the kitchen. On the other side of the garden wall, her father was deciding what a man could afford to lose and still call himself the owner of his own house. Here, the Bermans were doing the same thing in a language of ledgers, machines, and names spoken carefully.

Outside, a whistle answered from the south.

The city had begun to empty, and already it was leaving things behind.
