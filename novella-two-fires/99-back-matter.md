## A Note on History and Invention

The world of this novella is real; the people in it are not.

Between 1945 and 1989 the Polish state managed its churches through an Office for Religious Affairs, which vetted candidates for bishoprics, licensed catechists, approved or refused the building and closure of churches, and maintained, in cooperation with the Security Service, files on priests. The Church's own position in those years was not simple: the 1950 agreement between the Episcopate and the state conceded a great deal, and the Primate, Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński, was detained from 1953 to 1956. The confrontation over the Millennium — the Church's Great Novena and the state's counter-celebration of a thousand years of Polish statehood — was gathering through the late 1950s, and the campaign of "patriotic priests" and loyalty declarations was a real instrument of that struggle. The Warsaw Rising of 1944, the pacification of villages in the Kielce region, the deportations to forced labour in Germany, and the long, quiet war over the catechism in a flat on a back staircase are all drawn from the historical record.

Everything else — Wierzbica as it appears here, the Kędzierskis, the Zarembas, the parish of St Kazimierz in Żoliborz, Colonel Rygier, the see of Sandomierz and the men proposed for it — is invented. No character in this book is a portrait of a real person. Where the historical record was ambiguous, I have preferred the version that best served the truth of the lives rather than the truth of the file.

The Polish is left in the Polish. A few words, for the reader who wants them: *pani* (madam, Mrs), *panna* (miss), *ksiądz* / *ojciec* (Father), *towarzyszka* (Comrade, fem.), *powiat* (district), *voivodeship* (the largest regional administrative unit), *dwór* (the manor house), *gimnazjum* (academic secondary school), *złoty* (the currency), *śliwowica* (plum brandy).

## A Note on the Two Fires

The proverb in the epigraph is not in any collection. It was put into the mouth of a woman who could neither read nor write, and who taught her daughter the faith by the only method she had, which was to say the same true thing every day of her life until it wore a groove in the child. It is the kind of thing such a woman would have said. That is how folk sayings begin, and it is also, I have come to think, how novels begin.
