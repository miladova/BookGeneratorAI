# Chapter Two — Two Fires

## Wierzbica, 1938

The village lay in a fold of the hills like something set down and forgotten. There was one road through it, rutted, white in summer, and the road crossed the river at the mill and went up past the church and out the other side toward Kielce, eighteen kilometres away, which was as far as most people in Wierzbica had ever been and further than some of them thought advisable. The houses were timber and clay, thatched with straw gone black at the ridge, and behind them the fields went up in strips of rye and potato and oats, and behind the fields there was pine wood, and behind the wood there was nothing at all, only the hills going on and on into the haze.

Her mother said a village had two fires. There was the fire in the hearth, which was the family, and there was the fire in the church, which was God, and a woman's life was the business of carrying a coal from one to the other without dropping it.

Marianna Kędzierska said this in the same tone in which she said that the milk would turn before Thursday, so it was some years before her daughter understood that she had been given a catechism.

Zofia was sixteen in the summer of 1938 and she was, by the standards of the village, spoiled. She had been to school for seven years, to the end of the seventh class, which was two years more than her brother Antek and four more than most of the girls. She could read fast, and write without thinking about it, and she had once come second in a district examination in Kielce in front of an inspector from the Curatorium who had said, in front of her father, that this one should go on to the gimnazjum. Her father had said nothing all the way home and then, at the gate, had said, *And what would I pay with, the potatoes?* and gone in to his supper. Zofia had stood at the gate for a while, and then had gone in and eaten her supper, and had never mentioned it again, and had thought about it every day for two years.

She was not beautiful in the way the girls in the magazines in the manor house were beautiful. She was brown from the sun, and her hands were a labourer's hands, and she had a chipped front tooth from a fight with Antek when she was nine. But she had a face that people turned to look at, because there was something in it that was already going somewhere. Father Radoń, who had baptized her, said she had the devil's own energy and it would either make her a saint or take her to Warsaw.

The church of St Bartholomew had been built in 1683 by a man whose name was carved above the door in letters worn to shadows. It was stone, with a roof of shingle and a tower with one bell that had been recast in 1796, and inside it was dark and cool and smelled of cold stone and old incense and the wet wool of sixty people. On the left of the altar there was a painting of the Mother of God with a gold crown and a face of such ordinary sadness that Zofia had grown up believing she was a relation.

She sang in the choir. She had a voice that was too loud and not quite pure, and she loved it more than she loved anything except one thing, and on the feast of Corpus Christi she walked at the front of the procession in a white dress with a wreath of rue on her head, and the whole village watched her, and she knew it, and she looked for one face in the crowd and found it and looked away.

---

The organist's son had come home for the summer.

Wiktor Zaremba had been the organist at St Bartholomew's for thirty years, a small, dry, exact man who kept bees and tuned the piano in the manor house and could read any music put in front of him. His wife Aniela sewed for the manor and for the wives of Kielce and had a mouth on her that she kept closed in front of her husband. Their son Aleksander was seventeen, and had been at the gimnazjum in Kielce for four years, boarding with his mother's sister above a shoemaker's shop, and he came home at Christmas and Easter and for the whole of the summer, and in the summer he was everywhere.

He was tall and had not grown into his height. He had his mother's grey eyes and his father's hands, and he laughed at things before other people had noticed they were funny, which either made him the best company in the village or an idiot, depending on who you asked. He could play the organ, badly, and the piano, well, and he had a black notebook in which he wrote things down — poems, he said, though when Zofia had stolen it and read it, it was mostly lists, and one poem about a horse, and a page on which he had written her name eleven times in different sizes.

She had known him since she was four. She had disliked him until she was eleven, when he had pulled her out of the river by the hair and she had hit him in the mouth, and after that they had been friends in the way of children who have decided, without discussion, that they are on the same side of something.

In the summer of 1938, they were no longer children, and the whole village knew it before either of them did.

It was the hay. That was where it happened, or where it finished happening. The meadow by the river belongs to the four families who have rights in it, and in the last week of June they cut it together, men and women in a line with scythes and rakes, and the smell of cut grass in that heat was so strong it made your eyes water. Zofia worked with the rakes and the pitchforks, and Olek worked with the men, badly, because a boy who has spent four years in a gimnazjum in Kielce has a scholar's back, and Antek mocked him for it ceaselessly and with love.

On the third day, in the afternoon, when the heat was a hand pressed flat on the back of your neck, she went down to the river to drink. The water was low and warm at the edges and cold in the middle, and she knelt and drank from her hands and then sat back on her heels.

Olek came down the bank and knelt beside her and drank, and then wiped his mouth on his sleeve and said, "There's a heron."

"Where?"

"There." He pointed, and she looked, and there was no heron.

When she turned back he was looking at her, and he did not look away, and she felt the whole summer turn over like a stone in her chest.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Look at me like that." Her voice came out strange. "Somebody will see."

"Nobody can see. There's nobody—"

"There's always somebody."

He was quiet. Then he said, "Zosia."

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said my name," she said, "in that voice," and got up and went back up the bank with her heart going like a bird in a sack, and worked the rest of the afternoon without looking at him once, and felt every inch of the distance between them like a wire pulled tight.

That evening they finished the last of the hay and stacked it, and the men opened a bottle and the women brought out bread and dripping and sour milk, and they ate sitting on the ground in the dark while the bats came out. Old Szymon the miller told a story about a priest and a goose that Zofia had heard nine times and laughed at anyway. Antek fell asleep with his head on his arms. Her mother sat with her back against the haycart, mending by touch, the needle going in and out in the firelight.

Zofia got up and walked away from the fire, down toward the water, because she had seen him get up a quarter of an hour before and go that way.

He was under the willow. It was an old tree, a white willow, split near the base into two trunks and leaning out over the water, and in the dark it looked like a woman bending down to wash her hair. He was sitting on the root with his knees up.

"I thought you'd come," he said.

"I thought you'd be here," she said, and sat down beside him, and neither of them said anything for a long moment. The river made its small continuous sound. Up the hill a dog was barking at something that had already gone.

"Zosia," he said. "I'm going to say it badly."

"Then say it."

"I've been in love with you since I was eleven and you hit me in the mouth."

"That was because you pulled my hair."

"I know. I'm telling you the chronology." He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "And in September I have to go back to Kielce and I'll be there until June and I can't — I can't sit in that room above the shoemaker's with your name in my notebook and not know whether—"

She kissed him.

She had thought about it for a year, and had constructed in her mind a hundred ways it would be, and it was none of them. It was clumsy and his nose was in the way and she was shaking, and when they broke apart he said, "Oh," in a stunned voice, like a boy who has been hit on the head, and she started to laugh and then to cry, and he put his arms round her and held her, and the willow moved above them in the small wind.

"I'll wait," she said, into his shoulder. "I'll wait until June and then I'll wait some more."

---

They had eleven months.

She would think about those eleven months for the rest of her life, and what she would remember was not the war, which had not yet come, but the ordinary things, which were the things the war would take. She remembered that he could not whistle. She remembered that he was afraid of the dark in a way he was ashamed of, and that when they walked back from the church at night he talked continuously and too loudly so that neither of them would notice. She remembered his hands on the piano in the organ loft, the way he played with his head down and his lips moving. She remembered the smell of beeswax in his father's workshop, and the taste of the honeycomb he stole for her from the hives, and the way he said her name — *Zosia* — with the accent on the first syllable, so that it sounded like a question that had been answered.

She remembered the letters. He went back to Kielce in September and wrote to her twice a week, and the letters came to the post office in the mill and Szymon's wife kept them behind the counter, and Zofia walked down to fetch them and read them in the wood where nobody could see her face. He wrote about the Latin class and the mathematics teacher who had a glass eye and the manifesto that a boy in the fourth form had been expelled for. He wrote about the Germans, more and more, as the winter went on. In March he wrote: *They have taken Austria and nobody did anything. Last week they took a piece of Lithuania and nobody did anything. Zosia, I think we are living in a country that is being got ready.*

She wrote back. She wrote badly; she had never written a letter that was not a list; and she wrote the same things every time, which was that she missed him, and that the sow had farrowed, and that her mother had a pain in her side, and that she had been thinking about the willow. And then she would add, at the end, a sentence that took her an hour: *Everything is the same here and I am the same and I will be the same in June.*

In October she went to Kielce.

She had never been. She had been to Mniów and to the market at Strawczyn and once, at the age of nine, to a fair at Jędrzejów, and the town was a thing she knew from the front of a postcard. She went with her father's cousin's cart as far as Mniów and then by bus, and she carried a basket of eggs to sell and a clean blouse in a paper parcel, and she was so frightened on the bus that she could not feel her hands.

The gimnazjum was a long yellow building with a courtyard and a gate with iron letters over it. She stood at the gate at three o'clock and watched the boys come out — a hundred of them, in caps and coats, shouting, shoving, and she did not see him, and then she did, at the back, with a book under his arm and his collar up, talking to a boy with spectacles, and she saw him laugh at something and she saw, quite clearly, in the moment before he saw her, that he had a whole life in this town that she had no part in.

Then he saw her. His face changed — the whole of it, like a lamp being turned up — and he came through the crowd at a run and stopped a metre away because there were four hundred boys in that courtyard and a master at the gate.

"Zosia," he said. "What are you — is something wrong? Is your mother—"

"Nothing's wrong. I came to sell eggs." She held up the basket. "And to see the town."

He took her to the church of St Wojciech, where there was a side chapel with a painting he wanted her to see, and she looked at it and could not have told anyone afterward what was in it, because she was watching him instead. He took her to a bookshop on Sienkiewicza Street with books in the window and a bell over the door, and she stood in it with her basket on her arm and her mouth slightly open, and he bought her a book for one złoty eighty — a thin book, poems, Norwid — and she said, "I can't take that," and he said, "You can. It's my money. I earned it giving lessons to a boy who can't spell."

And then he took her up into the organ loft of the cathedral, because the organist was his father's friend and would not mind, and he played for her.

The cathedral was enormous and empty and grey, and the instrument came in under her feet and up through the bench and into her chest, and he played badly and then well and then stopped, and in the silence afterward she heard him breathing.

"You love it," she said.

"I love it more than anything," he said, and then he stopped, and then he said, "That isn't true. I didn't mean it like that."

But she had heard it. She sat on the bench beside him in the dark of the loft with the great cold space of the church below them, and she understood — for the first time, and not for the last — that there was a room in him that she would not be allowed into, and that it had nothing to do with her, and that it had been there since before she was born.

On the bus home she held the book in both hands and did not read it, and by the time she came up the road to the village in the dark, with the lamp in her mother's window, she had decided that she did not mind, that a man was allowed a room, and that she would be the whole of the rest of the house.

At Christmas he came home and they went to Midnight Mass together, and afterwards, in the dark outside the church, with the snow coming down in the light of the one lamp, he gave her a thing he had made: a little cross of dried willow twigs, bound with red thread, small enough to hide in a fist.

"It's not a proper present," he said. "It's not even a proper cross. I made it in the workshop."

"It's a proper cross," she said, "if you made it."

"Zosia." He was serious now, in a way that happened to him rarely. "If I don't come back from anywhere — I mean, if there's a war, and I have to go — plant something by the water. So I know where you are."

"Don't talk like that."

"Plant something," he said. "A willow. There's plenty of them. Then when I come back up the road I'll see it."

"You'll come back up the road," she said, "and I'll be standing at the gate, and you'll be dirty and hungry and I'll have made bread, and you'll say, *Zosia,* in that voice, and I'll hit you in the mouth."

He laughed and said that was fair, and they stood in the falling snow with their foreheads together and did not go in until the last of the candles had burned down inside the church and Father Radoń came out and told them, kindly, to go home.

---

In May there were the devotions.

The whole village walked in procession behind the statue of the Mother of God, out through the gate of the churchyard and down the road to the cross at the crossroads and back, singing, and the girls carried wreaths and the men carried the banners and the sun came through the lime trees in coins of light, and Zofia walked at the front with the others and sang until her throat hurt.

Her mother did not walk. She had a cough that had been there since March, and by May she had begun to sit down in the middle of things, and once, in the pantry, she had gone white and held the shelf. Zofia had said, "Mama, you should see the doctor at Mniów," and her mother had said, "And what will he tell me, that I am fifty and I have been working since I was eight? I could have told him that for nothing."

After the procession, in the organ loft, with the window open and the smell of the lime trees coming in, Olek said, "Father Radoń asked me something yesterday."

"What?"

"He asked me if I had ever thought about the seminary."

She laughed. She laughed for longer than the joke deserved, because the loft was warm and her blood was up from the singing and she was seventeen years old and she had a whole life arranged.

"You? You can't get up in the morning. You can't keep your hands off the piano long enough to eat your dinner. You'd be the worst priest in the diocese of Kielce and they'd send you home inside a year."

"I know," he said. "I told him I was going to be a schoolmaster and marry a girl from Wierzbica and have eleven children and teach them all to play."

"Eleven?"

"Nine. I was rounding up."

"Nine is also too many."

"Six, then. And two of them will be girls and they will be exactly like you and I will not survive it." He put his hands on the keys and played the first bar of something and stopped. "He said something else. He said, 'It is a good life, my boy, and it is the only life in which a man is allowed to be poor on purpose.' And I said, 'Father, I'm going to be poor anyway. Everyone here is poor. I'd rather be poor with a piano.'"

She put her head on his shoulder. The window was open, and beyond it the lime trees and the churchyard wall and the wood going up the hill, and a smell of cut grass coming in with the sound of someone's mother calling someone in for supper.

"Whatever you do," she said, "do it with me."

"I will," he said. "That's the only part I'm certain of."

He said it easily. He was eighteen years old and he said it the way you say a thing that has never been tested, and she believed him completely, and for the rest of her life she would be unable to decide which of them had been the more honest.

---

In June 1939 he came back, and the summer was hot and strange, and the whole village was waiting for something it could not name. The manor had a wireless, and the news from it came down to the village the way water comes down a hill: distorted, secondhand, arriving in the mouths of people who had heard it from a man who had heard it from the groom. The Germans wanted Danzig. The Germans had always wanted Danzig. The Germans had a man with a little moustache who shouted on the wireless and the English had a man with an umbrella who waved a piece of paper, and old Szymon said it would come to nothing, and Father Radoń said from the pulpit that the times were in God's hands and men should mind their own souls and their harvest, which was the same thing the Church had said in 1914 and 1920, and which had been good enough then.

Zofia was seventeen. She had a whole future arranged in her head, and it was small, and it was exact. In the autumn Olek would go back to Kielce for his last year and take his examinations, and then he would go to the university in Kraków or Warsaw on a scholarship, or he would not, and if he did not he would come home and be the organist after his father and teach the village children their letters, and either way, in two years or three, there would be a wedding at St Bartholomew's, and there would be a house, and there would be children, and her life would be a thing she had chosen and had built, with her own hands, out of the materials that were available.

She did not want to leave Wierzbica. This surprised her. She had spent two years raging at the gate about the gimnazjum, and now, at seventeen, with her hand in his, she found that what she wanted was not Warsaw or Kielce or a life in a city with trams, but the meadow, and the wood, and the river at dusk, and the sound of him playing in the loft above her while she sat in the empty church with her mother's rosary in her hands and the smell of cold stone all around her.

On the last night of August they sat under the willow, and the water was low and the leaves had begun to turn, and he said, "They'll call us up. If it comes, they'll call up everyone."

"Then they'll call me up too."

"They don't call up women."

"They call up nurses. I'll be a nurse." She said it lightly. "I'll be a terrible nurse. I'll drop the wounded."

"Zosia." He took her face in his hands, and his hands smelled of beeswax. "Say you'll wait."

"I'll wait," she said. "I'll wait until the water runs uphill."

"Say it properly."

"I'll wait," she said. "I'll wait, and I'll plant something by the water, and when you come back up the road I'll be at the gate."

He kissed her forehead, and then her mouth, and then he said the thing that she would carry with her into every bad hour of the next twenty years, and he said it easily, the way you say a thing you believe, and it was not a promise about God or the Church or Poland, because none of those things had yet been asked of him.

"Whatever happens," he said, "we're the same person. You and me. They can take everything else."

The river went on. The willow moved. Up in the village, someone was singing, and a lamp came on in the manor and then went out again, and somewhere beyond the hills, on the other side of a border that had been drawn by men in Vienna who had never seen this valley, a great machine was already turning over, very slowly, the way a millstone turns when the sluice has been opened and the water is coming down the channel and nobody has yet heard it.
