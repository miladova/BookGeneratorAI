# Chapter One — The Dossier

## Warsaw, October 1958

The coal stove in the corner of her office had a temperament. On damp mornings it sulked and smoked, and by nine the room smelled of burning lignite and wet wool, a smell Zofia Kędzierska had long ago decided to think of as the smell of government. She opened the window a hand's width. She had kept her maiden name in the office. It was not unusual — half the women in that building had kept theirs, or had been given one by a husband who had since become a comrade in another ministry — and she had decided in 1946 that it was a small, necessary piece of property: a name that no man had given her.

Below, on Krucza, a tram ground past with its windows steamed, and a man in a good coat was buying nuts from a girl with a brazier, and the sky over the rooftops was the colour of dishwater.

She had been at her desk since half past seven. There was a great deal to do in October. There was always a great deal to do in October: the school year had begun and with it the small war over the catechism; the harvest had come in and with it the bishops' pastoral letters, which had to be read, summarized, and reduced to a page and a half for men who would not read the page; the Party's plenum was approaching, and the Plenum required a formulation, and the formulation required that nothing be true and everything sound correct.

She wrote, in her small, upright hand, at the top of a clean sheet:

*Informacja o sytuacji w zakresie wyznaniowym w województwie kieleckim — za wrzesień.*

The figures for September. Eleven churches closed for "renovation." Four reopened. Two hundred and six religious instruction points struck from the list, of which — she permitted herself the honesty of the private draft — one hundred and forty-one continued to function anyway, in sacristies, in kitchens, in the back rooms of presbyteries, in fields. Forty-one baptisms per thousand of population, up from thirty-eight. Fifty-two vocations to the seminaries, of which the bishop had told the authorities there were seventy.

She lit a cigarette and looked at the number fifty-two for a while.

In the corridor someone dropped a metal wastebasket and swore, and Pani Irena put her head around the door with the post. Irena Wolska was fifty and had the forearms of a woman who had spent the war carrying things; she had been Zofia's secretary for six years and had never once asked her a personal question, which Zofia considered the highest form of loyalty available in Warsaw.

"The director wants you at ten," Irena said. "And this came from the Episcopate's secretariat by courier." She laid a buff envelope on the desk as though it were a fish.

"Which director?"

"Turski. The other one has been in the building since six. He asked who was in." A pause, precisely calibrated. "He asked whether you were in."

"Thank you, Irena."

When the door closed, Zofia finished her cigarette before she opened the envelope, because she had learned that urgency was a thing other people had. Then she slit the flap with the brass letter knife and shook out four typewritten pages and a photograph the size of a playing card, clipped to the first.

The Episcopate had sent the *terna*: three names for the vacant See of Sandomierz. The diocese had been without a bishop for nineteen months. Rome had grown tired of waiting; the Episcopate had grown tired of pretending to wait; and so in the last week of September the Primate's secretary had come to Krucza with a face like a plate and explained, in the manner of a man laying down a losing hand, that the Church proposed to present three candidates and would be grateful for the opinion of the state.

The state's opinion was Zofia's job.

She read the first name. Father Czesław Dembiński, forty-eight, of the Caritas association, a man with soft hands and a soft voice who had spent the war in a parish in the Kraków countryside and the peace writing articles about the Church's role in socialist construction. He had a brother in the Party. He was, Rygier liked to say, "our priest," and he said it without irony, which was the most frightening thing about Rygier.

The second name. Father Zygmunt Kalinowski, fifty-five, canon of the cathedral chapter, author of a dissertation on the synods of the fifteenth century, a man who had never in his life said anything interesting and never intended to.

The third name was Aleksander Zaremba.

She read it the way she read everything, at speed, in units of information: *Zaremba, Aleksander, son of Wiktor and Aniela, born 4 May 1922, Wierzbica, poviat Kielce. Ordained 1950, Kraków. Vicar, Radoszyce, 1950–52. Arrested 9 February 1952. Released under amnesty, 22 December 1954. Since 1955 parish priest, St Kazimierz, Warsaw-Żoliborz. Former soldier, Home Army, pseudonym "Ryś."*

Her hand did not shake. She was thirty-six years old and had not cried in front of another person since the spring of 1945, and she was not going to begin in a government office with a typing pool on the other side of the wall. She put the page down flat on the desk, squared it with her thumb, and looked at the photograph.

It had been taken in a courtyard, in front of a wall the colour of weak tea. He was standing with his hands behind his back and his chin slightly lifted, in a black clerical coat that did not fit him at the shoulders. He was thin. The boy she remembered had been all wrists and laughter and a neck that reddened when he was teased; this man had a long face, a high forehead, and the beginnings of grey at the temples, though he was thirty-six, her age, four months younger — born in May while she had come in the January of the same year, and he had never once let her forget it.

The eyes were the same. That was the filthy trick of it. The eyes were exactly the same — that particular grey, like water under ice, with the little fold at the outer corner that made him look, even in a photograph of a man in a courtyard, as if he were about to laugh at something he had just understood.

She turned the photograph face down.

Then she turned it back.

---

At five to ten she went along the corridor with her folder under her arm, and Pani Irena said, "He's in a mood," and Zofia said, "He's always in a mood," and went in.

Adam Turski's office was twice the size of hers and had a carpet and a portrait of the First Secretary at an angle that suggested the painter had been instructed to make him look taller. Turski himself was at the window with his back to the room, smoking, a small, fastidious man of fifty-eight in a suit of English wool that must have been fifteen years old and had been worth it. On the wall behind his desk hung a framed page of music, manuscript, with a dedication in ink: he had been, before the war, a music critic of some reputation, and after the war a critic of no reputation at all, and now he was Director of the Office for Religious Affairs, a job in which his considerable intelligence was spent entirely on the management of other men's souls.

"Zofia," he said, without turning. "Sit. Tell me something true."

"That depends on what you want to hear."

"I want to hear that they have given up on Sandomierz and decided to appoint a nice Presbyterian."

"They've sent three names." She laid the pages on the desk. "Dembiński. Kalinowski. And a priest called Zaremba."

Turski turned around. He had the face of an intelligent old heron, and he looked at her for slightly too long before he sat down. "Zaremba," he said. "The young one. The one Rygier's people kept for nearly three years."

"The file says twenty-two December 1954."

"The file is generous. They let him out because the amnesty was announced and it would have been awkward to have him die in the middle of it." He picked up the photograph and studied it with real interest, the way he used to study a score. "Thirty-six. Bishop at thirty-six. The youngest in the history of the Polish Church since — I don't know. Since the partitions. Since the Apostles."

"It won't happen."

"No. Probably not. Rome is cautious and the Primate is in no position to be bold." He tapped the photograph against his knuckle. "But this is what they are offering us. A martyr who is still young enough to be useful, and who has, according to our own people, spent four years being careful."

"Careful," Zofia said.

"He kept his mouth shut in prison and he has kept his mouth shut since. He says Mass, he hears confessions, he preaches — I'm told he preaches well, in the sentimental way — and he does not write letters to the West and he does not sign things. Men who do not sign things are, in my experience, either cowards or the most dangerous people in the country."

"What does Rygier want?"

"Rygier wants to arrest the vicar." Turski said it mildly. "A boy at St Kazimierz, twenty-six, teaching the catechism to children in a private flat in Żoliborz, which is of course illegal, and which half the parishes in Warsaw do. Rygier's people have been watching the flat since August. He wants to make an example before the Plenum, and he wants me to write him a note saying the Office raises no objection, and I would rather be shot in the courtyard of the Mokotów prison than write that note for a twenty-six-year-old who teaches children their prayers."

"You've written worse."

"I have written worse," Turski agreed. "And I remember all of it. That's the trouble with my memory." He stubbed out his cigarette with precision. "Here is the situation. The Episcopate needs a bishop. We need — the state needs — a bishop who will not embarrass us, who will speak at the Front of National Unity congress, who will sign the declaration on the Millennium when we finally force the Millennium, and who will, in the fullness of time, be photographed shaking the hand of a man in a good suit. Dembiński will do all of that and be despised by every Catholic in Poland within a year. Kalinowski will do none of it and sleep perfectly well. Zaremba—" He shrugged.

"Zaremba will do none of it and be loved."

"Yes." Turski looked at her with sudden and unwelcome attention. "You know him."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below in the street a lorry changed gear.

"Everyone in that part of the country knows that name," Zofia said. "There aren't many of them left. The ones who came back."

"Ah," said Turski. He lit another cigarette. He did not believe her, and he did not disbelieve her, and he let it lie, which was one of the reasons she had tolerated him for six years. "Well. I need a recommendation on the file by Friday. Not a decision — a recommendation. The Episcopate will make the decision and we will make the decision, and the two decisions will be made in different rooms by people who will never meet, and then somebody will announce that the Holy Spirit has been consulted."

"And Rygier's vicar?"

"Rygier's vicar has until Christmas, unless Rygier decides otherwise, which he will. I want you to read the file and tell me what the boy is." He used the word with the particular contempt of a man who had once been young. "Not what the file says. What he is."

---

The file on Aleksander Zaremba was four centimetres thick and smelled of dust and the particular sourness of old paper. It took her the rest of the morning, and she read it the way she read everything, at speed, and this time it was a mercy.

A report from the Kielce voivodeship office, 1946: *Zaremba, A., former AK, pseudonym "Ryś," participant in the Warsaw Rising, wounded, treated in German hospital at Częstochowa, returned to Wierzbica April 1945, entered the seminary at Kraków September 1945. Mother and father deceased. No siblings. No known political affiliations. Recommend observation.*

Observation. She looked at the word for a while.

A report, 1948: *Subject observed in the company of Father Michał Stępień, canon, suspected of maintaining contact with the émigré centre in London. No evidence.*

A report, 1949: *During the retreat at Kalwaria, subject preached a sermon on the text "Render unto Caesar." Contents as yet unknown; informant describes the congregation as "moved."*

A report, 1951: *Subject has organized a library of sixty volumes in the parish at Radoszyce. Among them works by Norwid, Mickiewicz, and the French novelists. Recommend confiscation of the French novelists.*

She laughed out loud, once, in the empty office, and then put her hand over her mouth.

And then 1952: the arrest, in February, at night, in the presbytery. The interrogation protocols, which were typewritten and dull, and which she read faster and faster. A statement, signed on the eleventh of March 1952, in a hand she knew — a round, upright, boyish hand, a hand that had once written her name in the dust of a cart — in which he declared that he had been misled, that he had taken part in "reactionary activity" under the influence of "hostile elements," that he repudiated the same, and that he undertook to abstain in future from any action against the Polish People's Republic.

Signed, Aleksander Zaremba.

She put her finger on the signature. Twenty-nine years old, in a cellar on Rakowiecka Street, with, according to a note in the margin in another hand, a broken wrist.

There was nothing else in the file for the whole of 1953. Then 1954, the amnesty, the release, a note that he had spent two months in a sanatorium at Otwock for his lungs, and then silence, and then, in 1955, the parish at Żoliborz: *Subject conducts himself correctly. Popular with youth. Refuses to sign the declarations. Maintains that the Church does not engage in politics.*

At the bottom of the file, in Rygier's own hand, in green ink, a note dated the second of October, four days ago:

*If this one is given the see, we will have a bishop we cannot touch and cannot forgive. Recommend we do everything to prevent, and if we cannot prevent, everything to compromise. Ask K. to handle. She is qualified.*

*Ask K. to handle.*

Zofia sat very still. The stove ticked as it cooled. She was aware, in a remote way, of her own pulse in her throat, and of the cigarette burning down between her fingers, and she put it out, and folded her hands on the file, and looked out of the window at the grey October light coming down over the rooftops of Warsaw on a city that had been, thirteen years before, a field of bricks.

*She is qualified.*

She had been eighteen when the war came, and twenty-two when it ended, and she had spent the years in between learning that the world was made of men who decided things and people who were decided about. Her mother had taught her the rosary and the names of the seven sorrows. Her mother had also taught her that a woman's life was a thing done to her by weather, by landlords, by priests, by men who came to the door with papers. Zofia had believed the first and doubted the second, and then in August 1944 she had stood in a barn with eleven other women from Wierzbica while a man in a grey uniform smoked a cigarette and decided which of them would be sent to work and which of them would be shot, and she had understood that the two teachings were the same teaching and that the Church had told her the truth in the wrong language.

She had been sent to work. She had spent nine months in a factory at the edge of Leipzig making parts for aeroplanes, and she had come home in May 1945 weighing forty-one kilos with two fingers that would never again be warm, and a Party instructor named Wanda Sroka had met her at the repatriation point at Kielce with a bowl of soup and a question.

*And what will you do now, comrade?*

Zofia had looked at the soup. Then she had looked at the woman, who was forty, and ugly, and had a pistol in her handbag and no husband and no children and the calmest face Zofia had ever seen.

*I'll do whatever needs doing,* she had said.

---

In the afternoon there was a petitioner.

This happened twice a week and Zofia had a system. Irena brought them in at three; they sat on the chair with their hats on their knees; they explained; she listened for four minutes and then told them the truth in a form they could survive.

This one was an old woman from a village near Płock, and she had come for the fourth time in a year. Her name was on a card in Zofia's drawer, with the dates. Her church had been closed in 1956 and converted into a grain store, and the grain store had burned down in July, and the village had written to the Curia, and the Curia had written to Krucza, and Krucza had written to the voivodeship, and the voivodeship had written back that the building was required for the purposes of the national economy.

"The roof is gone," the old woman said. "The rain goes in. The Blessed Mother is under the rain, comrade." She had a face like a walnut and a black kerchief tied under her chin, and she said *comrade* as though it were a word in a foreign language she had been forced to learn. "We don't ask for a priest. We ask for a roof."

Zofia looked at the card. Fourth visit. There was a note in her own hand from March: *No grounds. Advise Curia.*

"The building is no longer a church," she said. "Under the decree of 1949 it passed to the state. I cannot give you a roof for a building that is not a church."

The old woman was silent for a moment. Then she said, "Where is your mother buried, comrade?"

Zofia's pen stopped.

"I'm sorry?"

"Your mother." The old woman's eyes were pale and perfectly steady. "When you go to her grave, is there a roof?"

There was a long pause in the office. In the corridor, a typewriter stopped and then started again. Zofia found that she was looking at the old woman's hands, which were split and black at the knuckles, and that she was thinking, with a clarity that frightened her, about the fact that she had not been to her mother's grave since 1946, and that the grave was in a field behind the church at Wierzbica, and that the church at Wierzbica had a roof, because Wierzbica had been lucky, because the Germans had burned the village and left the church, because the church had been built of stone in 1683 by a man whose name nobody remembered.

"Pani," she said, and her voice was even, and she was proud of it. "I will write to the voivodeship office. Tar paper. Not a roof — tar paper over the north aisle, so the rain does not fall on the altar. That is what I can do."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she stood up and put her hat on, and said, "You are a good woman. That is the worst of it." And went out.

Zofia sat with her hands flat on the desk for some time. Then she picked up the telephone and asked for the voivodeship office at Płock, and was told the official was in a meeting, and left a message, and dictated a letter, and at half past five she put on her coat and went out into the dark.

---

They lived in Mokotów, in a block built in 1952 for people who had done well out of the rebuilding, on the fourth floor, with a lift that worked on alternate Thursdays. Her husband, Bogdan Leszczyński, was already home. He was forty-four, an official in the Ministry of Foreign Trade, a heavy, kind, disappointed man who had joined the Party in 1945 because everyone he respected had joined the Party in 1945, and who had discovered by degrees that the revolution had no particular use for a man who was good at arithmetic and bad at lying.

He was in the kitchen in his shirtsleeves, frying onions. Piotr was at the table with his elbows on it, drawing a horse.

"Your mother is late," Bogdan said, without turning.

"Your mother is always late," said Piotr, without looking up, and Zofia put her hand flat on the back of his head and he ducked and grinned, and she felt the day come off her shoulders like a coat.

The flat had three rooms and a balcony and a wireless and a bookcase with sixty books in it, forty of which were Bogdan's. There was a rug from Łódź and a photograph of the two of them at a Party function in 1950 on which Zofia had her hair in a roll and looked, she thought, like a woman in a Soviet film about surgeons. There was a reproduction of a painting of a birch wood over the sideboard. There was no crucifix, no holy picture, no Christmas wafer; there had been no baptism for Piotr, no first communion, no godparents, and on the twenty-fourth of December they had a tree and gifts and a fish, and it was a good evening, and it was the only time of the year when Zofia felt the thing she could not name.

Over dinner — the onions, and buckwheat, and a piece of pork that had cost more than it should — Piotr said, "Mama, does it hurt to die?"

Bogdan's fork paused. Zofia went on chewing, because she had learned to eat through anything.

"Why do you ask?"

"Jurek's grandmother died. He said they put her in the ground and she goes up to heaven and then on the last day she comes out again." He was ten, and had his father's heavy brow and her own quick, sideways way of looking at people. "But if she's in the ground, how does she come out?"

"Jurek's grandmother is in the ground," Zofia said, "and what comes out of the ground is grass. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," said Piotr, in the voice of a child who does not understand, and who has learned that it is easier to say yes. Then he said, "Jurek goes to church. On Sundays. He says there's a man who turns bread into a god."

"Jurek's father is a watchman at the steelworks and his mother cleans offices," said Zofia, "and they are good people, and they are also ignorant people, and you will not go with Jurek to church. Is that clear?"

"Yes, Mama."

"Eat your buckwheat."

Later, when the boy was in bed, she stood at the window of the living room with a cigarette and looked down at the courtyard, where a single lamp hung over the rubbish bins and a cat was sitting in the wet. Bogdan came and stood behind her without touching her, which was the shape their marriage had taken.

"You frightened him," he said.

"I did not frighten him. I told him the truth."

"You told him the truth at a table with the boy's dinner in front of him, in the voice you use on the voivodeship offices." He was quiet for a moment. "Zofia. He is ten. Let him have his grandmothers in heaven for a year or two."

"His grandmother is not in heaven. His grandmother is in a field in Wierzbica and there's a stone on her." She turned. "You think I'm cruel."

"I think you are the least cruel person I have ever met and I think that you have arranged your life so that you never have to find out."

He went to bed. She stood at the window for a long time. Then she went into the bedroom, and took down from the top of the wardrobe a tin box that had once held biscuits, and sat on the edge of the bed with it on her knees, and opened it.

Inside: a wooden rosary, the beads worn to the shape of fingers that were not hers. A photograph of a girl of sixteen in a white blouse standing in front of a willow, squinting. A photograph of a boy of seventeen in a too-big jacket, laughing, with his hand up to shade his eyes. A sprig of dried leaves, thirteen years old, which crumbled when she touched it. A folded paper, which she did not unfold, because she had unfolded it four hundred times and knew what it said.

*Zosiu. If I don't come back, plant something by the water so I know where you are.*

She put everything back, and closed the tin, and put it on the wardrobe, and lay down beside her husband in the dark, and did not sleep.

At half past eleven she got up, and went into the kitchen, and sat at the table with a pencil and a piece of paper, and wrote, in her upright hand:

*Zaremba, Aleksander.*

Then, underneath:

*Ask what he is.*

She looked at it for a while. Then she reached for the telephone and asked the exchange for the Ministry of Internal Affairs, extension 214, and when a man's voice answered she said, "This is Kędzierska, Office for Religious Affairs. Tell Colonel Rygier I want the whole file. Not the extracts. The whole thing, from 1945, including the correspondence."

There was a pause on the line. "The colonel is at a meeting in the building."

"Then tell him when he comes out of it."

She put the receiver down. The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere on the floor above, a chair scraped, and a child laughed, and then the laugh stopped, and the night went on with its work, which is the same work as the day's, only done in the dark.

# Chapter Two — Two Fires

## Wierzbica, 1938

The village lay in a fold of the hills like something set down and forgotten. There was one road through it, rutted, white in summer, and the road crossed the river at the mill and went up past the church and out the other side toward Kielce, eighteen kilometres away, which was as far as most people in Wierzbica had ever been and further than some of them thought advisable. The houses were timber and clay, thatched with straw gone black at the ridge, and behind them the fields went up in strips of rye and potato and oats, and behind the fields there was pine wood, and behind the wood there was nothing at all, only the hills going on and on into the haze.

Her mother said a village had two fires. There was the fire in the hearth, which was the family, and there was the fire in the church, which was God, and a woman's life was the business of carrying a coal from one to the other without dropping it.

Marianna Kędzierska said this in the same tone in which she said that the milk would turn before Thursday, so it was some years before her daughter understood that she had been given a catechism.

Zofia was sixteen in the summer of 1938 and she was, by the standards of the village, spoiled. She had been to school for seven years, to the end of the seventh class, which was two years more than her brother Antek and four more than most of the girls. She could read fast, and write without thinking about it, and she had once come second in a district examination in Kielce in front of an inspector from the Curatorium who had said, in front of her father, that this one should go on to the gimnazjum. Her father had said nothing all the way home and then, at the gate, had said, *And what would I pay with, the potatoes?* and gone in to his supper. Zofia had stood at the gate for a while, and then had gone in and eaten her supper, and had never mentioned it again, and had thought about it every day for two years.

She was not beautiful in the way the girls in the magazines in the manor house were beautiful. She was brown from the sun, and her hands were a labourer's hands, and she had a chipped front tooth from a fight with Antek when she was nine. But she had a face that people turned to look at, because there was something in it that was already going somewhere. Father Radoń, who had baptized her, said she had the devil's own energy and it would either make her a saint or take her to Warsaw.

The church of St Bartholomew had been built in 1683 by a man whose name was carved above the door in letters worn to shadows. It was stone, with a roof of shingle and a tower with one bell that had been recast in 1796, and inside it was dark and cool and smelled of cold stone and old incense and the wet wool of sixty people. On the left of the altar there was a painting of the Mother of God with a gold crown and a face of such ordinary sadness that Zofia had grown up believing she was a relation.

She sang in the choir. She had a voice that was too loud and not quite pure, and she loved it more than she loved anything except one thing, and on the feast of Corpus Christi she walked at the front of the procession in a white dress with a wreath of rue on her head, and the whole village watched her, and she knew it, and she looked for one face in the crowd and found it and looked away.

---

The organist's son had come home for the summer.

Wiktor Zaremba had been the organist at St Bartholomew's for thirty years, a small, dry, exact man who kept bees and tuned the piano in the manor house and could read any music put in front of him. His wife Aniela sewed for the manor and for the wives of Kielce and had a mouth on her that she kept closed in front of her husband. Their son Aleksander was seventeen, and had been at the gimnazjum in Kielce for four years, boarding with his mother's sister above a shoemaker's shop, and he came home at Christmas and Easter and for the whole of the summer, and in the summer he was everywhere.

He was tall and had not grown into his height. He had his mother's grey eyes and his father's hands, and he laughed at things before other people had noticed they were funny, which either made him the best company in the village or an idiot, depending on who you asked. He could play the organ, badly, and the piano, well, and he had a black notebook in which he wrote things down — poems, he said, though when Zofia had stolen it and read it, it was mostly lists, and one poem about a horse, and a page on which he had written her name eleven times in different sizes.

She had known him since she was four. She had disliked him until she was eleven, when he had pulled her out of the river by the hair and she had hit him in the mouth, and after that they had been friends in the way of children who have decided, without discussion, that they are on the same side of something.

In the summer of 1938, they were no longer children, and the whole village knew it before either of them did.

It was the hay. That was where it happened, or where it finished happening. The meadow by the river belongs to the four families who have rights in it, and in the last week of June they cut it together, men and women in a line with scythes and rakes, and the smell of cut grass in that heat was so strong it made your eyes water. Zofia worked with the rakes and the pitchforks, and Olek worked with the men, badly, because a boy who has spent four years in a gimnazjum in Kielce has a scholar's back, and Antek mocked him for it ceaselessly and with love.

On the third day, in the afternoon, when the heat was a hand pressed flat on the back of your neck, she went down to the river to drink. The water was low and warm at the edges and cold in the middle, and she knelt and drank from her hands and then sat back on her heels.

Olek came down the bank and knelt beside her and drank, and then wiped his mouth on his sleeve and said, "There's a heron."

"Where?"

"There." He pointed, and she looked, and there was no heron.

When she turned back he was looking at her, and he did not look away, and she felt the whole summer turn over like a stone in her chest.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Look at me like that." Her voice came out strange. "Somebody will see."

"Nobody can see. There's nobody—"

"There's always somebody."

He was quiet. Then he said, "Zosia."

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said my name," she said, "in that voice," and got up and went back up the bank with her heart going like a bird in a sack, and worked the rest of the afternoon without looking at him once, and felt every inch of the distance between them like a wire pulled tight.

That evening they finished the last of the hay and stacked it, and the men opened a bottle and the women brought out bread and dripping and sour milk, and they ate sitting on the ground in the dark while the bats came out. Old Szymon the miller told a story about a priest and a goose that Zofia had heard nine times and laughed at anyway. Antek fell asleep with his head on his arms. Her mother sat with her back against the haycart, mending by touch, the needle going in and out in the firelight.

Zofia got up and walked away from the fire, down toward the water, because she had seen him get up a quarter of an hour before and go that way.

He was under the willow. It was an old tree, a white willow, split near the base into two trunks and leaning out over the water, and in the dark it looked like a woman bending down to wash her hair. He was sitting on the root with his knees up.

"I thought you'd come," he said.

"I thought you'd be here," she said, and sat down beside him, and neither of them said anything for a long moment. The river made its small continuous sound. Up the hill a dog was barking at something that had already gone.

"Zosia," he said. "I'm going to say it badly."

"Then say it."

"I've been in love with you since I was eleven and you hit me in the mouth."

"That was because you pulled my hair."

"I know. I'm telling you the chronology." He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "And in September I have to go back to Kielce and I'll be there until June and I can't — I can't sit in that room above the shoemaker's with your name in my notebook and not know whether—"

She kissed him.

She had thought about it for a year, and had constructed in her mind a hundred ways it would be, and it was none of them. It was clumsy and his nose was in the way and she was shaking, and when they broke apart he said, "Oh," in a stunned voice, like a boy who has been hit on the head, and she started to laugh and then to cry, and he put his arms round her and held her, and the willow moved above them in the small wind.

"I'll wait," she said, into his shoulder. "I'll wait until June and then I'll wait some more."

---

They had eleven months.

She would think about those eleven months for the rest of her life, and what she would remember was not the war, which had not yet come, but the ordinary things, which were the things the war would take. She remembered that he could not whistle. She remembered that he was afraid of the dark in a way he was ashamed of, and that when they walked back from the church at night he talked continuously and too loudly so that neither of them would notice. She remembered his hands on the piano in the organ loft, the way he played with his head down and his lips moving. She remembered the smell of beeswax in his father's workshop, and the taste of the honeycomb he stole for her from the hives, and the way he said her name — *Zosia* — with the accent on the first syllable, so that it sounded like a question that had been answered.

She remembered the letters. He went back to Kielce in September and wrote to her twice a week, and the letters came to the post office in the mill and Szymon's wife kept them behind the counter, and Zofia walked down to fetch them and read them in the wood where nobody could see her face. He wrote about the Latin class and the mathematics teacher who had a glass eye and the manifesto that a boy in the fourth form had been expelled for. He wrote about the Germans, more and more, as the winter went on. In March he wrote: *They have taken Austria and nobody did anything. Last week they took a piece of Lithuania and nobody did anything. Zosia, I think we are living in a country that is being got ready.*

She wrote back. She wrote badly; she had never written a letter that was not a list; and she wrote the same things every time, which was that she missed him, and that the sow had farrowed, and that her mother had a pain in her side, and that she had been thinking about the willow. And then she would add, at the end, a sentence that took her an hour: *Everything is the same here and I am the same and I will be the same in June.*

In October she went to Kielce.

She had never been. She had been to Mniów and to the market at Strawczyn and once, at the age of nine, to a fair at Jędrzejów, and the town was a thing she knew from the front of a postcard. She went with her father's cousin's cart as far as Mniów and then by bus, and she carried a basket of eggs to sell and a clean blouse in a paper parcel, and she was so frightened on the bus that she could not feel her hands.

The gimnazjum was a long yellow building with a courtyard and a gate with iron letters over it. She stood at the gate at three o'clock and watched the boys come out — a hundred of them, in caps and coats, shouting, shoving, and she did not see him, and then she did, at the back, with a book under his arm and his collar up, talking to a boy with spectacles, and she saw him laugh at something and she saw, quite clearly, in the moment before he saw her, that he had a whole life in this town that she had no part in.

Then he saw her. His face changed — the whole of it, like a lamp being turned up — and he came through the crowd at a run and stopped a metre away because there were four hundred boys in that courtyard and a master at the gate.

"Zosia," he said. "What are you — is something wrong? Is your mother—"

"Nothing's wrong. I came to sell eggs." She held up the basket. "And to see the town."

He took her to the church of St Wojciech, where there was a side chapel with a painting he wanted her to see, and she looked at it and could not have told anyone afterward what was in it, because she was watching him instead. He took her to a bookshop on Sienkiewicza Street with books in the window and a bell over the door, and she stood in it with her basket on her arm and her mouth slightly open, and he bought her a book for one złoty eighty — a thin book, poems, Norwid — and she said, "I can't take that," and he said, "You can. It's my money. I earned it giving lessons to a boy who can't spell."

And then he took her up into the organ loft of the cathedral, because the organist was his father's friend and would not mind, and he played for her.

The cathedral was enormous and empty and grey, and the instrument came in under her feet and up through the bench and into her chest, and he played badly and then well and then stopped, and in the silence afterward she heard him breathing.

"You love it," she said.

"I love it more than anything," he said, and then he stopped, and then he said, "That isn't true. I didn't mean it like that."

But she had heard it. She sat on the bench beside him in the dark of the loft with the great cold space of the church below them, and she understood — for the first time, and not for the last — that there was a room in him that she would not be allowed into, and that it had nothing to do with her, and that it had been there since before she was born.

On the bus home she held the book in both hands and did not read it, and by the time she came up the road to the village in the dark, with the lamp in her mother's window, she had decided that she did not mind, that a man was allowed a room, and that she would be the whole of the rest of the house.

At Christmas he came home and they went to Midnight Mass together, and afterwards, in the dark outside the church, with the snow coming down in the light of the one lamp, he gave her a thing he had made: a little cross of dried willow twigs, bound with red thread, small enough to hide in a fist.

"It's not a proper present," he said. "It's not even a proper cross. I made it in the workshop."

"It's a proper cross," she said, "if you made it."

"Zosia." He was serious now, in a way that happened to him rarely. "If I don't come back from anywhere — I mean, if there's a war, and I have to go — plant something by the water. So I know where you are."

"Don't talk like that."

"Plant something," he said. "A willow. There's plenty of them. Then when I come back up the road I'll see it."

"You'll come back up the road," she said, "and I'll be standing at the gate, and you'll be dirty and hungry and I'll have made bread, and you'll say, *Zosia,* in that voice, and I'll hit you in the mouth."

He laughed and said that was fair, and they stood in the falling snow with their foreheads together and did not go in until the last of the candles had burned down inside the church and Father Radoń came out and told them, kindly, to go home.

---

In May there were the devotions.

The whole village walked in procession behind the statue of the Mother of God, out through the gate of the churchyard and down the road to the cross at the crossroads and back, singing, and the girls carried wreaths and the men carried the banners and the sun came through the lime trees in coins of light, and Zofia walked at the front with the others and sang until her throat hurt.

Her mother did not walk. She had a cough that had been there since March, and by May she had begun to sit down in the middle of things, and once, in the pantry, she had gone white and held the shelf. Zofia had said, "Mama, you should see the doctor at Mniów," and her mother had said, "And what will he tell me, that I am fifty and I have been working since I was eight? I could have told him that for nothing."

After the procession, in the organ loft, with the window open and the smell of the lime trees coming in, Olek said, "Father Radoń asked me something yesterday."

"What?"

"He asked me if I had ever thought about the seminary."

She laughed. She laughed for longer than the joke deserved, because the loft was warm and her blood was up from the singing and she was seventeen years old and she had a whole life arranged.

"You? You can't get up in the morning. You can't keep your hands off the piano long enough to eat your dinner. You'd be the worst priest in the diocese of Kielce and they'd send you home inside a year."

"I know," he said. "I told him I was going to be a schoolmaster and marry a girl from Wierzbica and have eleven children and teach them all to play."

"Eleven?"

"Nine. I was rounding up."

"Nine is also too many."

"Six, then. And two of them will be girls and they will be exactly like you and I will not survive it." He put his hands on the keys and played the first bar of something and stopped. "He said something else. He said, 'It is a good life, my boy, and it is the only life in which a man is allowed to be poor on purpose.' And I said, 'Father, I'm going to be poor anyway. Everyone here is poor. I'd rather be poor with a piano.'"

She put her head on his shoulder. The window was open, and beyond it the lime trees and the churchyard wall and the wood going up the hill, and a smell of cut grass coming in with the sound of someone's mother calling someone in for supper.

"Whatever you do," she said, "do it with me."

"I will," he said. "That's the only part I'm certain of."

He said it easily. He was eighteen years old and he said it the way you say a thing that has never been tested, and she believed him completely, and for the rest of her life she would be unable to decide which of them had been the more honest.

---

In June 1939 he came back, and the summer was hot and strange, and the whole village was waiting for something it could not name. The manor had a wireless, and the news from it came down to the village the way water comes down a hill: distorted, secondhand, arriving in the mouths of people who had heard it from a man who had heard it from the groom. The Germans wanted Danzig. The Germans had always wanted Danzig. The Germans had a man with a little moustache who shouted on the wireless and the English had a man with an umbrella who waved a piece of paper, and old Szymon said it would come to nothing, and Father Radoń said from the pulpit that the times were in God's hands and men should mind their own souls and their harvest, which was the same thing the Church had said in 1914 and 1920, and which had been good enough then.

Zofia was seventeen. She had a whole future arranged in her head, and it was small, and it was exact. In the autumn Olek would go back to Kielce for his last year and take his examinations, and then he would go to the university in Kraków or Warsaw on a scholarship, or he would not, and if he did not he would come home and be the organist after his father and teach the village children their letters, and either way, in two years or three, there would be a wedding at St Bartholomew's, and there would be a house, and there would be children, and her life would be a thing she had chosen and had built, with her own hands, out of the materials that were available.

She did not want to leave Wierzbica. This surprised her. She had spent two years raging at the gate about the gimnazjum, and now, at seventeen, with her hand in his, she found that what she wanted was not Warsaw or Kielce or a life in a city with trams, but the meadow, and the wood, and the river at dusk, and the sound of him playing in the loft above her while she sat in the empty church with her mother's rosary in her hands and the smell of cold stone all around her.

On the last night of August they sat under the willow, and the water was low and the leaves had begun to turn, and he said, "They'll call us up. If it comes, they'll call up everyone."

"Then they'll call me up too."

"They don't call up women."

"They call up nurses. I'll be a nurse." She said it lightly. "I'll be a terrible nurse. I'll drop the wounded."

"Zosia." He took her face in his hands, and his hands smelled of beeswax. "Say you'll wait."

"I'll wait," she said. "I'll wait until the water runs uphill."

"Say it properly."

"I'll wait," she said. "I'll wait, and I'll plant something by the water, and when you come back up the road I'll be at the gate."

He kissed her forehead, and then her mouth, and then he said the thing that she would carry with her into every bad hour of the next twenty years, and he said it easily, the way you say a thing you believe, and it was not a promise about God or the Church or Poland, because none of those things had yet been asked of him.

"Whatever happens," he said, "we're the same person. You and me. They can take everything else."

The river went on. The willow moved. Up in the village, someone was singing, and a lamp came on in the manor and then went out again, and somewhere beyond the hills, on the other side of a border that had been drawn by men in Vienna who had never seen this valley, a great machine was already turning over, very slowly, the way a millstone turns when the sluice has been opened and the water is coming down the channel and nobody has yet heard it.

# Chapter Three — The Machine and the Willow

## Wierzbica, 1939–1944

The war came on a Friday, at four in the afternoon, in the form of three aeroplanes going west.

Zofia was in the potato field with her mother. They heard them before they saw them, a sound that was not like any sound she knew — not thunder, which rolls and finishes, but a flat, unending grinding, as though someone were dragging a table across the floor of heaven. The three machines came over low, so low she could see the glass of the cockpit and a man's face in it, and they were gone over the hill before she had time to be afraid. Then, from the direction of Kielce, there was a series of soft, heavy thumps, one after another, like a man walking on a drum.

Her mother stood with a hoe in her hand and her mouth open, and then she said, "Get the children," and Zofia ran.

By the end of the first week the roads were full of people going east. They came up the road past the mill in carts and on bicycles and on foot, with bundles on their backs and children on the bundles, and they had come from Poznań, and then from Łódź, and then from towns whose names nobody in Wierzbica had heard of. They stopped at the well and asked for water. They said the Germans had tanks that went sixty kilometres an hour. They said the Polish cavalry had charged the tanks with sabres. They said the English and the French had declared war and would be here by Christmas. Then they said nothing at all, and went on east, and the road was full for nine days and then it was empty.

On the twelfth of September a motorcyclist came through the village and stopped at the mill and asked the way to Kielce, in Polish, badly, and Szymon the miller told him, and the man gave him a bar of chocolate and rode on. That was the first German anybody in Wierzbica spoke to. The second came on the seventeenth, and there were four thousand of them, and they stayed for five years.

---

Olek came home in the second week, on foot, from Kielce, with his school satchel and a cut over one eye. He had walked for two days. He had seen a horse that had been killed by a bomb and had been cut open by hungry people before it was cold, and he had seen a woman sitting on the road with a dead child across her knees, and he did not speak about either of these things for a year, and then, once, in the dark, in the loft, he spoke about them both and then he was sick.

They stood in the yard of the organist's house and looked at each other and did not touch, because his mother was at the door, and because a German staff car was at that moment going up the road to the manor with three officers in it. He said, "Well."

She said, "Well."

And he said, "I've come back up the road," and she laughed and cried at once, and Aniela Zarembowa said sharply, "Inside, both of you, before the whole village sees," and they went inside and ate bread and lard at the table with their hands shaking, and it was, in a way she would not have believed possible, almost happy.

---

The occupation had a shape, and the shape was this: the manor was taken by a German administrator named Kling, who wore riding boots and shot hares from the upstairs window and left them where they fell. The church stayed open, because the Germans had not yet decided what to do with churches. The school was closed. The mill worked for the Germans and ground for the village at night, illegally, and Szymon kept two sets of books, and if he was afraid he did not show it, and he was beaten to death in 1943 for a sack of flour. The ration was one hundred and fifty grammes of bread a day for a person who was not working, and the village did not live on the ration, the village lived on the potato and the pig and the cow and the wood, and the Germans took the pig and the cow and the wood, and the village lived on what was left, which was less each year.

Father Radoń was arrested in the spring of 1941, along with three other priests from the district, and taken to the prison at Kielce, and he came back in November, and he was not the same man. He had lost the hearing in one ear. He said Mass correctly, and preached briefly, and he no longer spoke about anything except the sacraments, and he had a way of stopping in the middle of a sentence and looking at the door.

Olek was eighteen and then nineteen and then twenty. He had been going to be an organist or a doctor or a poet, and instead he carried water and chopped wood and learned to make a potato last four days. In the winter of 1940 a man came through the village selling thread and stayed three nights at the organist's house, and after he had gone Olek was different. He was quieter. He went for long walks in the wood in the evenings. He came back with pine needles in his coat and a new way of holding his shoulders.

"Don't ask me," he said. "Not ever, in the house, not to anyone. Not to your mother."

"I'd die before I told anyone."

"Don't die," he said. "Just don't tell."

She knew, of course. Everyone knew without knowing. The village was a machine for knowing things and calling it nothing. In 1941 the first of the boys went away and came back in the night and slept in barns, and there was a rifle in the thatch of the Kaczmarek barn that everybody had seen and nobody had seen, and Father Radoń heard confessions for four hours on a Saturday and never once asked a question that had an answer in a court of law.

In 1942 the Germans put up a placard at the crossroads, in Polish and German, saying that any person who gave shelter to a Jew would be shot, together with his family.

---

There had been twenty-six Jewish families in Wierzbica before the war. There had been the Boruchs, who had the shop by the crossroads and sold herrings and paraffin and bootlaces; there had been the Finkelsztejns, who had the grain; there had been old Jakub Icek, who mended pots and told stories and had once given Zofia a boiled sweet for carrying his tools. In 1940 they were moved into two houses at the end of the village and made to wear armbands. In 1941 the Boruchs' shop was taken and given to a Pole from the next parish, and Zofia's mother cried about it in the pantry where nobody could see, and said, "God forgive us, we are all going to hell on a cart."

In August 1942 the Germans came at dawn with lorries and took them away. Zofia saw it from the wood, from the edge of the trees where she had gone to gather mushrooms. She saw the lorries, and the soldiers, and the line of people in the road with their bundles — the old pot-mender, and the Boruchs' daughter who had been in her class at school for two years, and a boy of six who was carrying a pillow. They were put into the lorries, and the lorries went east, and the village was silent for three days.

Nobody spoke about it. That was the thing she could never explain afterwards, to the Party instructors in Kielce or to anyone else: that it had happened in the full light of morning, and that afterwards the village went on, and that the silence was not even a decision, it was a kind of weather.

But a farmer called Wojtek Pilarz had hidden the Finkelsztejn boy in his barn, and had fed him for two weeks, and in October the boy was found, and the Germans shot Pilarz in his own yard and the boy in the road, and they made the village come and look, and afterwards Father Radoń said a Mass for the dead of the parish without naming any names, and the whole village came, and nobody confessed to anything, and everybody in that church knew that on the Day of Judgement there would be a great deal to explain.

Olek did not go to the Mass. He stood outside in the cold the whole hour, and afterwards, when she came out, he said, in a voice she had never heard from him: "There are men in the wood who say we should have done something. There are men in the wood who say we couldn't. I don't know which of them is lying." He looked at her. "And I keep thinking — I keep thinking that if it had been you in that road, I would have wanted somebody to come out of the wood."

She took his hand, and it was cold, and she held it.

---

They had, in those years, a room above the church.

The organ loft was reached by a wooden stair from the sacristy, and it had a broken window that let in the wind, and it held the organ, and a bench, and a chest in which Wiktor Zaremba kept the music and the spare candles and, from 1940, a geography book, a history book, and eleven children between the ages of seven and twelve.

The Germans had closed the school. The Poles had opened it again, in the organ loft, at four in the afternoon, on Tuesdays and Fridays. Olek taught. Zofia taught the younger ones their letters. It was illegal, and everyone knew that it was illegal, and the parents paid in eggs, and the children came up the stair with their shoes in their hands so as not to make a noise.

On the twenty-fourth of March 1943 the Germans found out about a school in a village called Stara Wieś, eleven kilometres away, and they shot the teacher, a woman of thirty-four, in front of her class. After that Wiktor Zaremba went to the other members of the parish council and said that the school in the organ loft would continue, and if anybody was afraid, he would take the children himself, and he was sixty-one years old and had never in his life raised his voice. Zofia heard him say it. She was standing in the sacristy with a bucket of water and she heard this dry, precise, small man say, "This is the church, and the church teaches," and she went out and stood in the churchyard and shook.

Six weeks later they took him.

It was the wireless. He had a wireless in the attic that he had built himself in 1938, and he listened to London in the evenings with a towel under the door, and somebody told somebody. They came at night, four of them, and they took him and the wireless, and they beat him in the yard of the manor for two hours, and Aniela stood at the gate with her hands folded and her lips moving, and the village stood in its doorways and watched, and nobody moved.

They let him go after nine days. He walked home from Kielce, eleven kilometres, with two broken ribs and a kidney that had been kicked until it stopped working properly, and he went to bed, and he died on the fourth of August 1943, and his son was not there, because the Germans had taken forty men from the district to dig trenches and Olek was one of them.

He came back in September. His mother had buried his father in the churchyard with the money from selling the bees. He went into the empty house, and then into the church, and up into the loft, and he sat down at the organ and put his hands on the keys and did not play anything for a long time.

Zofia came up the stair at dusk and sat beside him on the bench and said nothing. The wind came in through the broken pane. Down below, in the dark church, the sanctuary lamp made its small red stitch in the black.

"I keep thinking about the towel," he said at last. "Under the door. He put a towel under the door for five years and I used to laugh at him for it. I used to think, Papa, do you think the Gestapo can't hear through a towel." He put his face in his hands. "He was afraid of the dark. Did you know that? All my life he got up in the night and lit a lamp to go down the passage. And they had him in a cellar for nine days. In the dark."

"Olek."

"And I wasn't here."

"You were in a trench in the rain with forty men and a guard."

"I know where I was." He looked at her, and his face was wet, and his eyes were the eyes she had known since she was four years old, and there was something in them now that had not been there before, something set, like a bone that has healed crooked. "Zosia. If I had stayed, do you know what I would have done? Nothing. I would have stood at the gate with my hands folded, like my mother. Like the whole village. Like everybody in this country except about four thousand people." He was quiet. "I don't want to be good. I want to be at the gate with a rifle."

"Then be at the gate with a rifle," she said. "But not tonight. Tonight sit here with me and let me hold you."

And he did.

---

That winter they nearly.

It was the twelfth of December 1943, and they had been to a wedding at the next parish, and they were walking back in the dark along the road from the mill, and it was snowing, and the snow made the whole world soft and close, and they were both a little drunk on the wedding vodka. They stopped under the willow. There was ice at the edge of the river, and the tree was bare, and the snow was already filling the shape of the roots.

She was twenty-one. He was twenty-one. She had spent four years being a woman in a village under occupation, which is a course of instruction in what a body is worth and who may take it. She had been grabbed in the street by a German sergeant and had bitten his hand and got away, and she had been told by her mother that a girl who is not careful is a girl who ends up in a ditch, and she had decided, somewhere in the middle of the war, that she was going to have one thing in her life that was hers.

"Zosia," he said. "I don't know what's going to happen. I don't know if there's going to be a next winter. And I—"

"I know," she said.

"I would marry you tomorrow. I would marry you in the sacristy with the whole village outside and Radoń's bad ear and—"

"Then marry me."

"Zosia. If they take me, you're a widow at twenty-two. If they take you—"

"Take me," she said. "Take me now, and whatever comes after, at least there's a night that was ours and nobody gave it to us."

He took her face in his hands. She could feel him shaking. And then he said, "No."

"Olek—"

"Not like this. Not in a ditch under a tree with the snow coming down and a rifle in the wood that might be mine or might be theirs." He pressed his forehead to hers. "I want to marry you in the church with my father's organ playing, and I want Father Radoń to be able to hear it, and I want your mother to cry and your brother to get drunk and make a fool of himself. I want it in daylight. I want it to be a thing that happened, not a thing we did in the dark."

She was angry. She was so angry that she could not speak. And then she understood that he was giving her the only thing he had that the war had not taken, which was a picture of the future, and that if he had taken her under the willow that night the picture would have died and they would have had a memory instead, and a memory is a small cold thing to live on.

"All right," she said. "In daylight."

"In daylight," he said. "When it's over."

She put her cold hand inside his coat and held it against his chest, and he held her until the snow was in their hair.

---

In July 1944 the machine came back.

It came from the east this time, and it was the Russians, and the Germans went past Wierzbica in the night, going west, in a long slow river of horses and carts and men on foot and men in lorries, and there were wounded in the carts, and one of them was a boy of sixteen who died in the road at the crossroads and was left there. The village woke up on the twenty-eighth of July and there was no German administration, and Kling had gone with his boots and his shotgun, and the manor stood open with its drawers pulled out.

That morning the men came out of the wood.

There were eleven of them, and they had rifles and armbands, and they were going to Kielce to join the concentration of forces, and they were going to take Olek with them. She was in the yard when he came to say goodbye. His mother was at the door. He had a haversack and a rifle that was too long for him and a face she had never seen before, a face that was terrible and happy, and she understood in that moment that he had been waiting four years for permission to do something.

"Warsaw," he said. "They say Warsaw." He gripped her arms. "It's starting. The Rising. The whole city. The boys in Kielce are going."

"Warsaw is a hundred and sixty kilometres away."

"We'll get there."

"Olek."

"Zosia." He was not listening, and he was listening to nothing else. "This is it. This is the thing. I've been standing at a gate with my hands folded for four years—"

"Your father—"

"My father listened to London with a towel under the door," he said. "I'm going to be the towel."

She hit him. She hit him in the chest with both fists, and he let her, and then she stopped, and he held her, and he was shaking all over like a horse.

"I'll come back up the road," he said. "You said you'd be at the gate."

"I'll be at the gate."

"Plant the willow."

"There's already a willow, you idiot."

"Plant another one," he said, and laughed, and then he was crying, and then he let go of her and went out of the yard to where the eleven men were waiting in the road with their rifles and their terrible happiness, and she stood at the gate with her hands at her sides and watched him go up the road and over the crest, and he turned once at the top and lifted his arm, and then he was gone.

She stood there for a long time. Her mother came out and said, "Come in and eat," and she said, "In a minute," and her mother said, "Zosia," and she said, "In a minute," and her mother went in.

At dusk she went down to the river with a spade and cut a withy from the old willow and planted it in the soft ground by the water, and pressed the earth down with her hands, and said, out loud, to nobody, "There. Now come back."

---

The Germans came back on the fourth of August.

There were three lorries and a half-track, and they came up the road at eight in the morning and stopped in the square by the church, and everybody in Wierzbica knew what it meant, because everybody in that district had heard about the villages in the hills behind Kielce, and because there had been a placard in Kielce for a year saying that the families of bandits would be destroyed.

They took forty men out of the church and shot them against the wall of the cemetery, and they shot Szymon's son, who was nineteen, and the schoolmaster from Stara Wieś who had been hiding in his sister's house, and they put the village into the church, and then they set fire to the houses.

Zofia was in the church with her mother and her sister Hanka, who was fourteen, and two hundred other people, and through the narrow windows they watched the thatch go up. The straw burned fast and the smoke was white and then black, and the bell fell out of the tower at some point in the afternoon and nobody ever found out how, and the church got so hot that the candles bent.

It was the church that saved them; that was the thing she could never say afterwards without her voice going strange. The Germans did not burn the church. They might have; they had burned churches elsewhere, with people in them. But this was a stone church from 1683 with a painting of the Mother of God that people came from three parishes to see, and a Hauptmann stood in the doorway at four in the afternoon and looked at two hundred Poles and said, in bad Polish, "The guilty have been punished. The innocent may go to the forest."

They went out into the smoke. And there, in the square, in the ash, stood her mother's house and her mother's yard and her mother's henhouse and everything her mother had, and her mother stood and looked at it and said, quietly, "Well."

That was when the second group came up the road. They were not regular soldiers. They were men in odds and ends of uniform, and they had a list, and the list had names on it, and Zofia's brother Antek was on it, and the men took Marianna Kędzierska by the arm and asked her where her son was, and she said she did not know, and they asked again, and she said, "I do not know," and the man hit her in the face with the back of his hand, and she fell, and got up, and said it a third time.

They shot her in the yard of her own house.

Zofia was nine metres away with her sister's hand in hers. She heard it. She saw her mother's kerchief come off and lie in the ash, and she saw the ash move in the wind, and then somebody grabbed her from behind and she was on the ground with her mouth full of soot and a man's knee in her back, and Hanka was screaming, and there was a long time — she never knew how long, a minute, an hour — in which the world was only noise and grit and her own blood in her mouth.

When they let her up, they put her in the lorry with Hanka and eleven other women. They put a girl of fifteen from the next farm in with them, and she had been shot in the leg and she died in the lorry before they reached Kielce and they put her out at the roadside.

Zofia did not see her mother again. She did not see her father, who was in the wood, or Antek, who was in the wood, or the church, or the village, or the willow. The lorry turned left at the crossroads and she looked back and the whole valley was smoking, one column, going up straight in the still air, and the last thing she saw of Wierzbica was the tower of St Bartholomew's above the smoke, and the ash coming down on the fields like grey snow in August.

---

They were four days in the cattle trucks, and then they were in a factory at the edge of Leipzig, in a compound of six wooden barracks with a wire fence and a watchtower, and they made parts for aeroplanes from six in the morning until six in the evening, and there were three hundred women in that compound from Poland and Russia and France, and the food was a bowl of soup and two hundred grammes of bread, and in January the dysentery came.

Hanka died on the nineteenth of January 1945, at twenty minutes past six in the evening. She was fifteen years old. She had been in the camp for five months and four days, and she had spent the last nine of them in a bunk with her sister's arms around her, and she had asked, in the last two days, for their mother.

Zofia had prayed for nine hours. She had prayed the rosary her mother had given her, all fifteen mysteries, and then again, and then she had run out of mysteries and prayed without words, and she had made a promise to God in the dark of that barracks that she would do anything, anything at all, that she would go to Mass every day of her life and give everything she had to the poor and never ask for anything again, if He would let Hanka live until morning.

At six o'clock in the evening, Hanka said, "Zosia, it's dark."

Zofia said, "It's all right. I'm here."

And at twenty past six, Hanka stopped breathing, and Zofia held her for a long time, and then she got up and went out into the snow and stood in the middle of the compound, and looked up at a sky that had nothing in it at all, and said — out loud, in Polish, so that there could be no confusion about who was being addressed — "Then I'm not yours any more."

She was twenty-two years old.

In April the Americans came, and the wire came down, and the women went out onto the road in a crowd, and Zofia walked west for a week and then east for four weeks, and she came back into Poland in the last days of May 1945, in the back of a Russian lorry, with a tin mug and a photograph and a wooden rosary, and no sister, and no mother, and no house, and one thing left in the world that she had never once doubted.

She did not know it yet, but that thing had also gone.

---

# Chapter Four — What the Living Do

## Kielce, May 1945

The repatriation point was in the railway yards, in a shed with a concrete floor and a stove made out of a petrol drum, and there were four hundred people in it and every one of them had a story that had cost them everything to be able to tell.

Zofia had been in the shed for two days. She had a tin mug, a photograph, a wooden rosary, and a piece of paper with her name on it typed by an American clerk in Leipzig, and she had lice, and she had a cough that had been in her chest since February and would be in her chest for the rest of her life. She weighed forty-one kilogrammes. Her hair had come out in handfuls in the winter and was growing back in a soft dark pelt, and she had two fingers on her left hand that were dead white to the second knuckle and would never again be warm.

She sat on the concrete with her back against the wall and watched the door and did not ask anyone for anything. She had learned in the camp that the people who asked for things were the people who died first, and the people who waited with their hands still were the ones who were still there in the morning.

On the third day a woman came in.

She was not tall and not handsome. She was about forty, in a grey coat that had been good three years ago and boots that had been good ten, and she had a leather satchel across her body and a red-and-white armband, and she moved through that shed the way a farm wife moves through a henhouse, without hurry and without any doubt about her right to be there. She had a clipboard. She stopped in front of Zofia and looked down at her, and her eyes were the colour of weak tea and entirely without pity, and Zofia, who had been looked at by a great many men in the last nine months, understood at once that this was a different kind of looking.

"You're Kędzierska," the woman said. "Zofia. From Wierzbica."

"Yes."

"Twenty-three. Factory at Leipzig. Sister died in the camp." She was reading from the clipboard. "Your brother was Antoni Kędzierski, pseudonym 'Sokół,' killed in the forest at Wygoda in September."

Zofia's mouth was dry. "How do you know that?"

"Because I asked." The woman crouched down, which put her face level with Zofia's, and Zofia saw that she was tired to the bone, and that she had done this a hundred times, and that she would do it a hundred more. "My name is Wanda Sroka. I'm from the Voivodeship Committee. Do you want some soup?"

Zofia looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "Yes."

---

The soup was a thin barley soup with a piece of potato in it and no salt. Zofia ate it slowly, with both hands round the bowl, and Wanda Sroka sat on the floor beside her with her legs out in front of her like a man, and smoked a cigarette and did not say anything for a while, which Zofia would remember later as the single most intelligent thing anybody did for her that year.

"Now," Wanda said at last. "What are you going to do?"

"Go home."

"Home to what?"

"To my father. If he's alive."

"He's alive. He's in Wierzbica, in a shed, and he has a cough worse than yours and he's been digging graves for the district since November. There are four hundred and eleven people in that parish and there are two hundred and sixty left." She tapped ash off her cigarette. "The manor's empty. The mill's burned. The Germans took the bell out of the church and the Russians took the horses, and the only thing in that valley that is still standing is the church, because it's stone and God looks after stone."

Zofia said nothing.

"You don't believe that."

"I believe that stone is stone."

"Good," said Wanda Sroka, and looked at her with a flicker of something that might have been approval. "Then we can talk."

---

She walked home.

It took her two days. She hitched a lift on a Russian lorry as far as Jędrzejów and then walked, and the road was full of people walking in both directions, and the fields were full of women digging, and in one village there was a sign in Polish and Russian saying that the land belonged to the peasants, and in another village there was a sign saying that the enemies of the people would be punished, and in a third there was no sign at all, only a burned church and a dog sitting in the road.

She came up over the crest on the twenty-ninth of May, in the late afternoon, and saw the valley.

She had prepared herself. She had spent a month in a lorry preparing herself. It did no good. The village was not a village; it was a shape. You could see where the houses had been, because the grass was greener there, and there were cellars open to the sky like mouths, and the church stood in the middle of it with its roof on and one wall of the tower blackened, and around the church the graves in the cemetery were marked with new wooden crosses, dozens and dozens of them, and some of them had names burned into the wood with a nail, and some of them had nothing.

Her father was in the cemetery, digging.

Tomasz Kędzierski was fifty-two years old and looked seventy. He had been a big man; he was not a big man now. He was standing in a half-dug grave with his shirt off, and his back was a map of everything the war had done to him, and when he saw her he put down the spade and climbed out and stood there with his hands hanging, and said, "Zofia."

She said, "Papa."

He did not embrace her. That was not a thing that happened in that family, or perhaps it was a thing the war had taken. He stood and looked at her and then he said, "Your mother's over there. Third row. Hanka?"

"She died in January."

He nodded slowly, as though she had told him something he had already worked out for himself, on his own, in the shed, in the winter. "I thought so. I felt it." He wiped his hands on his trousers. "There's a girl from the next farm who came back in March and she told me. She said Hanka was in the ground at Leipzig and she didn't know where you were. So." He picked up his spade again and then put it down again. "Well. You're here. That's something."

"That's something," Zofia said.

They lived in the shed. It had been a pig shed and now it had a stove, two planks for a bed, and a window that her father had made out of a car windscreen. He had salvaged the stove from the mill and the door from the manor and the table from a German lorry, and he had built the shed with his own hands, and he was proud of it in a way that Zofia found unbearable and understood completely.

On the second night she went down to the river.

The old willow was still there. It had been burned on one side, and the burned side was black and smooth as iron, and the other side had put out leaves in May, absurdly green, and there was a hollow at the base big enough to sit in. And eight metres downstream, in the soft ground by the water, the withy she had planted the day Olek went away had taken root and was a sapling as tall as her shoulder, with silver-green leaves moving in the last of the light.

She stood and looked at it for a long time. Then she sat down in the hollow of the old tree and put her arms around her knees, and did not cry, because she had not been able to cry since January and had begun to think that the machine inside her that did that had been broken along with everything else.

---

Aniela Zarembowa was living in Kielce, in one room above a shoemaker's shop on Wesoła Street, which was the same room her son had boarded in before the war. She was sixty and her hair had gone white and she sewed for a living and her hands were as quick as ever. When Zofia came up the stairs and stood in the doorway, Aniela looked at her for a long moment and then said, "Oh, child," and put her arms round her, and that was the first time anyone had held her since the lorry.

"Sit down. Sit down. Is that your coat? Take it off, it's not the weather for it." She was moving about the room, putting water on, cutting bread. "Your father's alive? Good. Good. And Hanka—" She stopped. "Ah."

"Yes."

"Then sit, and I'll tell you about mine." Aniela put the bread in front of her and sat down opposite and folded her hands. "Wiktor is in the churchyard, you know that. And Olek is alive."

The room went very still.

"He came home in April," Aniela said. "He walked from Częstochowa. He was in the Rising, in Warsaw, from the first week. He was wounded on the second of September in the Old Town, in the side, and they took him out through the sewers and he was in a German hospital for four months. He was in the hospital when the Russians came." She said the word *Russians* without any colour at all. "He came home in April and he sat in that chair where you're sitting and he told me he'd been in the hospital, and that a priest had come to him, and that he had made a promise to God."

Zofia's hands were flat on the table.

"A promise," she said.

"He's going into the seminary. In Kraków. In September."

"He's — Aniela. He's twenty-three years old."

"He's twenty-three years old and he has been in a rising and a hospital and a war, and so have you, and so have we all." Aniela's voice was not unkind. It was the voice of a woman who had buried her husband and made her peace with God in instalments. "Do you think I want it? Do you think I want my son in a seminary, in a country like this, with the Russians in it? I wanted grandchildren. I wanted a girl in this kitchen with flour on her hands." She looked at Zofia, and there was something in her look that was almost terrible. "But I saw him when he came home. He was — Zofia, he had nothing in him. Nothing. Not grief, even. Grief is a thing a person has. He had nothing, and then he came back from that hospital with this, and it was like a man who has been in the water and found a plank. I am not going to take the plank away from my son."

"Where is he?"

"He's at the parish. He's been helping Father Radoń since April. He's there now." She paused. "He asked about you. In April, and in May. He wrote to the Red Cross twice."

Zofia stood up so fast her chair went over.

"Child—"

"Thank you," Zofia said. "Thank you for the bread."

---

He was in the churchyard, in the long light, cutting the grass around the new crosses with a sickle.

He was thin. He was thinner than she had ever seen him, and there was a way he held himself that was careful, as though something in his middle were still not healed, and his hair had been cut short and badly by somebody who did not know how to cut hair. He was wearing a black jacket that had belonged to his father. He heard her on the gravel and looked up, and for a moment there was nothing in his face at all.

Then he said, "Zosia," and dropped the sickle, and stood.

They did not run to each other. She would think about that afterwards — that they did not run. They walked, quickly, and then they were standing a metre apart and neither of them could move, and he said again, "Zosia," and his voice went to pieces on the second syllable.

"You're alive," she said.

"I'm alive."

"You went to Warsaw."

"I went to Warsaw."

"You're going to be a priest."

He closed his eyes. Then he opened them. "Yes."

They stood in the churchyard among the wooden crosses, in the smell of cut grass, and it was the most beautiful evening of that summer, and somewhere a blackbird was going mad in the elder bushes, and Zofia felt something in her chest turn over and settle, and she knew, with an absolute clarity that she would never be able to explain to anyone, that this was the worst hour of her life — worse than the yard, worse than the lorry, worse than the barracks in January — because it was the first hour in which she had allowed herself to hope.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

He told her about Warsaw: the barricades, the sewers, the girl of fourteen who had carried ammunition in a sack and been shot in the mouth on the third of September; the way the city had burned from the top down, floor by floor, and how from the roof of a house on Długa he had watched a whole street go up like a torch and had thought, with a calm that frightened him, *this is what the end of the world looks like, and it is beautiful, and I am going to die in it.*

He told her about the wound: the piece of shrapnel that had gone into his side on the second of September and the cellar where he had lain for two days with a woman holding a candle and a boy dying in the corner, and the fact that when they carried him out he had stopped breathing twice, and a Russian doctor — no, a Polish doctor, a woman, with a cigarette in her mouth — had put her hand flat on his chest and said, "Not this one, not yet."

He told her about Częstochowa: the hospital in a school, the smell of iodine and wet plaster, the priest who came on Sundays, an old man with a limp, who heard confessions and anointed the dying and who, on the eleventh of December, had sat on the edge of Olek's bed and said, "And you, my boy, what are you going to do with the life they gave back to you?"

And he told her what he had said.

"I said, if I live, I'll give it to God." He was not looking at her; he was looking at the crosses. "And then I lay there for four months and I thought about it. Every day. Not because I was holy, Zosia — I wasn't. I was so frightened I couldn't hold a spoon. I thought about it the way a man thinks about a debt. And then in April I came home and I stood in that valley and there was nothing in it. Nothing. My father in the ground, my mother in one room in Kielce, the village gone, the country gone, my friends in the ground from Warsaw to here, and the Russians in the manor house, and nobody in that whole valley could tell me what any of it had been for." He turned to her then. "And then I went into the church. And it was the only thing that was still standing. And I got down on my knees in front of the altar, and I said, all right. All right. If there's nothing else, then this."

"And me?" she said. "Was I in the ground?"

His face changed. "They told me you were dead."

"Who told you?"

"A woman from Stara Wieś. In April. She said the Kędzierski women were taken on the fourth of August and that the older one was shot in the yard and the two girls went to Germany and died there. She said it as a fact. She had it from her sister." He was very pale now. "I believed her. I believed her because everybody in that valley was dead, and because it was easier to believe that you were dead than to believe that you were alive somewhere and I could not get to you. Do you understand? I put you in the ground with the others, Zosia, because I could not carry you."

"I was in a barracks at Leipzig," she said. "I held my sister while she died. I prayed for nine hours. And then I came home, and the first person who told me anything true in this valley was your mother, and she told me that you were going to Kraków to be a priest."

He said nothing.

"Look at me." She took a step toward him. "Olek. Look at me. I'm not in the ground. I'm here, and I'm alive, and I have nothing, and neither have you, and we're the same person, you said so. You said it under the willow. *Whatever happens, we're the same person.*" Her voice was shaking now and she did not care. "So take it back. Whatever you promised in that hospital, take it back. God has had enough. He's had my mother and my sister and your father and half of Poland. He can spare you."

"Zosia—"

"Take it back."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

He looked at her, and she saw that he was crying, and that he had not cried since Warsaw, and that it was costing him something to stand there.

"Because if I take it back," he said, "then it was all for nothing."

She hit him.

She had hit him once before, in 1938, when he pulled her hair; she had hit him in the chest in July 1944 when he went away. This time she hit him across the face with her open hand, a girl's slap, a thing she had not done since she was eleven, and the sound of it went out across the churchyard, and a woman in the presbytery garden looked up.

He did not put his hand to his face. He stood there and let it happen, and that was the worst of it — that he stood there and let it happen and looked at her with an enormous, patient, unbearable tenderness, the way you look at someone who is grieving.

"You'd rather have a dead girl," she said, "than a living one. Because a dead girl doesn't ask you anything."

"That isn't true."

"It's true. You can be a martyr for a dead girl. You can have a dead girl in a silver frame and a church full of people and it's all very beautiful, and a living girl wants a house and a bed and children and bread on the table, and God forbid anyone should want bread." She was backing away from him now, and her voice was a stranger's voice. "You're a coward. You were a coward at the gate in 1943 and you're a coward now. You couldn't take me under the willow because you wanted it in daylight, and now you can't take me because you want it in heaven."

"Zosia—"

"Don't." She held up her hand. "Don't say my name. You've lost the right to say my name."

She walked out of the churchyard. She walked past the presbytery, past the woman in the garden, down the hill to the road, and she did not run, and she did not look back, and when she got to the bottom of the hill where the road turned and the town was out of sight she stopped and bent over with her hands on her knees and made a sound that had no shape to it.

He did not follow her. She would learn, later, and would never entirely forgive, that he had tried, and that Father Radoń had come out of the church and put his hand on the young man's arm and said, "Not today, my boy. Not today."

---

Two letters on the same day.

Hers, written in Kielce on the third of June, on paper she bought with the money from selling her mother's wedding ring, which had been in her pocket all through Leipzig:

*Olek. I am sorry that I hit you. I am not sorry for anything I said. I am going to my father's house and then I do not know. If you change your mind, write to me at Wierzbica and I will come. If you do not change your mind, do not write, and I will know what that means. But I want you to know that I would have lived in a shed with you. I would have lived in a ditch. I would have eaten bread and salt and been happy. You are the only thing I have ever wanted that was not given to me. — Z.*

His, written in Wierzbica the same night, on the back of a parish bulletin:

*Zosia. I know you will not read this. I am writing it anyway because I have to put it somewhere. I did not choose God over you. I chose God because you were dead, and now that you are alive I do not know what I have done, and I am afraid that I have done something that cannot be undone. If you ever read this, come to the church at Wierzbica and stand at the back on a Sunday and I will know, and I will come down out of the loft, and we will go and live in a shed together. I mean it. God forgive me, I mean it. But I cannot say it to your face because you would believe me, and I do not trust myself to be a man who keeps his word. — A.*

He gave the letter to the blacksmith's boy to take to the post. She posted hers at the station the next morning, and stood and watched the man put it in the sack, and then she went and sat on a bench in the square and waited four hours for a train that she did not get on.

Neither letter arrived.

---

Wanda Sroka's office was on the second floor of the Voivodeship Committee building, and it had a window that looked out over the square, and a map of Poland with the new borders drawn on it in red pencil.

"Sit down," Wanda said. "You look like a woman who has been crying for a week."

"I have."

"Good. It's better out than in." She poured two glasses of tea from a pot on the stove. "I heard about the priest."

Zofia's head came up. "How?"

"Because this is a small city and I have been in it for four months, and because a woman came to the Committee on Monday and said that the Zaremba boy's girl had come back from Germany and there had been a scene in the churchyard." She sat down behind the desk. "Do you want to tell me about it?"

"No."

"All right." Wanda drank her tea. "Then I'll tell you about the world, and you can listen or you can go back to your father's shed, and I want you to understand that I am not going to make a speech at you. I am forty-one years old and I have been in this since 1939 and I have buried two men and a child, and I don't have the strength for speeches."

She set down the glass.

"Your mother was shot in her own yard by men with a list. Your sister died of dysentery in a factory dormitory nine hundred kilometres from here. Your brother was killed in a forest at twenty-four years old, and there are boys like him still in those forests, being hunted by men who were in the same forests with them two years ago. That is what happened to you. Now I'll tell you why it happened, and you can argue with me, and if you argue well I'll respect you more.

"It happened because Poland was a country owned by other people. Owned by the gentry, owned by the colonels, owned by the priests who told your mother that the meek would inherit the earth and that she should be obedient to those set over her, and owned in the end by the Germans, who walked in here in seventeen days because the men who owned the country had spent twenty years making sure that nobody else had anything to fight with. The Church taught your mother to kneel. And she knelt, and she was shot in her own yard, and there was a priest at the next parish who said a Mass for her and then went home to his dinner."

Zofia said, "That isn't fair. Father Radoń—"

"Father Radoń is a good man and he was in the prison at Kielce for seven months and he came back with one ear and he says Mass and he does not speak about anything else. I know. I have his file." Wanda said it without malice. "The Church is not a conspiracy of wicked men. That's the mistake people make. The Church is a beautiful old building full of frightened good men who have been told all their lives that their job is to keep the building standing. And the building is standing. And your sister is in the ground at Leipzig."

Zofia was silent.

"Here is what I am offering," Wanda said. "Not charity. Work. You can read and write and you have a head on you and you have been to the bottom and come back, which is the only education that counts. I can get you a room, a card, and a place at the Party school in Łódź for six months, and then a job. Not a grand job. A clerk's job in the Voivodeship Office, and after that, if you're any good, something better." She leaned forward. "And I will tell you what it costs, because I don't lie to people I'm recruiting. It costs everything you have left. It costs your father, perhaps. It costs whatever is left in you of that church you were baptized in. It costs the priest. And in exchange you get to be in the room where the decisions are made, for the rest of your life, instead of in the yard where the decisions land."

Zofia looked at the map on the wall. The red pencil line. Lwów on the other side of it now, and Wilno, and the villages where her grandmother's people had come from, all of it on the wrong side of a line drawn by men in a room.

"How old were you," she said, "when you stopped believing?"

Wanda Sroka's face did something complicated.

"Nineteen," she said. "In Lwów. There was a pogrom and the priest stood in the doorway of his church and told the people to go home." She stubbed out the cigarette. "I didn't stop believing in God. I stopped believing that God was going to do anything about it. That's a different thing, and it's worse, and it never goes away."

She pushed a form across the desk, and a pen.

Zofia looked at the form. It asked for her name, her date of birth, her parents' names, her occupation, her brother's name. It asked her to state that she accepted the programme and the statutes of the Polish Workers' Party.

She thought about the smell of the barracks at Leipzig. She thought about her mother's kerchief in the ash. She thought about a man in a black jacket, standing in a churchyard with his hand at his side, letting her hit him.

She signed.

The nib was bad and the ink sputtered, and her name came out in her small, upright, government hand: *Zofia Kędzierska.*

"There," said Wanda Sroka, and her voice was not warm, but it was not cold either. "Now. Have you eaten?"

---

She wrote to him three times.

The first in August, from the Party school in Łódź, when she was sleeping eleven hours a night and dreaming about the camp and waking up in a clean bed with her heart going. She wrote four pages. She told him she had joined the Party. She told him she was not sorry. She told him that she had gone to the church at Łódź on a Sunday in July and stood at the back and had not been able to kneel. She told him she missed him with a violence that frightened her, and that she was going to get over it, and that she hoped he would be a good priest, and that she hated him.

The second in December, from Kielce, when she had a room of her own with a key and a window and a chair, and she had begun to be good at her job, and she had begun to understand that she was good at it. This one was three lines.

*Olek. I am still here. I don't know why I keep writing. — Z.*

The third in March 1946, when she heard from a woman from Wierzbica that Aniela Zarembowa had said her son did not want to see anyone from the village again.

*Olek. This is the last one. I am told you don't want to see anyone from the village, so I will stop being from the village. I hope you are well. I hope your mother is well. If you are ever in Kielce, do not come to the Committee, because I would have to offer you tea and we would both be very polite and I would die. Be a good priest. Say a Mass for Hanka sometimes. She was fifteen. — Zofia.*

She posted all three, at the station, in the same way, and waited, in the way of a woman who has not yet learned that the world is not obliged to answer.

She never received a reply.

In the archive of the Security Service at Kielce, in a file opened in September 1945 under the number 41/VI, there was a section headed *Korespondencja* — correspondence — and in that section, eventually, there would be three letters in a small upright hand, and nine in a round boyish hand, and a folder of intercept slips signed by men who are now dead, and none of it would be seen by anyone for twelve years.

# Chapter Five — The Plank

## Kraków, 1945–1950

The seminary was in a building on Podzamcze that had been a monastery in the seventeenth century and had been a barracks under the Austrians and had been, for four years, a store for the German army. When Aleksander Zaremba arrived in September 1945 with a cardboard case and a letter from Father Radoń, the windows on the second floor still had no glass, and the refectory smelled of cabbage and lime, and there were forty-one men in the building for a hundred and ten places, because the war had taken the rest.

He was twenty-three. He was the oldest in his year and the youngest in his heart, and both of these things caused him trouble.

The rector was a canon named Kaczorowski, seventy, who had spent the war in the seminary at Kraków doing what the seminary at Kraków had done, which was to keep forty boys alive and out of the labour roundups, and who had a voice like a door opening. On the third day he took the new men into the chapel and said, "You are here because you think you have been called. Some of you have. Some of you have lost your families, your homes, your health, your faith in the world, and you have come here because this is the only building in Poland that is still standing and you want a roof. Both of those are reasons. Only one of them will hold."

Aleksander wrote that down in his black notebook, in the back, where he had once written Zofia's name eleven times.

He was not a good seminarian. He was obedient, which is not the same thing. He could not get up in the morning; he fell asleep during the meditation at five; he was reprimanded twice for laughing in the corridor and once, seriously, for playing Chopin in the recreation room on a Sunday evening when a professor's mother had just died. He was very good at the liturgy, which surprised him, and very good with the boys from the villages who had come in at sixteen with no Latin and no manners and were terrified, and he taught them grammar at night in the dormitory by the light of a candle stub, and it was the only thing in that building he did without being asked.

And he had doubts. He had doubts the way a man has a limp.

"You have a vocation to the Church," Father Zawadzki told him in the second year, in the little office that smelled of pipe tobacco. Zawadzki was the spiritual director, a small, sharp, ironic man of fifty who had been in Auschwitz for two years and never mentioned it. "That's not the same as a vocation to the priesthood. Do you understand the distinction?"

"No, father."

"A man can love the Church the way a man loves a house he was born in. That is a good thing, and it is not enough, because the house will ask him to die for it and he will want to know why." He tapped his pen on the desk. "You came here out of grief, Zaremba. Grief is a great engine. It burns hot and it burns out, and when it burns out, what's underneath?"

"I don't know, father."

"Neither do I. That's why we're here for six years." He looked at him. "Are you praying?"

"I say the offices."

"That's not what I asked."

Aleksander was silent.

"Say the offices," Zawadzki said. "Say them badly. Say them while you hate them. But once a day, say something in your own words, even if the words are, *I don't want to be here.* God has heard worse from better men."

He was ordained on the twenty-fifth of June 1950, in the church of the Franciscans at Kraków, in a heatwave, with eleven other men. His mother came from Kielce in a borrowed hat and sat in the third row and cried without making any noise, and afterwards, at the reception in the refectory, she took his face in her two hands and said, "Your father would have played the organ so loud the roof would come off," and then she said, "Do you know what I pray for? I pray that you never have to choose between God and a person. Because I did, and it's not a thing a body should be asked to do twice."

He thought she was talking about herself. It was eight years before he understood that she was talking about him.

---

## Radoszyce, 1950–1952

His first parish was at Radoszyce, forty kilometres from Kielce, in the hills. There were nine hundred souls in the village and three thousand in the parish and two other priests, and the pastor was sixty-six and had been in Dachau and had a temper like a wasp. There was a wooden church with a miraculous image of the Mother of God, and a presbytery with dry rot, and a pig, and a bicycle with no brakes.

He was happy there. He would say so, later, to the men who came to interrogate him, and they wrote it down and thought he was being clever.

He taught the catechism to two hundred children in a room that had been the parish granary. He heard confessions for three hours on a Saturday. He learned to preach in a way that made people come back on Sunday evening, which the pastor said was vanity and which he did anyway. He started a library of sixty books in a cupboard, mostly novels, because a boy of seventeen in a village that has no books will read anything, and if he reads anything he may read everything. He bicycled to the outlying villages with the Blessed Sacrament in a tin box in his pocket in winter, through the snow, to the sick, and once fell off the bicycle into a ditch and lay there in the dark laughing at the sky.

And he did not forget her. That is the thing that no one tells you about a vocation, he would think later: it does not replace anything. It adds.

In the summer of 1946 he had gone to Kielce.

He had gone with a letter in his pocket that he had written and rewritten eleven times, and he had walked from the station up to the square, and he had stood at the corner of Wesoła Street and looked at the Voivodeship Committee building, and he had waited for two hours in the sun. And at four in the afternoon the door had opened and she had come out.

She was with three other people, one of them the woman in the grey coat, and she was wearing a good blue dress and her hair was long again and pinned up, and she was carrying a folder under her arm, and she was laughing at something one of the men had said — laughing the way she laughed, with her head back and her whole face, the way she had laughed at Szymon's story about the priest and the goose in the hayfield in 1938. She looked, in that moment, entirely alive, and entirely at home in the world, and entirely someone else's.

He stood at the corner of the street with his hand in his pocket on the letter, and he did not go to her.

Why? He had asked himself that question for twelve years and had never once come up with an answer he could stand to live with. He was a seminarian in a black coat in a city where the Party was hunting the Church. He had a letter in his pocket and no courage in it. He had believed her dead for nine months and had promised God a life, and the promise had worked, and he was afraid that if he went up to her the promise would come apart in his hands like a wet paper bag, and then what would he have? He would have a girl who had joined the Party and a God who had let the world burn and nothing at all in between.

He told himself it was for her. He told himself that she had built a life and he had no right to knock it down.

But the truth, which he knew then and knew better later, was that he was afraid she would not want him, and he was more afraid of that than of anything the Security Service would ever do to him.

He turned around and walked back to the station and got on the train and went back to the seminary, and he never told anyone. Not Zawadzki, not his confessor, not his mother. He put the letter in the back of his breviary and carried it there for six years until the binding came apart, and then he burned it, and it took a long time to burn because it was folded so many times.

---

The Security Service came for him on the ninth of February 1952, at half past five in the morning.

There were four of them, and a car with its lights off in the lane, and the pastor stood in the hall in his nightshirt and said, "By what right?" and one of them said, "By the right of the Polish People's Republic, father," and the pastor said, "I have never heard of that right," and Aleksander, who was already dressed because he had been up since four with a sick call, said, "It's all right. I'll get my coat."

They took him to Kielce and then to Warsaw, to Rakowiecka Street, and put him in a cell in the basement with three other men, and for the next two years and ten months he was a prisoner of the state.

The charges, when they were finally produced, were these: that he had, in the course of his duties, maintained contact with "reactionary elements"; that he had distributed "illegal publications" (the novels in the cupboard — Mickiewicz, Norwid, a French novel by Bernanos); that he had taught the catechism to children without a permit; and, most seriously, that he had received and passed on information about the activities of the Security Service in the district. That last was nonsense. It was also the only charge that could have got him twelve years, and he understood, with the particular clarity of a man in a cellar, that the point of the charge was not to be true.

For five weeks they left him alone. That is the thing people do not understand about that building: the worst of it is the waiting. He was in a cell of two metres by three with a bucket and a window at the height of a man's chest, painted over so that the only thing it admitted was the colour of the day. He was taken out twice a day for ten minutes to walk in a concrete yard under a net. He was given a kilo of bread every three days and a bowl of soup with a potato in it, and he was not beaten, and nobody spoke to him, and after three weeks he began to talk to himself in order to hear a voice.

The interrogations began in March.

The man who interrogated him was about forty and had a small moustache and a good grey suit and a way of being reasonable that was the most frightening thing Aleksander had ever encountered. His name was Rygier. He offered cigarettes. He spoke about literature. He said that he himself had been in the AK, in the Wilno district, and that he had come to understand that the war had been a tragedy in which everyone had been used, and that a man of intelligence had a duty to help build something in the ruins. He said this with every appearance of sincerity, and it was perhaps even sincere, and that was the horror of it.

"Do you know what I have in this file?" Rygier said, on the fourth day, tapping the folder. "I have a report that you were in the Rising. I have a report that you were wounded in the Old Town. I have a report that your father listened to London for five years and was beaten to death for it. And now I have a young priest in the hills who teaches Mickiewicz to peasants and tells them the state is the Antichrist." He sat back. "Father, I'm going to be frank with you, because I think you're intelligent. I don't care about the books. I don't care about the catechism. I care that you are the kind of man who makes other men brave, and brave men are expensive. So. I want you to make a statement, and then you can go home to your pig."

"I have nothing to state."

"Everyone has something to state." Rygier smiled. "We'll talk again tomorrow."

On the eleventh of March they did not talk.

He did not remember all of it afterwards, and what he remembered came back in pieces for years, in dreams, in the middle of a good afternoon. He remembered being made to stand for eleven hours. He remembered a rubber truncheon and a man's forearm across his throat. He remembered his own wrist against the edge of a table and a sound like a branch breaking, and a white flash, and vomiting. He remembered being asked a name — the name of a catechist, a widow of fifty, Mrs Wójcik, who taught the little ones in her kitchen at Radoszyce — and he remembered thinking, very clearly, with the strange calm of a man at the end of his strength: *they will go to her house and they will take her and she has a bad heart.*

And he remembered signing.

The statement was two pages. It said that he had allowed himself to be used by reactionary elements, that he had acted against the interests of the Polish People's Republic, that he regretted it, and that he undertook to abstain from all such activity in future, and to inform the authorities of any attempt to draw him into such activity. He read it once. He could not read it twice. They put a pen in his left hand because his right was in a splint, and he wrote his name at the bottom in a round boyish hand, and the signature came out crooked, and it took him three tries to make the pen go where he wanted.

Then they took him back to his cell and gave him a bowl of soup, and he was sick, and then he lay on the plank and looked at the ceiling.

He was a priest. He had been ordained for twenty months. He had, in that time, anointed four dying people and heard perhaps two thousand confessions and held a newborn child over the font, and he had believed — with the specific, unexamined, structural belief of a man who has been in a seminary since he was twenty-three — that whatever else happened to him, there were things he would not do.

And now he had done one of them. And the worst part was not the shame, and it was not the fear, and it was not even the knowledge that Mrs Wójcik might be arrested anyway.

The worst part was that it had been easy. It had not even been a decision. There had been a light, and then there had been the pen, and in between there had been nothing at all, no choice, no resistance, no moment in which he could have said *no* — only a body that wanted the light to stop and a hand that had taken the pen. He had believed that he was a man with a floor in him. There was no floor. There was only the dark going down and down.

He did not pray for four months. He said the office because he had said it every day since he was twenty-three, and because the saying of it was the only structure left in the world, and he said it the way a man in a cellar counts the bricks. But he did not pray. He did not think he had the right.

It was an old man in the cell next to his who brought him back. He never saw his face. He was a former officer of the AK, a man of sixty, who had been in the building for four years, and who sang, very quietly, every evening at nine, a hymn that Aleksander had known since he was a child: *Serdeczna Matko, opiekunko ludzi.* And one night in July, when the heat in the cell was so bad that men were fainting in the yard, Aleksander heard the old man's voice through the wall, going on with the third verse, and he found that his own mouth was moving.

He never spoke to him. In November the old man was taken away and Aleksander heard, from a guard who had been a farm boy from Opatów and was not unkind, that he had died in the prison hospital of a heart attack at the age of sixty-one.

He said a Mass for him in his own mind, in the dark, without bread or wine, and wept for the first time since the Rising.

---

He was released on the twenty-second of December 1954, under an amnesty, in a coat that did not fit him, with a paper saying he had been "rehabilitated," which was the word they used for the thing that could not be undone. He had been in the building for two years, ten months, and thirteen days.

His mother was waiting for him at the gate in the snow. She had come from Kielce with a thermos of tea and a pair of gloves. She was sixty-nine and she had aged ten years, and she put her arms round him and held him for a long time, and then she said, "Well. You look terrible," and he laughed and could not stop laughing, and the guard at the gate looked at them both with an expression of profound fatigue and went back inside.

He spent two months at a sanatorium at Otwock for his lungs, where he read a great deal and slept badly and was, for the first time in his life, seriously tempted by the idea of leaving. Not leaving the priesthood. He had passed through the place where that was possible somewhere in the winter of 1953 and had come out the other side; the Church was the only thing he had, and it was not a plank any more, it was a floor, though he could not have said on what it was resting. What tempted him was something smaller and worse: the idea that he might be a small, harmless, diocesan priest in a village, saying Mass, burying the dead, keeping his head down, and never again in his life being asked to be brave.

He was thirty-two years old. It would have been so easy.

In April 1955 Bishop Julian Rożek sent for him.

## Warsaw, 1955–1958

Rożek was seventy-one then, the retired auxiliary of Sandomierz, living in two rooms in a canonry on Miodowa Street, half blind, with a mind like a filing cabinet. He had ordained Aleksander in 1950 and had followed his case through the years of his imprisonment the way a man follows a chess game in a newspaper, and when Aleksander came into the room he said, without preamble, "You signed a statement. Tell me about it."

Aleksander told him.

The old man listened with his hands folded and his eyes closed, and when it was over he said, "Was it true?"

"No."

"Was any of it true?"

Aleksander thought about it for a long time. "There was a sentence. *I allowed myself to be used by reactionary elements.* There were men in the wood at Wierzbica who had rifles and no plan, and I carried messages for them when I was nineteen, and I did not ask what the plan was, and some of them were brave and some of them were fools and one of them, I think, was a murderer. So. In a way that would satisfy no court and no historian, there was a sentence in it that I could not say was false."

"Hm," said Rożek. "That's a dangerous kind of honesty. It will get you into trouble and it is the only kind worth having." He opened his eyes. "You will be given a parish in Warsaw. St Kazimierz, in Żoliborz. It is a difficult parish — the Security Service has a man in the third house on the corner and there are three informants among the church council, and the vicar is twenty-three and will need watching. You will not sign anything. You will not write anything. You will not speak to foreigners, and if a foreigner speaks to you, you will be extremely boring. You will say Mass, hear confessions, teach the children, visit the sick, and bury the dead. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Excellency."

"And you will not be a martyr." The old man's voice was suddenly sharp. "I have buried eleven priests since the war, and eight of them were martyrs, and the Church in this country does not need any more martyrs, it needs men who will be alive in twenty years. Do you understand the difference?"

"I think so."

"You do not," said Bishop Rożek. "But you will."

---

St Kazimierz was a red-brick church of 1888 in a district of Warsaw that had been, in 1944, a field of rubble with one wall standing, and had been rebuilt by people who had come back to nothing. The presbytery was cold. The parish was seven thousand souls, of whom perhaps nine hundred came to Mass, and of whom perhaps forty came every day, and those forty were mostly women over sixty, and they ran the place like a farm.

His housekeeper was Pani Bronisława, sixty, widowed in the Rising, who cooked badly and disapproved of him in a motherly way and had a nephew in the militia whom she mentioned at intervals, like a woman showing you a photograph of a dog that might bite.

His vicar was Father Tadeusz Rybak, twenty-three, from a village near Płock, with a face like a calf and a spine of piano wire. Tadeusz taught the catechism to ninety children in the parish hall, and to twenty more in a private flat on Felińskiego Street on Thursday afternoons, which was illegal, and which he did not consider illegal, and which he had explained to Aleksander on his first day in the parish with the words, "Father, they closed the schools to us in '56 and opened them again in '56 and closed them again this year, and I have children in that flat whose parents were in the Rising, and if the state wants to arrest me for teaching a nine-year-old the Ten Commandments then the state had better send a bigger car."

Aleksander had looked at him for a moment. Then he had said, "The car is a Warszawa M-20 and it holds four men. Two of them will stand at the door. The third will take you out by the arm and the fourth will carry the register, so burn the register in the stove before you open the door, and do not open the door after eight in the evening, because after eight the neighbours are asleep and nobody will see them take you." He paused. "And, Tadeusz. If they take you, say nothing at all for the first three days. Nothing. Not your name. They will hurt you on the second day and you will want to give them something to make it stop, and if you give them nothing on the second day then on the third day you will find that you are a different man."

Tadeusz stared at him.

"Was it like that for you, father?"

"No," said Aleksander. "It was worse."

He was, by the standards of that parish and that city, a good priest. He was not a saint and he knew it. He was impatient with pious ladies, he smoked too much, he had a temper he kept in a box, and he had a habit of going very quiet in the middle of an argument which people found more frightening than shouting. But he was good in the confessional, because he had learned in a cellar that there is nothing a man can tell you that has not been done before by someone who was frightened; and he was good with the young, because he did not lie to them; and he was good with the dying, because he had been in the hospital at Częstochowa and had watched a boy die in a corner while a woman held a candle, and he was no longer afraid of that room.

And he was careful, in the way Bishop Rożek had meant. He did not sign the declarations that came round every spring from the Office for Religious Affairs. He did not speak at the Front of National Unity. He did not write to anyone in the West. Twice a year a man from the Office came to the presbytery and sat in the good chair and asked him questions about the catechism and the youth, and Aleksander gave him tea and answered every question truthfully and none of them usefully, and the man went away and wrote a report.

He did not know that the reports went to a woman he had once loved. He did not know that the file he was building, month by month, in a cabinet on Krucza Street, was being read by someone who knew the exact shape of the scar on his left forearm from a burn he had got at the age of nine, stealing honeycomb.

He thought about her. Less often as the years went on, and then, in the autumn of 1956, when the prisoners came home and the newspapers said things they had not said for ten years and everyone in Warsaw talked at once, he thought about her every day for a month and nearly telephoned, and did not, because a priest does not telephone a woman in the Central Committee.

---

The bishop came to him in September 1958.

Bishop Rożek was seventy-four now and had had a stroke in the spring, and he came up the presbytery stairs one step at a time with a stick, and refused help, and sat in the good chair and said, "The See of Sandomierz has been vacant for nineteen months. Rome is losing patience. The Chapter has sent a *terna* and the Episcopate has added a name, and the name is yours."

Aleksander put down the teapot.

"That's not possible."

"It is entirely possible. It is also unlikely, which is a different thing. You are thirty-six years old, you have been a priest for eight years, you have been in prison, and you are — I say this without pleasure — the most interesting priest in this diocese under fifty." The old man's mouth twitched. "There has not been a bishop of thirty-six in Poland since the eighteenth century. There has not been one at all, so far as I can discover, in the history of the Polish episcopate, and I have looked."

"Excellency, I am the parish priest of a church with a leaking roof in Żoliborz."

"You are the parish priest of a church with a leaking roof in Żoliborz because I put you there to see whether you would survive it. You have survived it. The youth come to you. The workers come to you. You do not sign things and you do not make speeches and when Rygier's people arrested the woman who taught the infants in your parish in February you went to the prosecutor's office yourself, in your cassock, and sat in the corridor for six hours, and she was released in nine days. Do you think nobody noticed?"

"That was a woman of sixty with a bad heart."

"Yes," said Bishop Rożek. "That is exactly what a bishop is. A man who sits in a corridor for six hours for a woman of sixty with a bad heart. God has been trying to teach you this since 1952 and you are a slow learner."

Aleksander got up and went to the window. Outside, two boys were playing football against the wall of the church with a ball made of rags, and a woman was carrying a cabbage in a string bag, and the sky over Warsaw was the colour of a knife.

"I don't want it," he said.

"Nobody wants it. That is the first qualification." The bishop's voice softened. "Aleksander. Sit down. I am going to tell you the truth, and then you are going to say yes, and I want you to understand both halves of it.

"The truth is that the Party does not want you and cannot simply refuse you. If they refuse you, Rome appoints you anyway or appoints worse, and there is a scandal, and the Primate is still weak from three years in Stoczek and does not want a scandal. So they will consider you. They will read your file. They will look for a way to use you, or to break you, or to make you refuse the nomination yourself, which would be the tidiest outcome of all. And they will send someone to talk to you. Someone clever. And that person may have more power over you than any bishop in Poland, and you must be ready for it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you are going to say yes," said Bishop Rożek, "and then I am going to die, and there will be no one left to tell you the truth."

The letter came on the fourth of October, in a brown envelope with the crest of the Polish People's Republic and a stamp that said *Urząd do Spraw Wyznań.*

It was three sentences. It requested that Father Aleksander Zaremba present himself at the Office for Religious Affairs, ul. Krucza, room 214, on Thursday the ninth of October at eleven o'clock, in connection with the vacancy of the See of Sandomierz.

It was signed, for the Director, in a small upright hand:

*Zofia Kędzierska, Deputy Director.*

# Chapter Six — Room 214

## Warsaw, 9 October 1958

He read the letter four times.

Then he put it on the table, face up, and went and stood at the window of the presbytery study, and looked out at the yard where Pani Bronisława grew tomatoes in a strip of earth against the wall, and said, aloud, in an ordinary voice, "Zosia."

The name had not been in his mouth for thirteen years. It came out small and flat and strange, like a coin from another country.

He had a week. He spent it badly. He said Mass, heard confessions, buried a man of eighty-two who had been a tram conductor before the war and had asked, in September, for the Twenty-Third Psalm at his funeral, and on the Wednesday before the Thursday he took the tram to Miodowa Street and sat in Bishop Rożek's two rooms and told him everything.

The old man listened without moving. When Aleksander had finished, he said, "How long were you in love with her?"

"From 1938 until about a quarter past three on a Thursday in 1946, when I stood at the corner of Wesoła Street and watched her come out of the Voivodeship Committee building and decided that I was too much of a coward to cross the road."

"Hm," said Bishop Rożek. "And since then?"

"Since then I have been a priest."

"That is not an answer. That is a very good sermon." The bishop shifted in his chair; the stroke had left him with a drag in his left side and he hated it visibly. "Aleksander. Listen to me, because I am going to be blunt and I have very little breath. There are two ways this ends. Either she destroys you, and you spend the rest of your life in a parish in the Bieszczady saying Mass for eleven people, and the Church survives it, and you survive it, and in the end it is not a tragedy, only a waste. Or she does not destroy you, and you are made a bishop at thirty-six, and you spend the next thirty years of your life in a room with men who will ask you to sign things, and every one of them will have a file, and one of them will have your file, and the file will be held by a woman who knew you when you were a boy and loved you and has spent twelve years learning how to use a man's past against him." He paused. "Which of those do you want?"

"Neither."

"Then you have understood the job." Bishop Rożek closed his eyes. "Go. Say nothing that is not true. Sign nothing. And remember that she is not your enemy — she is a woman in a room with a file. Those are two different kinds of danger, and the second is worse, because you will want to save her, and a man who wants to save the person holding his file will tell her everything."

---

The Office for Religious Affairs was on the third floor of a building on Krucza Street that had been a bank before the war and had kept, in the entrance hall, a floor of black and white marble that no one had bothered to tear up. There was a porter's desk and a brass directory board and a smell of floor polish and coal, and there was a queue of six people on the stairs, which was how you knew it was a government office and not a bank.

He waited forty minutes on a bench in the corridor with his hat on his knee.

The others on the bench were a Baptist minister from the Praga district with a folder of papers tied with string; a woman of about fifty in black who was holding a paper with a stamp on it and who told him, without being asked, that her son had been refused permission to be married in a church because the girl was under eighteen; and an old priest in a shabby cassock who sat with his eyes closed and his lips moving and who, when Aleksander sat down beside him, opened one eye and said, "Sandomierz?"

"Yes."

"Ah." The old priest closed the eye again. "I was at Sandomierz in 1944. The Germans put a shell through the roof of the cathedral and it landed on the altar and did not go off. The bishop at the time said it was a miracle. The Germans said it was the manufacturer." He sighed. "They will offer you a car. Take the car. God has no opinion about cars."

At eleven o'clock exactly the door at the end of the corridor opened and a woman of fifty in a grey skirt and a brown cardigan came out and said, "Father Zaremba," in the voice of a woman who had said that sentence four hundred times.

The room was not large. There was a desk, a green shaded lamp, a portrait of the First Secretary in an overcoat, a filing cabinet with three drawers and a lock, and a window that looked out on the flank of the building next door and a slice of sky.

There was a coal stove in the corner, and it was smoking slightly.

And behind the desk there was a woman of thirty-six in a dark blue suit with her hair pinned up and a pen in her hand, and she looked up from the file as he came in, and she said, "Father Zaremba. Please sit down."

---

He had prepared himself for this. He had prepared himself for twelve years, in a way, without knowing it: he had imagined the meeting so many times, in so many forms, in the seminary and in the cellar and on the tram, that he had built up a kind of armour of imagined meetings, and the armour was no use at all.

She was thinner than he remembered. That was the first thing. The girl had had a round face and a great deal of colour and a way of holding her chin that made her look as though she were about to argue with God; the woman had cheekbones and a drawn look around the mouth and two deep lines between her brows. Her hands were the same hands, broad and capable, and there was a wedding ring on the left one, and two fingers on that hand were white to the second knuckle, and he saw that and had to look at the wall for a moment.

Her eyes were the same. That was the second thing.

"Thank you for coming," she said. "You are prompt. Most of the clergy are not."

"I was told eleven o'clock."

"You were told eleven o'clock because the man who wrote the letter was told to write eleven o'clock, and I have a meeting at half past eleven, so we have thirty minutes." She opened the file. She did not look at him while she said it. "I am going to ask you a number of questions. Some of them will seem to you intrusive. Some of them will seem to you stupid. I would ask you to answer all of them, and I would ask you to answer none of them with a speech."

"All right."

"Your name."

"Aleksander Zaremba."

"Date of birth."

"Fourth of May, 1922."

"Place."

"Wierzbica, poviat Kielce."

"Father's occupation."

"Organist."

She wrote, in her small upright hand, the way she had written everything since she was seven years old. The pen scratched. The stove ticked.

"Education."

"Primary school at Wierzbica. Gimnazjum at Kielce, 1934 to 1939. Seminary at Kraków, 1945 to 1950."

"Your studies were interrupted."

"Yes."

"By what?"

"By the war. And then by a decision."

She looked up at him then, and he looked back at her, and the room was very quiet, and something passed between them that was not speech and had nothing to do with the Polish People's Republic.

She looked down first.

"Were you a member of the Home Army?"

"Yes."

"From when?"

"1940. I was in the Szare Szeregi from 1940 and in a line unit from 1942. My pseudonym was Ryś. I carried messages, and from 1942 I was in a unit that — " He stopped himself. "I will answer the question. I was in a unit that was trained for sabotage and did not do very much of it, because we had no explosives and very little sense. In July 1944 we were mobilized for the action at Kielce. I went to Warsaw in the first week of August and fought in the Old Town until the second of September, when I was wounded."

The pen had stopped moving.

"You were in the Rising," she said.

"Yes."

"So was half of my department's staff, in one way or another. Most of them have the decency to lie about it." She wrote something. "You were arrested in 1952."

"Yes."

"Charged with?"

"Contact with reactionary elements, distribution of illegal publications, teaching the catechism without a permit, and passing information about the Security Service."

"Were you guilty?"

He considered it. "Of the first, partly, in the sense that any man who was in the AK in 1940 was in contact with reactionary elements in 1940, and I did not stop being in contact with them when they became old men with farms. Of the second, yes, if Bernanos is an illegal publication. Of the third, yes. Of the fourth, no."

"Of the fourth," she said, "you were guilty of nothing at all, and you were held for two years and ten months, and in March 1952 you signed a statement." She turned a page. "Would you like to tell me about the statement?"

He looked at her.

"You have it in front of you," he said.

"I would like to hear you tell me about it."

"Why?"

"Because the file is the version of a man that other men have made," she said. "I am asking for the man's own."

He was silent for a moment. The stove ticked. He heard someone in the corridor laugh and then stop.

"They broke my wrist on the eleventh of March," he said. "They had had me standing for eleven hours. There was a man whose name I never learned, and he had a rubber truncheon, and he was not angry, which is the thing I still dream about. And then an officer called Rygier came into the room and put a piece of paper in front of me and said that if I signed it I could go home to my pig." He paused. "And I signed it. It took three tries to make the pen go where I wanted, because of the wrist."

"What did it say?"

"That I had allowed myself to be used by reactionary elements. That I regretted it. That I would abstain in future. That I would inform the authorities of any attempt to involve me in such activity."

"And did you inform?"

"No."

"Did you abstain?"

"No. I have taught the catechism in this city for three years, illegally, in a flat on Felińskiego Street, and I will go on doing it until they stop me." He said it evenly. "You asked for the man's own version."

She did not write anything down.

"You signed a statement in 1952," she said at last, "and you have not signed anything since. Not the declarations, not the appeals, not the letters of support for the Front, not the protest against the closure of the churches in the Kieleckie. Nothing. And yet you are, according to your own account, a man who signs."

"I was a man who signed once," he said, "and the price was that I understood what I am. I have not been able to afford to understand it twice."

---

She got up and went to the window, and stood with her back to him, and lit a cigarette. He watched the smoke go up into the grey light, and the shape of her shoulders, and remembered her standing exactly like that at seventeen, in the doorway of the organ loft, with the wind coming through the broken pane.

"Father Zaremba," she said, to the window. "I am going to speak to you now as the representative of the state. Not as myself. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"The See of Sandomierz has been vacant for nineteen months. The Holy See wishes it filled. The Episcopate wishes it filled. The state does not object in principle to its being filled, and the state is prepared to look favourably on a candidate who is a Polish patriot, a man of the people, and a priest who understands that this country is engaged in an enormous and difficult work of reconstruction after a war that killed six million of its citizens." She turned around. "There are three names on the list. One of them is a man who has spent the last eight years writing articles about the Church's contribution to socialist construction. He would be appointed tomorrow, and within a year every Catholic in that diocese would know exactly what he was, and the Church would be diminished, and the state would be diminished, because a bishop nobody believes is a bishop nobody obeys.

"The second is an old canon who has never said anything interesting in his life. He would be appointed, and he would be harmless, and he would be forgotten, and the diocese would drift for ten years.

"The third is you." She tapped ash into a saucer. "You are thirty-six years old. You were in the Rising. You were in Rakowiecka for three years. You are loved in Żoliborz by the workers and the students and the old women, and you are respected by the Curia because you have never once made a fool of the Church in public. If you are consecrated, the Church in that diocese will have a bishop who is believed by everybody, for the first time since the war. And the state will have — what?"

"That is the question," he said.

"That is the question. And the answer is not a speech."

He sat with it. Bishop Rożek had said: say nothing that is not true. He looked at her, and she was standing in front of the window with her cigarette, and the light was behind her, and she was waiting, and he understood, quite suddenly, that she was afraid. Not of him. Of the answer.

"If I am consecrated," he said, "the state will have a bishop who will say Mass, and teach, and govern his diocese, and will not conspire against the Polish People's Republic, and will not call for resistance from the pulpit, and will not use the sacraments as a weapon. I will not bless a rising. I will not bless a party either. I will not tell the workers of Sandomierz to strike and I will not tell them to vote. I will tell them what the Church has always told them, which is that they are the children of God and that the man in front of them is their brother, and if the state finds that dangerous then the state has a very low opinion of itself."

"That is a very good answer," she said. "It is also not enough."

"I know."

"Because the state does not need a bishop who stays out of politics. The state needs a bishop who is seen to be inside them. It is not enough that you do not oppose us; you have to be photographed with us, and the photograph has to be one that a worker in Sandomierz can look at without despising you." She put out the cigarette. "There is a document. It is two pages long. It is a declaration of loyalty to the Polish People's Republic and of support for the Front of National Unity and for the peaceful coexistence of the Church and the socialist state, and it is being signed this autumn by a number of priests, and it will be published in the newspapers with the names, and when it is published the Church will be forced to answer it, and the answer will split the Episcopate. That is the purpose of the document. You know that. I know that you know it. I am telling you because I do not intend to trick you into signing it. I intend to ask you to."

"No."

"You have not read it."

"I do not need to read it. It is a lie with a photograph attached."

"It is a statement of fact. The Church coexists with the socialist state. It has coexisted with it since 1945, with the full consent of the Episcopate, which signed an agreement in 1950 that was worse than this document in every particular and which you have never once denounced. The bishops accepted the state's right to veto appointments, they accepted the party's youth organisations, they accepted the closure of the *Caritas*, they accepted the imprisonment of their own Primate for three years without a word of public protest, and now a parish priest in Żoliborz with a leaking roof proposes to be more fastidious than the Cardinal." Her voice had not risen. "I have read your file. I have read what your bishop said to the authorities in 1953. Do not talk to me about lies."

"Then let us both talk about something else," he said.

"Yes," she said. "Let us."

---

There was a long silence.

He noticed that her hands were not quite steady. She had hidden it well; she had put them on the desk, flat, the way she used to put them on the table when she was arguing with her mother, and the two white fingers lay on the green baize like something the war had left behind.

"Father Tadeusz Rybak," she said.

He went cold.

"Your vicar. Twenty-six years old. He has been teaching the catechism to twenty children in a private flat on Felińskiego Street since March, and there is a report on my desk from the district office, and the report recommends that he be charged under Article 22 of the decree of 1949, which carries five years." She looked at him. "The case is at present with the prosecutor. It has been there since April. The prosecutor is a man named Zięba who is waiting to see what happens in Sandomierz, because Zięba is a careerist and careerists wait."

"You are telling me this," he said slowly, "because you want me to understand that if I sign, the case goes away."

"I am telling you this because you will find it out anyway, from your own people, and because I would rather you heard it from me than from a rumour." She paused. "Yes. If you sign, the case goes away. I will not pretend otherwise. That is how the state works, father, and you have been in it long enough to know that. If you do not sign, the case will probably go forward in January, and the boy will get two years, and he will come out in 1960 with his health broken and his vocation probably intact, because it is extraordinary how intact the vocations are, and you will have kept your hands clean."

"You are asking me to sell a boy for a bishopric."

"I am asking you to buy a boy with a bishopric," she said. "It is not the same transaction, though I grant you it looks better from where you are standing. And you should know the rest of it. If you refuse, the state will not simply let you alone. We will not permit a bishop who has publicly refused the state's declaration; that would be worse for us than Dembiński. We will block the nomination. And then the see will go to the man who writes articles, and the diocese will have ten years of him, and your vicar will have two years in Rakowiecka, and you will be back in Żoliborz with your leaking roof, and you will have saved something. Something real. That is not nothing." She said it almost gently. "It is what most men do, and most of them are right to."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"How long have you been doing this?" he asked.

The question landed. He saw it land. Something went out of her face — the official face, the competent face — and for a moment there was just a tired woman of thirty-six in a blue suit.

"Since 1946," she said.

"Twelve years."

"Twelve years."

"And in twelve years, how many men have you sat in that chair and offered a bishopric to?"

"Two."

"And how many have you destroyed?"

Her jaw tightened. "That is not a question you have the right to ask."

"I have been in a cellar with your people," he said, and his voice was quiet and it was shaking, "and I have held a pen with a broken wrist while a man in a good suit told me that if I signed it I could go home, and I have asked God every day since then to let me forget the sound of that man's voice, and now I am sitting in a room with a woman I have loved since I was eleven years old and she is making me the same offer in a better accent. So I will ask you the question. How many?"

She stood up.

"Father Zaremba," she said, "you are here at the request of the Office for Religious Affairs, and I am obliged to hear you out, and I have heard you. You will answer the document in writing within seven days. Not to me. To the Director. And I will give you a piece of advice, which I am not obliged to give you, and which you will ignore." She came round the desk and stood in front of him, and she was very close, and the smell of her was coal smoke and soap and cigarette, and he could see the fine lines at the corners of her eyes and the small scar on her chin from a fall off the haycart in 1937. "Do not be a hero in a small room. Heroes are made in public. In a small room with a pen, a man can be a coward for twenty years and nobody will ever know, and it is the only mercy this country has left."

He stood up. He was taller than her, as he had always been, and it had never mattered less.

"Zosia," he said.

The name went into the room like a stone into water. He saw her flinch as though he had raised his hand.

"Don't."

"Did you get my letters?"

She stared at him. Her mouth opened and closed. And in that moment, for perhaps two seconds, the whole apparatus came off her — the suit, the file, the twelve years, the Deputy Director — and there was a girl of seventeen standing in a government office with her hands at her sides and her eyes full of something that had been sealed up since 1945 and was now, unmistakably, coming apart.

"What letters?" she said.

The door opened.

"Comrade Director," said Pani Irena. "The delegation from Płock is here."

Zofia Kędzierska turned her face to the window, and when she turned back it was over, all of it, gone, like water going down a drain.

"Show them into the conference room," she said. "I'll be two minutes." She picked up the file and closed it, and looked at him, and her eyes were perfectly level and perfectly dry. "Thursday. In writing. Good morning, father."

He walked to the door. He was aware of the marble, and the corridor, and the six people on the bench, and the old priest who opened one eye as he passed and then, seeing his face, closed it again and said nothing.

At the door he stopped.

"I wrote to you nine times," he said, without turning round. "In 1945 and 1946. To Wierzbica and to the Committee. Nine letters. And in June of 1946 I stood at the corner of Wesoła Street with a letter in my pocket and watched you come out of that building laughing, and I was too much of a coward to cross the road." He paused. "You should know that. Whatever else you do with me. It was not you who did not write."

He went out.

---

He walked for two hours. He went down Krucza to the river and along the embankment, past the barges and the men fishing with poles and the new flats going up on the far bank with their cranes against the sky, and then he turned back up through the Old Town, where the houses had been rebuilt in 1953 in the colours of a Canaletto painting, too fresh, too bright, like a stage set for a play about a city that no longer existed.

He went into the church at the corner of Freta Street, where a woman was scrubbing the floor and a baby was asleep in a pram by the door, and he sat in the last pew with his hat on his knee and did not pray, exactly; he sat in the dark and let the silence do what silence does.

*What letters?*

She had said it like a woman who had been struck. He had seen her face. It was not a performance; he had known her since she was four years old and he knew every note in her.

If she had not received his letters, then the letters had gone somewhere. And if his had gone somewhere, then so had hers, and the whole of the last twelve years had been built not on a choice but on a cage — a cage made of paper, by men in good suits, in offices with green lamps.

He thought about the signature at the bottom of the letter that had summoned him. *Zofia Kędzierska, Deputy Director.*

And he thought: she did not know. She has been in that building for twelve years and she did not know.

He got up, and genuflected, and went out into the street, and stood on the pavement in the cold with the smell of coal and wet leaves, and understood that he was no longer a man deciding whether to accept a bishopric. He was a man who had just been handed the only thing he had ever wanted, in the worst possible form, at the worst possible hour, and he had no idea in the world what to do with it.

# Chapter Seven — The File

## Warsaw, October 1958

The delegation from Płock was six men and a woman, and they wanted a church.

There had been a church at Radzanowo since 1411, and it had been closed in 1955, and the parish had been merged with another, and for three years the people of Radzanowo had walked eleven kilometres to Mass in the next parish and had buried their dead without a priest. Now the parish council had come to Warsaw with a petition of nine hundred signatures — nine hundred, in a village of six hundred and forty, because the dead had been counted in, and the infants, and the men who had gone to Silesia to work in the mines and had signed anyway — asking for the church to be reopened.

Zofia sat at the head of the table in the conference room and listened for forty minutes, and then she said what she always said, which was that the closure had been carried out under the decree of 1949 on the basis of a report from the voivodeship office, that the building had been transferred to the state for use as a cultural centre, and that the Office could not in law reverse a decision of a voivodeship office.

The leader of the delegation was a man of about sixty with a farmer's hands and a sheet of paper in a plastic folder.

"Comrade Director," he said, "there is no cultural centre. There is a room with a stove and a table and a portrait of the First Secretary, and nobody goes into it. Last winter we had a meeting about potatoes in it. Two men came."

"Then the building is being used."

"The building is being used for nothing," he said, "and God is not being used at all, and I would like to know whose fault that is."

She looked at him for a moment.

Then she said, "Comrade, I will tell you what I can do. I can write to the voivodeship office and ask for a report on the use of the building in the last twelve months. If the report shows that it has not been used for the purposes for which it was transferred — and I will ask for the attendance figures — then the transfer may be reviewed. That is a process. It will take eight or nine months. It will involve three offices. It is entirely possible that it will fail. If you want to write to the voivodeship office yourself, the address is on this paper, and I would advise you to write to them every month, and to keep a copy of every letter, because men who receive a letter every month begin to behave differently from men who receive one letter a year."

The farmer looked at her.

"Why are you helping us?" he said.

"Because I am a servant of the Polish People's Republic," said Zofia, "and the Polish People's Republic does not need nine hundred people in Radzanowo to believe that it is unjust. It can survive that. It cannot survive the fact that they are right." She stood up. "Good morning."

She shook hands with all seven of them, which was not standard, and then she went out of the conference room and along the corridor and into the women's lavatory at the end, and locked the stall, and stood with her forehead against the cold tiles for four minutes, and did not cry, and shook, and then washed her hands and went back to her office and dictated two letters.

---

At half past nine that night she was still at her desk. Irena had gone home at six; the building had emptied; the porter had come up to say that he was going down to the gate and would she please turn off the stove, and she had said yes, and he had gone, and now the whole third floor was hers and one green lamp.

She had been sitting with the telephone in front of her for an hour.

At ten o'clock she picked it up and asked for the Ministry of Internal Affairs, extension 214.

"Rygier."

"It's Kędzierska."

"Ah." A pause, and the sound of a chair. "It's late, comrade. Are you well?"

"I need the whole of a file. Not the extracts. The documentation — the correspondence section, the intercepts, the departmental minutes. File forty-one stroke six, from the Kielce voivodeship office, opened in 1945, subject Zaremba, Aleksander."

The line was quiet for a moment.

"I gave you the whole file on the fourth," Rygier said. "I sent it myself. Four centimetres of it."

"You sent me the operational file. I want the archive. The correspondence."

Another pause, longer.

"Why?" said Rygier.

"Because I am writing a recommendation to the Director on the nomination of a bishop and I do not wish to be surprised."

"You have never in twelve years asked me for a file."

"I have never in twelve years needed one."

Rygier said nothing for a while. She could hear him breathing, and somewhere behind him a wireless playing something with violins in it.

"All right," he said at last. "I'll have Kielce send it up. It will take three days; the archive is in the cellar at Kielce and the cellar at Kielce is a disgrace." A pause. "Zofia. You should know that I have always liked you. I have liked you since 1946. I want you to be careful with this one."

"Good night, colonel."

She put the receiver down. Her hand was shaking. She sat and looked at it, at the two white fingers and the three that were not, and thought: *he said since 1946.*

---

The packet came on Saturday morning, in a courier's oilcloth bag, and it was heavier than the file.

She did not open it at work. She took it home in her briefcase and put it in the drawer of the sideboard and spent the whole of Saturday afternoon at the zoo with Piotr and Bogdan, in the cold, looking at a bear that walked up and down behind its bars in a way that made Piotr quiet. They ate sausages from a stall. Bogdan bought a paper and read it on a bench. It was, in its way, a good day, and she was not there for any of it.

At eleven that night, when the flat was asleep, she took the packet into the kitchen and opened it under the lamp.

There were four dossiers tied with tape, and a bundle of letters tied with string, and a folder of thin flimsies — carbon copies of intercept slips — and a departmental minute in a green folder.

She opened the letters first.

There were twelve of them. Nine in a round, boyish, forward-leaning hand, and three in a small upright hand that she had seen every day of her life in her own mirror when she wrote a shopping list.

She untied the string.

*Sandomierz, 4 June 1945. Reverend Father — no. No. Zosia. I am going to write this and then I am going to burn it, and then I am going to write it again, and at the end of the week I will send one of them.*

*Zosia, I do not know where you are. I have written to the Red Cross at Kraków and to the Polish Red Cross at Lublin and to a woman in Kielce who knew your mother. My mother says you were in the camp at Leipzig and that the Russians have lists. I have looked at every list. I have looked at the lists in the newspaper and I have stood in the square at Kielce in front of the noticeboard like a man looking for his own name, and I have found nothing.*

*I want to tell you about the hospital. It was a school. There was a piano in the corridor with the top missing, and when I could walk I used to go and put my hands on the keys and play nothing, just the keys, because the sound of a piano is the only thing in the world that has never lied to me. The priest who came was called Father Bonifacy and he had a limp and he smelled of snuff. He asked me what I was going to do with my life and I said I would give it to God, and he said, "And what does that mean?" and I said I didn't know, and he said, "Then don't promise it yet."*

*But I promised it, Zosia. I promised it before I said a word to him, in the cellar in the Old Town, on the second of September, when I could not feel my legs.*

*If you are alive, come. If you are alive and you have read this and you are married to someone and you have a house and children, then do not come, and I will understand, and I will say a Mass for you every year on the fourth of August for the rest of my life. But if you are alive and there is any part of you that is still standing at the gate — I am at the church at Wierzbica. I will be here until September. — A.*

*P.S. I planted a willow. You will laugh. It is by the water, nine metres downstream of the old one, because you always said the water comes up in April and takes everything that is too close.*

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the kitchen table and looked at the wall for a while, at the calendar with the picture of the harvest, and then she picked it up and read it again.

The second was dated 19 June. The third, 3 July. In the fourth he wrote: *I have been to Wierzbica. Your father would not speak to me. He stood in the door of the shed with a spade in his hand and told me that his daughter had gone to Kielce and joined the Party and would not be coming back, and that if I was a priest I should go and be a priest and leave the dead alone. Zosia, I am going to tell you what I did. I said, "Panie Tomasz, I love her," and he said, "Then you should have been here in August," and he shut the door.*

*And he was right. That is the thing I cannot get past. He was right.*

She stopped reading and went and stood at the window. The courtyard was dark. A single bulb burned over the bins. She put her hand flat against the cold glass and stood there until her breathing was ordinary, and then she went back to the table, because she was forty-one letters away from the end and she was not going to stop.

The letters went on. July, August, October. In November he wrote: *I have entered the seminary. I want you to know that I did it after, not before. Whatever anyone tells you. After.*

In January 1946: *I am at Podzamcze. It is cold and there are forty of us and I am the oldest and the worst. I am not going to write to you at the Committee any more after this, because a priest's letter to a Party office is a piece of paper that will one day be used against one of us, and I find that I mind less about myself than I expected and more about you.*

And the last one, in March 1946, was addressed to *Panna Zofia Kędzierska, Wierzbica, poczta Mniów*, and it was one page:

*Zosia. I am going to stop. Not because I have stopped, but because a man who keeps writing to a woman who does not answer is not a lover, he is a beggar, and I have been a beggar once already in my life and it was in a cellar with a broken wrist.*

*I have heard from a woman from Stara Wieś that you are in Kielce and that you are doing well and that you have a good coat. I am glad. I am so glad that it frightens me. If they are treating you well, then let them, and let me be the thing that happened to you in the village before the war, and be happy, and let me say Mass for your mother on the fourth of August.*

*I will not write again. God keep you, and if He will not, then I will.*

*— A.*

She sat for a long time with that page in her hand.

Then she opened the folder of flimsies.

They were the intercept slips — one for each letter, with the date of posting, the date of interception, the name of the officer, and a stamped box for the disposition. Every one of them had the same box ticked: *Withhold. Retain.*

And beneath the box, on the slip for the letter of 4 June 1945, in a hand she knew very well because she had been reading it in the margins of her own reports for twelve years, someone had written in green ink:

*Subject is in contact with the Party member K. Do not deliver. She is not to know that he wrote. He is not to know that she is alive. Retain both sides. This is a valuable situation and I do not want it spoiled by a clerk with a conscience.*

She put her hands flat on the table.

Her own letters were underneath. Three of them, in her own hand, with the stamps and the dates, and a slip for each: *Withhold. Retain.* And one of them — the third, the one from March 1946 — had a pencil note at the bottom in a different hand: *Opened and read. Contents affect subject's morale. Recommend continued withholding. — por. W.*

She read her own letter, the one she had written at twenty-three years old, sitting on a bench at Kielce station with her mother's ring money gone and her whole body aching:

*Olek. This is the last one. I am told you don't want to see anyone from the village, so I will stop being from the village. I hope you are well. I hope your mother is well. If you are ever in Kielce, do not come to the Committee, because I would have to offer you tea and we would both be very polite and I would die. Be a good priest. Say a Mass for Hanka sometimes. She was fifteen.*

And a man had read that. Two men had read that, in an office, in 1946, and had written *withhold* on it, and had put it in a drawer, and had gone home to their dinners.

She stood up so quickly that the chair went over.

She walked out of the kitchen and into the hall and stood in the dark by the coats, and then she went into the bathroom and ran the tap and put her hands in the water, which was cold, and stood there, and looked at herself in the mirror over the basin. A woman of thirty-six in a good blouse with her hair pinned up and her mouth pulled tight, and behind her eyes something enormous and black turning over and over like a millwheel.

*Twelve years,* she thought. *Twelve years of being the woman they made.*

And then she thought: *Rygier.*

She went back into the kitchen and read the departmental minute.

It was three pages, dated 14 March 1946, and it was an assessment of her.

*Kędzierska, Zofia, b. 1923 [the year was wrong], repatriate, former inmate of forced labour camp at Leipzig, sister deceased in camp, mother shot in pacification of Wierzbica 4 August 1944, brother Antoni Kędzierski "Sokół" killed in action. Joined PPR July 1945. Intellectually able, emotionally unstable, and entirely without family. Recommendation: retain in service. Her personal history makes her reliable in matters concerning the clergy and the German question. Note: her relationship with the subject Zaremba is a continuing asset. She believes the correspondence ceased at his initiative. Nothing is to be done to correct this. In the event of her promotion, the file is to remain at voivodeship level.*

*Signed: por. B. Rygier.*

*Approved:*

There was no name in the box. There was a signature, and she could not read it, and she had spent twelve years learning to read signatures.

---

She went to Wierzbica on Sunday.

The train to Kielce took four hours and smelled of coal and wet wool. She sat in a second-class compartment with a woman and a child and a man who ate a boiled egg and then slept with his mouth open, and she looked out of the window at the flat country and the stubble and the lines of willows along the drainage ditches, and thought about nothing at all, deliberately, the way you hold your breath under water.

The bus from Kielce to Mniów took an hour. From Mniów she walked.

It was eight kilometres. The road was the same road. The ruts were deeper and there was asphalt on the first two kilometres and then none. The hills were the same hills, with the wood coming down to the fields on the left, and at the fourth kilometre there was a cross at the side of the road that she did not remember, with a wreath of plastic flowers on it, and at the sixth kilometre the mill came into view, rebuilt, with a new roof and a man in a leather apron standing in the doorway who looked at her and did not know her.

She came over the crest at half past two in the afternoon.

The village was there. That was the thing she had not prepared herself for. She had carried the burned valley in her head for fourteen years, the cellars open to the sky, the ash, the woman's kerchief in the wind — and instead there were houses, twenty or thirty of them, new timber gone grey, with smoke coming out of the chimneys and a wireless playing somewhere and a boy on a bicycle going up the hill with a sack of something across the frame.

It looked like a village. It had the appalling ordinariness of a place where people have gone on living.

She stood at the crest and could not make her legs go down the hill.

In the end she went around by the fields, along the top of the ridge, to the cemetery.

The graves were there, in rows, and the wooden crosses of 1945 had been replaced, most of them, with stone, and there was a new wall on the north side paid for by a subscription in 1954 that had been refused permission three times and had been built anyway. She walked along the rows with her hands at her sides.

*Marianna Kędzierska, 1893–1944.*
*Antoni Kędzierski, 1920–1944.*
*Tomasz Kędzierski, 1893–1946.*

Her father's stone had been paid for by somebody. She stood in front of it and read it four times before she understood that the somebody had been Aleksander Zaremba, because there was a line at the bottom of it in small letters, the kind of line that a mason cuts for a price and a priest pays for out of his own pocket: *Requiescat in pace.*

She knelt down. Not because she had decided to. Her legs did it.

She stayed there for some time in the wet grass, and tried to remember how you did it. The words were all there — she could have recited the whole thing, she had said it ten thousand times, at her mother's knee, in the church, in the barracks at Leipzig with Hanka's head in her lap — and they came up into her mouth and lay there, flat and dead, like stones from a riverbed.

She said, "Mama."

The wind moved in the long grass along the wall. Somewhere beyond the wall a cow was lowing, and a woman was calling a child in for dinner, in the voice of every woman who has ever called a child in for dinner in that valley.

"I'm sorry," she said, in Polish, to a stone. "I did what I could. I don't know if it was anything. Hanka was with me. She wasn't alone. She asked for you and I said you were coming." She stopped. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

And then she said, "I don't know how to do this any more," and she understood that she was not talking to the stone.

---

Father Radoń was in the presbytery kitchen, sitting by the stove with a blanket over his knees and a cat on the blanket, and he was seventy-one years old and almost completely deaf in one ear and had a stick with a rubber tip.

He looked up when she came in and said, "Who is it?"

"Zofia Kędzierska, father. Tomasz Kędzierski's daughter."

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Ah. The one who went to Warsaw." He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way a man says a fact that he has had thirty years to get used to. "Sit down. There's tea in the pot, it's stewed, it doesn't matter. Take the chair with the cushion; the other one has a wobble."

She sat. The cat looked at her with contempt and went back to sleep.

"Your father died in the February of '46," Father Radoń said. "His lungs. He was digging graves in the cemetery in the frost and he came in and sat down in that shed and he did not get up. I anointed him. He was not afraid. He asked me to say the psalm about the rivers of Babylon." He poured the tea. "You did not come to the funeral."

"I was in Łódź."

"Yes," said the old man. "Well."

She held the glass in both hands and looked at the steam.

"Father, I've come to ask you something and I want the truth and I don't want it softened."

"I have never softened anything in my life. It's a great failing in a priest."

"The Zaremba boy. Aleksander. In 1945 and '46, when he came back. What did he do?"

Father Radoń put down the teapot and looked at her, and his face, which was the face of a peasant who had been beaten by the Gestapo and had come back with one ear and had said Mass in that church for seventeen more years, did something complicated.

"He came to me in the April of '45 with his side still open," he said. "He was on a bicycle. He had come from Kielce. He asked me where you were, and I said I did not know, and he asked me if your mother was alive, and I said no, and he sat down on that chair where you're sitting and put his head in his hands and stayed like that for a quarter of an hour. Then he got up and went out and I heard him be sick in the garden.

"He came back in the June, and in the July, and in the August. In the August he came and asked me to say a Mass for your mother on the fourth, and I said it, and there were four people in the church and one of them was him. In the September he came to tell me he was going to the seminary and he asked me if I thought he was doing the right thing, and I said, 'My boy, I have been a priest for forty-four years and I have never once known whether I was doing the right thing, so I am not going to start advising other people.'

"And then in the spring of '46 he came again." The old man paused. "He came on a Saturday. He had been to your father's shed. And he came to me afterwards and he sat in that chair and he was — I have seen men in that state maybe four times in my life. He said, 'Father, her father told me she has joined the Party and does not want to see anyone from the village.' And I said, 'And what did you say to him?' And he said, 'I said I loved her.' And I said, 'And what did he say?'"

She waited.

"He said, 'He said I should have been here in August.'" Father Radoń looked into the stove. "And I said to him, 'Well, my boy, you were in Warsaw, and a man cannot be in two places.' And he said, 'No, father, but a man can write a letter.'"

The kitchen was quiet. The cat snored.

"He thought you wanted nothing to do with him," the old man said. "He thought your father spoke for you. And you thought — what did you think?"

"That he had chosen the Church and that was the end of it."

"Ah." Father Radoń nodded slowly. "Well. That is what people do with each other, in the end. We put a silence in the place where the other person is, and then we live next to it for forty years, and we call it a decision." He shifted in his chair. "Do you know what he does every year on the fourth of August? He writes to me. Every year, since 1946, a letter, and in the letter there is money for a Mass for your mother, and every year I say the Mass and I write back and tell him I have said it. Last year he wrote something else. He said, 'Father, if she is alive, and if she ever comes, tell her that I said the Mass for Hanka too.'"

Zofia put the glass down because her hands were not reliable.

"Father," she said, "do you believe in God?"

The old man laughed — a dry, creaking laugh, entirely without bitterness.

"What a question to ask a man in his own kitchen," he said. "I believe in the Mass. I have believed in the Mass since 1941, when I was in the prison at Kielce and there was no Mass, and I discovered that I missed it the way a man misses water. I believe in the body of Christ on the altar because I have seen what it does to people's faces. God — " He shrugged. "God is a very large subject and I am a very tired old man. But I will tell you something, and you can take it back to Warsaw with you. You can believe in nothing at all and still do the thing that needs doing. It is harder, but it is possible. There are men in this village who believe in nothing and they buried their neighbours in '44 and they hid the Finkelsztejn boy's cousin in a hayloft in '43 and they have raised their children decently. They do it because it is the thing that needs doing. And on the day they die, if there is a God, He will know exactly who they were, and if there is not, then it will not matter, and either way they will have done the thing that needed doing, which is the only argument for God that has ever held water with me."

He leaned forward and put his hand on her wrist, and his hand was dry and hot.

"You are not here to ask me about God," he said. "You are here because you are standing in front of a door and you have been standing in front of it since 1945 and you would like somebody to tell you that it is all right to knock. So I will tell you. It is all right to knock. But I will also tell you the other thing, which is the thing your mother would have told you." He sat back. "The door is not a door. It is a man. And a man is not a solution to anything."

---

She went down to the river before she left.

The old willow had gone — it had come down in a storm in 1953, and there was a stump of it left, black and rotten, with new growth coming out of the side. But nine metres downstream there was a tree.

It was fourteen years old and it was magnificent. It stood about eight metres high with a spread of silver-green branches over the water, and the trunk was as thick as her thigh, and the wind was coming up the valley and the whole crown of it was moving, all of it at once, like a woman shaking out her hair.

She stood and looked at it for a long time.

Then she took out her knife and cut a withy from the low branch, and stripped the leaves off with her thumb, and put it in her coat pocket.

She went to Żoliborz on the Sunday evening, in the last week of October, at six o'clock, when the rosary devotions were beginning.

She had not been inside a church since 1946, when she had gone into the church at Łódź on a Sunday in July and had stood at the back and had not been able to kneel. She had not been inside one since; she had walked past them for twelve years the way a woman walks past the house of a man she is trying not to think about.

St Kazimierz was a red-brick church of 1888 with a leaking roof and a smell of cold stone and candle wax and wet coats. There were perhaps a hundred and fifty people in it, mostly women, a few old men, three boys in the front pew with their hair combed. The rosary had already begun. Zofia stood at the very back, by the door, with her collar up, and the words came at her out of the dark — *Zdrowaś Maryjo, łaski pełna* — and her mouth began to move before she had decided anything, the way a hand goes to a scar.

After the rosary he came up into the pulpit.

He was in a black cassock and a stole, and he looked tired, and he put his hands on the edge of the pulpit and looked down at the hundred and fifty people in the pews, and he said:

"The Gospel this evening is the young man who had great possessions, and he asks Our Lord what he must do to inherit eternal life, and Our Lord tells him the commandments, and the young man says, 'All these I have kept from my youth.' And then Our Lord looks at him, and loves him, and says: 'One thing thou lackest.'"

He paused.

"I have been thinking about that young man all week," he said. "Not because he was rich. He was, and that is a great deal, and I do not intend to pretend this evening that it is a small thing to be rich, because it is not, and Our Lord was not being figurative. But I have been thinking about the words *from my youth.* Here is a man who has kept every commandment since he was a boy. He has not killed, he has not stolen, he has not lied, he has not committed adultery, he has honoured his father and his mother. He is, by any measure available to his neighbours, a good man. And Our Lord looks at him, and loves him, and says: there is one thing you lack.

"And the thing he lacks is not a virtue. That is what I want to say to you this evening. It is not that he needed to be more generous, or more prayerful, or more chaste. He had those. What he lacked was the one thing that he had built his whole life on not knowing: that everything he had, he had been given, and that he was not the owner of any of it, and that the moment he understood that, he would have to give it away.

"Most of us are not rich. Some of us have nothing at all, and I know that, and I know who you are, and I see you in this church every Sunday and I am not going to insult you this evening. But every one of us has one thing that we have built our life on not knowing. And it is almost never a sin. That is the thing that frightens me. When I was a young man I thought that the enemy of my soul would come to me with a bottle and a woman, and it did not. It came to me with a grief, and it came to me with a decision I made in the dark in 1945 which I have never been able to unmake, and it said: this is yours. This is the thing that makes you who you are. Hold it. Hold it tight. Do not let anyone take it from you, not even God."

Someone in the pews was crying, quietly, the way people cry in Polish churches, without any fuss.

"You will meet, in your life, someone who has been given a great deal and does not know it, and someone who has lost everything and has built a house on the loss, and the two of them will look exactly alike from the outside, and you will not be able to tell them apart, and neither will I. So I am not going to tell you this evening to give away your possessions. I am going to tell you something smaller and harder. I am going to ask you to notice the thing that you have built your life on not knowing. You do not have to give it away tonight. God is patient; He has been patient with me for thirteen years. But notice it. Say to yourself, in the dark, before you sleep: there is one thing I lack, and I know what it is, and I am afraid of it."

He stopped. He looked down at the pulpit rail for a moment, and then he looked up, straight over the heads of the hundred and fifty people, at the back of the church, at the door, where a woman in a dark coat was standing with her collar up and her hands at her sides.

He did not move. His mouth opened and nothing came out.

The congregation waited. Somebody coughed. The candle on the altar guttered and stood up again.

Then he said, in a voice that was not quite the voice he had been preaching in, "The Mass will be at seven, and again at ten. I ask your prayers this week for a man I knew when I was a boy, who is dying in the hospital at Otwock, and who taught me to plant trees." He stepped back and came down out of the pulpit, and the organ began, and the hundred and fifty people rose and shuffled and knelt.

Zofia did not leave.

She stood at the back with her collar up and her hands at her sides, and the hymn went up around her, and she waited, in the way of a woman who has waited fourteen years, for the crowd at the door to thin out, and for the church to empty, and for the man in the black cassock to come down the aisle and find her.

# Chapter Eight — The Empty Church

## Warsaw, 26 October 1958

She was standing by the holy water stoup with her collar up.

The church was empty now except for the sacristan, who was putting out the candles with a brass snuffer, and an old woman in the third pew who had fallen asleep and would be woken, in a quarter of an hour, by the cold. The organ had stopped. The last of the hymn was still in the vault somewhere, the way a smell stays in a room.

Aleksander came down the aisle with his stole over his arm.

They looked at each other. Up close she was grey with fatigue; there were shadows under her eyes that had not been there in the office, and she had been crying, recently, and had done something about it with a handkerchief and cold water and had not entirely succeeded.

"Pani," he said.

"Father." Her voice was steady. "Have you got a room with a stove in it, or are we going to do this in the porch?"

He took her into the presbytery study.

It was a small room with a bookcase, a desk, two chairs, a portrait of the Sacred Heart, and a tiled stove that Pani Bronisława had lit at four o'clock and that was still warm. There was a bottle of plum brandy on the shelf that a parishioner had given him at Easter and two glasses that did not match. He did not offer her any. He hung up his coat and put his stole in the drawer, deliberately, because he wanted there to be no mistake about what he was and was not wearing, and then he sat down.

She stood by the stove with her back to him, and took off her gloves, finger by finger.

"I went to Wierzbica," she said.

He said nothing.

"I saw my father's grave. I saw the stone." Her voice was flat and careful. "Thank you."

"You're welcome."

"I saw Father Radoń. He told me about the letters. About how you came in '45 and '46, and about what my father said to you, and about the Mass on the fourth of August." She turned around. "And then I went back to Warsaw and I asked Rygier for the archive file, and on Saturday morning he sent it to me."

He watched her face.

"There were twelve letters in it," she said. "Nine in your hand and three in mine. Every one of them had a slip attached and every slip had the same box ticked. *Withhold. Retain.*" She said the words without any expression at all, as though she were reading a railway timetable. "And on the first slip there was a note in green ink that said that the subject was in contact with the Party member K., and that I was not to know that he had written, and that he was not to know that I was alive, and that this was a valuable situation and was not to be spoiled by a clerk with a conscience."

The stove ticked.

"And there was a departmental minute dated March 1946," she said, "which was an assessment of me, and which said that my personal history made me reliable in matters concerning the clergy, and that my relationship with the subject Zaremba was a continuing asset, and that in the event of my promotion the file was to remain at voivodeship level. It was signed by Rygier. It was approved by somebody whose signature I could not read, and I have been reading signatures for twelve years." She paused. "So. Now you know what I am. I am the asset. I have been the asset since the spring of 1946, and everything I have done since then — the school at Łódź, the office, the promotions, the flat in Mokotów, the car, the good coat that the woman from Stara Wieś told you about in 1946 — all of it was paid for by a man who had twelve letters in a drawer."

He got up.

He did not go to her. He went to the window and stood with his hands behind his back, and looked out at the yard and the strip of earth and the dark shape of the tomatoes.

"That isn't true," he said.

"Which part?"

"The part where you are the asset." He turned. "You were a girl of twenty-three in a shed with a dead mother and a dead sister and a dead brother and a priest who had promised himself to God, and a man in a good suit came to you with a bowl of soup and a card and a purpose. He would have taken you whether the letters were in the drawer or not. And then you were a woman of twenty-six who was better at her job than anyone else in that building, and then a woman of thirty who had read every file in the Kieleckie and could have had any of them for lunch, and then a Deputy Director at thirty-four, and none of that — none of it, Zosia — was given to you by Rygier. It was taken by you. From a world that has never given a woman like you anything."

"Don't be generous with me," she said. "I can't stand it."

"I'm not being generous. I'm being accurate." He came back and sat down. "The letters were a lie. Everything else you built with your own hands, and it is standing, and you did it in a country that eats people like you for breakfast. The only thing the letters did was to make you believe that the one thing you wanted had been taken from you by the one person you would have died for. That is not a small thing. It is a very great and particular cruelty." He looked at her. "Do you understand what I am telling you? He did not make you into what you are. He made you into a woman who believed that love was a thing that had been done to her once, by a coward, in a churchyard. That is what he did. He took the one thing in your life that was not the state, and he made it into another piece of the state."

She sat down in the chair.

She sat down the way a woman sits down when her legs have decided for her, and she put her elbows on her knees and her hands over her face, and she did not cry; she breathed. He listened to her breathe for a while.

"I signed a paper in 1951," she said, from behind her hands.

He waited.

"His name was Szymon Wróbel. He was a priest at Opatów. He was forty-one. He had organized — " She stopped, and started again. "He had been taking the catechism to children in four villages, and he had written a letter to a bishop that mentioned the arrests in the district, and the letter was intercepted. There were twelve of us in the room, and the file came to me, and I wrote the opinion. It was two pages. I said that he was a threat to public order and that the case should proceed." She took her hands away from her face. "He was sentenced to six years. He died in the prison at Sztum in November 1953 of tuberculosis, at the age of forty-three. His mother wrote to the Office four times. The letters came to my desk. I answered them with a form."

Aleksander sat very still.

"Why are you telling me this?" he said.

"Because I want you to know who is sitting in your study."

"I know who is sitting in my study."

"No, you don't. You think it's me. It isn't." She was looking at him now and her eyes were dry and very hard. "You think there's a girl under all this — the one from the hayfield, the one who sang in the choir. There isn't. There's a woman who read a file on a priest of forty-one and wrote two pages and went home and had her dinner and slept for eight hours. That is not a thing that happened to me. That is a thing I did. And I did it four or five times, and each time it was easier, and the last time I didn't even read the whole file. So when you tell me that Rygier made me — " She stopped. "Don't. Don't do me the kindness. I have been done a great many kindnesses by men with files and I know what they cost."

He got up and went to the shelf and took down the bottle and poured two fingers into each glass and gave her one, and she took it, and looked at it, and drank half of it in a swallow like a farm girl, which, he thought, she still was; she was a farm girl who had learned to drink at Party receptions and had never learned to drink like a woman at a Party reception.

"I have a question," he said, "and I want you to answer it honestly, and then we will never speak of it again."

"Ask."

"Are you sorry because he died, or because you are the one who did it?"

She looked at him.

"Both," she said. "But if you had asked me in 1953, I would have said the first. And that's the thing, Olek. That's the thing I can't get past. In 1953 I would have told you I was sorry he died and that the sentence was just. I believed it. I had a whole architecture in my head and it held up the sky, and Wróbel was a small item in it. And then in 1956 Khrushchev read out the list and they took the portrait of Bierut down in our building and put it in a cupboard, and I stood in the corridor and watched two men carry a portrait of the man I had worked for into a cupboard, and I thought: *if that comes out of the cupboard, what comes out with it?*" She turned the glass in her fingers. "So now I'm sorry for both. But my sorrow arrived in 1956, and his mother's arrived in 1953, and there's a three-year difference between us that nothing can fix. And that is the actual state of my soul, father. Not guilt. Arithmetic."

He sat down again.

"You asked me once," he said, "why I didn't take you under the willow."

"Don't."

"I'm going to tell you anyway, because you've been carrying the wrong version of it for fourteen years." He looked at the desk. "It wasn't because I wanted it in daylight. I said that, and it was true, but it wasn't the reason. The reason was that I was frightened. Not of you. Of the fact that if I had you, I would have something to lose, and I had already lost everything, and I had discovered that a man with nothing to lose can stand at a gate with his hands folded and be, in his own mind, a hero. If I had had you, I would have had to be a coward for you, every day, for the rest of the war. And I was twenty-one years old and I did not think I was that strong." He paused. "And then I lost you anyway. And I have been a coward for you ever since — in a street in Kielce in 1946, and in a room on Krucza Street three weeks ago, and now, tonight, sitting at this desk, because I have been in this room for an hour and I have not once said the thing that I actually want to say."

She set the glass down.

"Say it," she said.

"I want to." He said it simply. "I want to, and I can't, and the reason I can't is not God."

"What is it?"

"Because you are the only thing in my life that has never been a sin." He looked up at her. "Everything else I have done I have had to bring to that altar and lay down and be forgiven for. My cowardice. My signature. The eleven years I did not write to my own mother because I was ashamed. All of it goes into that box and it comes out forgiven, and it is a miracle every time and I have never once deserved it. But you — " He stopped. "If I put my hands on you tonight, then tomorrow I have to bring you to that altar as well. I have to call the one clean thing in my life a sin, and confess it, and be forgiven for it, and give it up. And I will not do that. I would rather be a man with a broken vow than a priest who has called you a sin. I have been in a cellar with a pen in my hand and I signed. I know exactly what I am capable of when I am frightened, and I am frightened now, and I will not put you in that drawer."

She stood up and came around the desk and stood in front of him, and he stood up, because a man does, and they were a hand's width apart.

"Then don't," she said. "Don't be a priest tonight. Be the boy at the gate. Nobody would know. There is nobody in this building. There is nobody in this city who would know."

"I would know," he said. "And in about four years, on a bad night, I would tell you. And you would forgive me, and it would be worse."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her hand up — slowly, deliberately, the way a woman does something she has decided to do and will not be talked out of — and put it flat on his chest, over his heart, and held it there.

He did not move.

He was aware of the stove, and the smell of coal, and the small sound of the wind in the yard, and the fact that her hand was warm and her fingers were trembling, and that two of them were cold.

"I used to think about you in the camp," she said, very quietly. "Not God. I want you to know that. When Hanka died I said a thing to the sky that I have not taken back and will not take back, and after that I did not pray. But I thought about you. Every night, in the bunk, for five months. I made a picture of your face and I put it behind my eyes and I looked at it in the dark." Her hand was still on his chest. "And when I came back, and I found out what you had chosen, do you know what I thought? I thought: he chose the thing that killed my sister. Not because I believed it. Because it was the only way I could stop the picture. I had to make you into something I could hate, because I could not be a woman who was still in love with a man who had left her for God. That is what I have been doing for fourteen years, father. I have been hating a man who does not exist."

"Zosia."

"Say it again."

"Zosia."

She took her hand away.

"All right," she said, and her voice was ordinary again, brisk, the voice of a woman in a meeting. "All right. Then we will do this the other way." She went back around the desk and sat down and pulled her bag onto her knees and took out a folded paper. "This is the document. It will be in your hands by Monday; the courier will bring it. I am giving it to you four days early because I want you to have read it and thought about it before Rygier's man comes to see you, and because I want you to understand what is in it before you refuse."

He took it. It was two pages of typewriting on good paper. He read it standing up, by the lamp, and it took him four minutes.

"No," he said.

"Read the third paragraph again."

"I've read it. *The Church, faithful to the traditions of the Polish nation, recognizes the leading role of the Polish United Workers' Party in the construction of socialism and undertakes to educate the faithful in the spirit of loyalty to the Polish People's Republic.*" He put it down. "I cannot sign a document that tells a lie about the Church and flatters a party that arrested my vicar's catechist and beat my father to death in the yard of the manor."

"Your father was beaten by the Gestapo."

"And the men who beat him wore boots and had a list, and the men who want me to sign this have files and good suits and are, in every particular that matters, the same men with better manners. I will not sign it. And I will tell you something else, Zosia, and then I will ask you a question." He leaned on the desk. "If I sign it, I am not a bishop. I am a photograph. And the Church in that diocese will be governed by a photograph for twenty years, and then I will die, and they will put a nice stone on me, and there will not be one man in Sandomierz who will be able to say what I actually believed. I would rather be a parish priest in the Bieszczady saying Mass for eleven people, and be able to look at them."

"And Tadeusz?"

He was silent.

"Tadeusz Rybak will be charged in January," she said. "Two years. He will be twenty-eight when he comes out. He will have lost the two best years of his priesthood, and he will be a different man, and you will be the man who could have bought him with a signature and did not." She folded her hands. "I want you to hear me say this clearly, because I will not say it twice. I am not threatening you. I am telling you what will happen in the world, and it will happen whether I am in this office or in a factory in Nowa Huta. The state is not a person. It does not hate you. It is a machine with a great many clerks, and one of the clerks is a woman who loves you, and that changes nothing at all about the machine."

He looked at her.

"Then what do you want me to do?"

"I want you to be a bishop," she said. "I have wanted it since the fourth of October, and I have spent three weeks trying to find a way to want something else, and I cannot. Not because of the Party. Because I have read every file on every priest in the Kieleckie for twelve years, and I know what happens to a diocese when it is governed by a man nobody believes. I have watched it happen in three dioceses. The churches fill up with bitterness and the seminaries empty and the young men go to Warsaw and become engineers." She stood up. "And I want one thing for myself, and I will say it once. I want there to be, in this country, in my lifetime, one man in a position of power who is exactly what he appears to be. One. That is all. It is a small thing to want, and it is the most expensive thing in the world, and I have not been able to want it for twelve years because I did not believe such a man existed."

"Zosia—"

"Don't." She picked up her gloves. "I'm going to go home now. My husband will be awake; he is always awake; he reads. I am going to go home and lie next to a good man who has not asked me a real question in six years, and I am going to think about this room." She pulled on her gloves. "I am not going to tell you what I will do on Thursday. I don't know what I will do on Thursday. I have never in my life not known what I would do, and I would like you to understand that this is the worst thing that has happened to me since 1944."

At the door she stopped.

"There was a bundle of letters," she said. "They're in my bag. They're yours — the nine of them. I've read them. I know them by heart; I don't need the paper." She put them on the hall table, tied with string, and stood with her hand on them for a moment. "I kept my three."

"Zosia."

"What?"

"May I keep one?"

She looked at him. Then she untied the string and took the top letter — the first one, the one from the fourth of June 1945 — and put it in his hand, and tied the rest back up.

"Give me your hand," she said.

He gave her his hand. She took something out of her coat pocket: a little string of beads, eleven of them, brown and slightly irregular, dull as old bone.

"Bread," he said.

"Bread." She pressed it into his palm. "A woman in the barracks made them out of bread and water and dried them on the stove. She made them for her daughter, who was in another camp, and then she died, and I took them, and I have carried them for fourteen years and I have never once used them." Her mouth twisted. "I thought I would give them to somebody who would."

He closed his hand round them. They were light and hard and slightly warm from her pocket.

"Zosia," he said. "Come on Thursday. Not to the Office. Come here, in the evening, after the devotions. Whatever happens on Thursday. Come here, and I will hear you."

She looked at him from the doorway, with her collar up and her gloves on and her bag under her arm, and for a moment she was seventeen years old in the doorway of the organ loft with the wind coming through the broken pane.

"I don't want absolution," she said. "I want an accomplice."

"I know," he said. "I've been one since 1938."

She went out.

He stood in the hall for a long time, with the letter in one hand and the bread beads in the other. Then he went back into the study and sat down and put them both on the desk in front of him, side by side, and looked at them.

And then he did the thing he had not been able to do since 1944, which was to pray in his own words, out loud, like a man in a room with somebody else.

He said: "I don't know what to do. I have never known what to do. You have been asking me since I was nineteen years old to be a man and I have been giving You the liturgy instead." He stopped. "She is going to destroy me, and she is going to do it because she is more frightened than I am, and I am supposed to hate her for it and I can't, and I don't want to be a bishop, and I don't want to be a martyr, and I want, more than I have ever wanted anything in my life, to be a man of forty who comes home to a house with that woman in it and a fire and a dog and nothing at all in the world to be brave about." His voice broke. "And I know that is not on offer. I know. But I'm going to say it to You anyway, because You are the only one in this building who has to listen."

The stove ticked. The wind moved in the yard.

"Give me until Thursday," he said. "That's all. Just until Thursday."

# Chapter Nine — The Opinion

## Warsaw, 27–30 October 1958

Colonel Bolesław Rygier had an office on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Internal Affairs with a window that looked out over the roofs toward the river, and a good carpet, and a photograph on the wall of himself as a young man in a partisan's cap in the Wilno district in 1944.

He was fifty-three. He had a small moustache and clean hands and the manner of a doctor who has just told you that the news is not good but that you should not be alarmed.

"Sit down, Zofia. Tea? No, you don't drink tea. Cigarette, then. These are Bulgarian, they're terrible, everyone in the ministry smokes them because they're cheap and we're all patriots."

She took one. She had not slept.

"I had a report on Friday," Rygier said, settling into his chair. "From the parish of St Kazimierz, Żoliborz. A woman of the parish council — you know how it is, they come to us because they are frightened of their own priest, or because their sister-in-law has a flat and they want it, or because they are simply lonely and we are the only people who will listen to them for an hour." He lit his cigarette. "It said that on Sunday the twenty-sixth, after the devotions, Father Zaremba took a woman into the presbytery and that they were together in the study for one hour and forty minutes, and that at nine o'clock she left and he stood in the hall for some time and then was heard — the informant is in the building opposite, she has a window on the yard, she is a very good source — he was heard speaking in a loud voice, alone, in the study. Praying, apparently. She was much moved by it. She is a pious woman and she is also paid eleven hundred złotys a month."

Zofia drew on the cigarette and said nothing.

"The woman was described as being about thirty-five, well dressed, dark coat, with her hair pinned up, and she arrived in a car with a driver who waited at the corner of Mickiewicza Street." He raised his eyebrows. "Now. I have a great many well-dressed women of thirty-five in this city, but very few of them arrive in a car with a driver, and I am a thorough man, and I asked the driver's pool, and the car was from the Office for Religious Affairs, and the driver was Antoni Góra, who has worked for you for four years."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below in the building a door slammed.

"Well," said Rygier. "Here we are."

"Here we are."

"I want to say something to you, and I want you to hear it as coming from a man who has been doing this work for fourteen years and who has, in that time, done things that he does not talk about in the evening." He tapped ash. "There is no version of this in which you come out of it well. You are a Deputy Director of a state office and you have been meeting, privately, at night, without a report, a man who is a candidate for the episcopate and who is the subject of an open file, and you have pulled an archive from Kielce that you had no operational reason to pull, and you have been to his church. If I wanted to, Zofia, I could have you in a room on Rakowiecka Street by Tuesday, and I would not enjoy it, and it would take about four days, and at the end of it you would be a woman of thirty-six who has been retired from public life on medical grounds and whose husband would be transferred to a ministry post in Rzeszów." He sat back. "I am not going to do that."

"Why not?"

"Because you are the best officer I have ever had, and because I have been fond of you since 1946, and because a state that eats its own good servants is a state that ends up with nothing but the bad ones." He said it without irony, and Zofia understood, with a clarity that was almost peaceful, that he meant every word, and that this was the most frightening thing she had ever encountered — worse than the men in the yard in 1944, who at least had been honest about what they were.

"You kept the letters," she said.

"Ah." He put his cigarette in the ashtray. "You've read the file."

"I've read the file."

"I kept the letters," he said, "because a man who has a letter that has not been delivered has a great many options, and a man who has delivered it has none. That is not a thing I am ashamed of. It is the work." He looked at her steadily. "But I will tell you the truth, since we are being honest, and the truth is more complicated than the file. In 1946 I made an assessment of you that was accurate in every particular, and one of the particulars was that your history made you reliable in matters concerning the clergy. That was true. It was true for eight years, and it was true because it was true — not because I had arranged it. You were reliable because you were angry, and because you had been hurt in a way that made you want to hurt the thing that had hurt you, and the clergy were the available object. That is how it works, Zofia. I did not make you angry. The war made you angry. I simply did not stand in the way."

"And the letters."

"The letters were insurance."

"Against what?"

"Against the day when you would stop being angry." He stubbed out the cigarette with one finger. "I am a thorough man. I do not like to lose an officer, and I have lost three in twelve years to exactly this: a priest, a man from the village, a piece of music, a photograph. The past is a minefield and every one of us has a spot in it where we stepped on something at the age of nineteen and have been carrying it around ever since. I knew where yours was. It was in a drawer in Kielce, and it was insurance, and I am not going to apologise for it, because if I apologised for that I would have to apologise for the whole building, and the whole building is the Polish People's Republic and I have given my life to it."

She looked at him.

"You are a very intelligent man," she said, "and you have spent your whole life doing small evil in the service of a large idea, and you have never once looked at the arithmetic."

"I look at it every night," said Rygier. "That is the difference between us. You look at it in the daytime, when it is easy."

He opened a drawer and took out a folder, and put it on the desk between them, and did not open it.

"Now. The business. The document goes out on the fifteenth of November. Twenty-two names so far, including two cathedral canons and a suffragan in the west. Zaremba's signature would be worth more than the other twenty-one together, and we are not going to get it, are we."

"No."

"I didn't think so." He sighed. "Dembiński will be in the newspapers on the sixteenth with his photograph and a statement about the Church's place in socialist society, and by the spring he will be the most hated priest in Poland, and in five years he will be a man of fifty-five with a good flat and no friends. And the see will still be vacant, or it will go to Kalinowski, who will be dead in four years, and then there will be another vacancy." He tapped the folder. "Unless you have something to tell me."

She had spent three nights preparing for this, and she had written it out four times and burned three of the drafts, and she had discovered, somewhere in the middle of the third night, that the only argument that would work on Rygier was the argument that was true.

"I have an opinion on the nomination," she said. "I would like to give it to you before it goes to the Director, as a professional courtesy."

"Go on."

"Block him and you get Dembiński. Dembiński will be consecrated in the spring and he will be a catastrophe for the state, not for the Church. He will be believed by nobody, he will be obeyed by nobody, and every sermon he gives in favour of the Front of National Unity will be worth ten sermons against it. In three years the diocese will be ungovernable in the way that a diocese is ungovernable: not with rifles, with silence. The churches will fill. The young men will go to the monasteries out of sheer contrariness. And every Catholic in Poland will know that the state chose their bishop for them, and they will remember it in 1966, when we are planning the Millennium and they are planning theirs."

"And if we approve Zaremba?"

"Then you get a bishop who will not sign your document, and who will not speak at the Front, and who will not denounce you either, ever, because he has told you that he will not, and he is the one man in this country who does exactly what he says." She leaned forward. "You do not need a bishop who loves the Party, colonel. You need a bishop you can predict. Dembiński is unpredictable because he is despised — a despised man will do anything to be liked. Zaremba is entirely predictable: he will say Mass, he will teach, he will visit the sick, he will not conspire, and he will refuse you to your face with perfect courtesy once a year for thirty years. And in thirty years, when he is sixty-six and the Party is still here and I am dead or in Poznań, you will be able to say to your superiors: we approved him, we watched him, nothing happened."

Rygier was silent for a long time. He lit another cigarette and looked at the window and the roofs and the river.

"You want him approved because you love him," he said at last.

"Yes," she said. "And I am telling you that the reason and the policy coincide, which has not happened to me once in twelve years, and you should take it, because it will not happen again."

He laughed. It was a short, ugly, entirely genuine laugh.

"God, you're good," he said. "You should have been a Jesuit." He opened the folder. "All right. Here is what happens. I am not going to approve anything. The Director approves; I merely advise; the Director is a coward who wants to be liked by everybody, and if you give him an opinion that makes him look clever and lets him sleep at night, he will sign it. Write your opinion. Write it well. I will not stand in the way, and I will not help you either, and if it goes wrong, it will be your opinion and your signature and your name in the file."

"And the price?"

"There is always a price, and you have already named it." He closed the folder. "You are going to Poznań in January. The Voivodeship Office, the section for denominational affairs. It is a promotion in grade and a demotion in everything else, and it will be presented to you by the cadres department as a recognition of your experience in the provinces. You will go, and you will not appeal, and you will not write to anybody, and in two years, if you are sensible, you will be the head of the section and nobody in Warsaw will remember your name."

She nodded. She had expected it.

"And the second part," said Rygier, "is a personal arrangement between the two of us, and it does not go in any file." He looked at her. "Four times a year, a note. Not a report — a note. Two pages. What he is doing, who he is seeing, what he is saying. Not to use against him. I want you to understand that: I have no interest in destroying a bishop we have just approved, that would be an admission of error, and this ministry does not admit error. I want to know what he is. He is going to be the most important churchman in this country under fifty, and I would like to know what he is, and the only person in Poland who knows what he is, is you."

Zofia sat with her hands in her lap.

If she said no, the opinion would go into the drawer, Dembiński would be consecrated in the spring, Tadeusz would go to Rakowiecka, and she would be in a room on Rakowiecka herself by Tuesday with a doctor and a piece of paper and a signature at the bottom that would be hers and would not be hers.

If she said yes, she would be a woman who wrote reports on the man she loved, four times a year, for the rest of her working life, in a city two hundred and eighty kilometres away.

"Two pages," she said. "Four times a year."

"Two pages."

"Then yes."

Rygier looked at her for a moment with something that might almost have been sadness.

"You know what you have just done," he said.

"Yes."

"No, you don't." He got up and went to the window. "You think you have made a compromise to save a man and a boy. And in six months you will write the first note, and it will be full of things that seem harmless — he preached on the family, he visited the seminary, he received a letter from Kraków — and in two years the note will be a habit, and in five years you will be writing about a man you no longer know, in a city you have never visited, and you will be good at it, because you are good at everything, and you will be paid for it, and you will tell yourself that you are protecting him." He turned. "And then one day you will write something, some small thing, a name, a date, and it will be used, and you will not be able to say that you did not know, because you have known since 1946 exactly what this building does with a name and a date."

"Then I will write nothing that can be used," she said.

"Everyone says that at the beginning," said Rygier. "Get out. I have a meeting at eleven."

---

She went home at four o'clock, which she had not done in three years.

Piotr was at the kitchen table with his schoolbooks. Bogdan was not home. The flat smelled of cabbage and the paraffin of the stove, and there was a pair of the boy's socks drying on the radiator, and the wireless was playing something with a lot of violins.

She took off her coat and hung it up and stood in the doorway of the kitchen and looked at her son.

He was ten years old. He had his father's heavy brow and her own quick, sideways way of looking at people, and he was chewing the end of a pencil, and there was a smudge of ink on his cheek, and he was, she thought, the only thing she had ever made that was not a document.

"Piotr," she said. "Where is the catechism?"

He went white.

"Don't lie to me. I have known since August." That was not true; she had known since Monday, from Rygier's file, and she had spent two days deciding how to do this. "Where is it?"

He got up and went into the bedroom and came back with a small blue book and put it on the table.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat down. He was frightened, and trying not to show it, and she saw him arrange his face the way she arranged hers, and it was like looking into a mirror made of everything she had lost.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. From the beginning."

And he told her.

He told her that it had started in the spring, when Jurek had taken him to the flat on Felińskiego Street on a Thursday afternoon, and that there were twenty children there and a young priest called Father Tadeusz who told them stories and taught them the Ten Commandments and the Hail Mary, and that it was illegal and they all knew it was illegal and they went anyway, up the back stairs, two at a time. He told her that Father Tadeusz had been arrested in April and that they had all cried and then he had come back after nine days and had said, "Well, children, we had better make up for lost time." He told her that he had not told her because she was in the Party and he knew what she thought, and that he had told his father, and that his father had said, "Then don't tell your mother, and don't be stupid about it."

She sat very still.

"And what do they teach you?" she said.

"That God made the world. That Jesus died for us. That when we die we go to — " He stopped.

"Go on."

"That we go to God, if we're good." He looked at the table. "Mama, Jurek's grandmother died in the summer and there was a funeral and the priest said she was with God, and Jurek wasn't sad the way I thought he would be. He was sad but he wasn't — " He searched for the word. "He wasn't in the dark. Do you know what I mean? He had somewhere to put it."

"Piotr."

"And I'm not baptized," he said. "They all are and I'm not. Father Tadeusz says it doesn't matter if you don't know, but it does matter, because if I die I'm not anything. I'd just be — " He made a small gesture with his hand, a scattering. "Nothing. Like a candle going out. And I think about it at night, Mama. I think about it in the dark and I can't stop."

She got up and went to the window and stood with her back to him.

She thought: *I have a file on my son.*

She thought: *twenty children, and a list, and the list is in the case file of Father Rybak, and if the case goes forward in January the list will be read in a courtroom, and there will be a woman from the school, and a man from the district office, and one of the names on that list will be Piotr Leszczyński, and then it will be in a file, and then my son will be in a file for the rest of his life.*

She thought, quite calmly: *and Rygier has known since Monday that I know, and he has been waiting to see what I would do, and he has not said one word about it, and that is the most elegant threat I have ever been given.*

"Mama," said Piotr, behind her, in a small voice. "Are you going to tell Father Tadeusz's bishop?"

She turned around.

"What?"

"Jurek says if someone tells, they arrest the priest." His face was working. "Are you going to arrest Father Tadeusz?"

She crossed the room and knelt down in front of him and took his face in her two hands, and her two white fingers lay against his cheek, and she said, "No. Listen to me. No. Nobody is going to arrest Father Tadeusz. Nobody is going to arrest you. Nobody is going to do anything at all, because I am going to see to it, and I have been seeing to things in this city for twelve years and I am better at it than any man in that building."

He looked at her. Then he said, "Will you come to the catechism?"

She laughed — a strange, cracked laugh, with no air in it — and put her forehead against his forehead.

"No," she said. "I won't come to the catechism. But you can go. Do you understand me? You can go, and you can tell Father Tadeusz that your mother knows and that she says thank you, and if anyone ever asks you in school, you say your mother is a Party member and your father works in the ministry and you don't know anything, and you keep going up the back stairs, two at a time, the way you have been."

"Really?"

"Really."

He put his arms round her neck and held on, and she knelt there on the kitchen floor with her son's arms round her neck and her eyes open and fixed on the calendar with the picture of the harvest.

---

Bogdan came home at seven.

He came into the kitchen, and he looked at her, and then at the blue book on the table, and then at her again, and he put down his briefcase.

"Ah," he said.

"Ah."

He took off his coat and hung it on the back of the door and sat down opposite her and rubbed his face with both hands.

"I should have told you," he said. "I know. I have been going to tell you since April."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you would have stopped it, and because I did not think that stopping it was the most important thing in the world." He looked at her. "Zofia. He is ten years old and he has one life and there is a great black hole in it where everybody in this country has a great black hole, and he went looking for something to put in it. I would rather he put a catechism in it than a bottle. That is my entire politics. That is what I have come to."

She looked at her husband.

He was forty-four years old. He had joined the Party in 1945 because everyone he respected had joined in 1945, and he had spent thirteen years in the Ministry of Foreign Trade moving numbers from one column to another, and he had never once been promoted, and he had never once asked her for anything, and he had never once, in twelve years, asked her where she went at night or why she sometimes stood at the window until two in the morning.

"You knew," she said.

"About what?"

"About me. About all of it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I knew there was a room in you that I was not allowed into," he said. "I assumed it was the camp. It is the camp for most people. I thought: she will tell me when she wants to." He looked at his hands. "I have been a good husband in the way of a man who is afraid. I have not asked. It is the one thing I could give you — that I never asked."

"Bogdan."

"I want to ask now," he said. "Not everything. But I want to ask one thing, and then I will never ask again." He looked up. "Are you going to leave?"

She got up and went round the table and sat beside him and took his hand.

"No," she said. "And I am going to tell you why, and it is not a beautiful reason. It is because in thirteen years you have never once made me into an instrument of anything. You have never once looked at me and seen a use." She turned his hand over. "And in January we are going to Poznań, and I am going to be head of a section in a voivodeship office at thirty-six years old, and nobody in Warsaw will remember my name, and I find, to my very great surprise, that the only thing I mind is that you will have to leave your flat."

Bogdan looked at her for a long moment.

"Poznań," he said.

"Poznań."

"Well," said her husband, and something in his face that had been closed for years came open a millimetre. "My mother was from Poznań. The bread is better."

---

On Wednesday night, when the flat was asleep, she took the file out of her briefcase.

It was the case file of Father Tadeusz Rybak, which she had requested and which she should not have had in her flat, and it was thin: a report from the district office, a statement from a neighbour, a list of the children who had been seen going up the back stairs at Felińskiego Street on Thursdays in March and April, and a note from the prosecutor, Zięba, saying that the case was held pending a decision on the nomination.

She read the list.

Nineteen names, written in a policeman's hand, with ages. Seventeenth on the list: *Leszczyński, Piotr, ur. 1948, ul. Belgijska 4 m. 11.*

She sat at the kitchen table and looked at her son's name for a long time.

Then she got up and opened the door of the stove, and with the tongs she took a lump of coal that was burning through at the edge, and she held it in the tongs until the flame had gone out of it and it was red at the heart, and she put it in the tin ashtray on the table.

She tore the list off the file at the perforation.

She held the paper over the ashtray and touched the corner of it to the coal, and the paper caught, and the flame went up the page in a rush, and she held it while it burned down and dropped the last corner in, and watched the ash curl and go grey, and then she took the tongs and ground the ash into powder in the bottom of the tray, and then she put the tray in the sink and ran the tap on it.

She was aware, as she did it, that she was committing a crime. It was not a large crime; it was a small crime, the sort of crime that goes into a file and is never mentioned until it is mentioned all at once, and she had spent twelve years on the other side of the desk from people who did exactly this, and she had written opinions about them.

*There,* she thought, with her hands in the cold water. *Now I am one of them.*

She went back to the table and sat down and took a clean sheet of paper and her pen.

At the top she wrote, in her small upright hand:

*OPINIA w sprawie kandydatury ks. Aleksandra Zaremby na stolicę biskupią w Sandomierzu.*

Then she sat for a while, and looked at the window, and at her own reflection in it, and at the two fingers that were dead white and the three that were not.

Then she wrote, and she wrote for two hours, and it was the best thing she had ever written, and it was the last document she ever signed in Warsaw.

At four in the morning she finished it, and read it through once, and signed her name at the bottom, and put it in her briefcase, and went to bed beside her husband, and slept for five hours without dreaming.

---

On Thursday evening she went to Żoliborz.

It was the thirtieth of October, and the first hard frost of the year had come in the night, and the pavements were white and the air smelled of coal and the sky was clear and enormous and full of stars over the black shape of the church.

The devotions were over. The doors were shut. There was one light in the presbytery window, and a lamp over the sacristy door.

She stood at the bottom of the steps for a while, with her hands in her pockets, one of which had a withy in it, cut from a fourteen-year-old willow at Wierzbica.

Then she went up the steps and knocked.

# Chapter Ten — The Second Fire

## Warsaw, 30 October 1958

The sacristy was cold and smelled of starch and old candle wax. There was a cupboard of vestments, a sink, a chair, a table with a white cloth, and a small door that led into the church.

He had put two chairs by the stove and left the confessional open, and the red lamp was burning on the altar beyond the door, and it was the only colour in the room.

"Sit down," he said.

"No." She took off her gloves and put them in her pocket. "I'll do it properly. I've been doing it improperly for fourteen years."

She went into the church and he heard her heels on the stone, and then the small sound of the confessional door, and then nothing.

He went in by the other side and sat down in the dark.

There was a grille between them, and a shelf, and beyond the grille the shape of a woman's head and shoulders in the light that came through the window from the street, and the smell of her — coal smoke and soap and cigarettes — which was, he thought, the smell of his entire life.

"Bless me, Father, for I have sinned," she said. Her voice was steady. "It is fourteen years since my last confession. I was twenty-two years old and I had just come home from Germany and I went to the church at Kielce and I stood in the door and I did not go in."

There was a long silence.

"Go on," he said.

"I don't know how to do this."

"Nobody knows how to do this. That's why it's a sacrament and not a conversation."

She laughed, once, without any air in it. Then she was quiet for a while, and when she spoke again her voice had changed.

"I have killed a man," she said. "Not with my hands. With two pages." She said it flatly, like a woman reading a charge sheet. "His name was Szymon Wróbel. He was a priest, at Opatów. He was forty-one years old and he had a mother in Radom and he taught children in four villages, and the file came to my desk and I wrote the opinion and he was sentenced to six years and he died in the prison at Sztum in November 1953 of tuberculosis. He was forty-three." A pause. "And there were others. Four others. I could give you the names but I don't think you want the names."

"I want whatever you have."

"I have the names and I have the letters from his mother and I have the fact that I answered the last one with a form. The form said that the Office was not empowered to intervene in matters before the courts." She exhaled. "I have hated my mother for dying. That is the next thing. She was shot in the yard and I was nine metres away with my sister's hand in mine, and for fourteen years there has been a place in me that says: you could have run. You could have stood in front of her. You were twenty-two years old and you had two good legs and you stood there with your mouth full of soot and you did nothing, and then you got into a lorry and you left her in the ash. And I know what a priest would say to that. I have heard it said to other women. I do not want it said to me."

"All right."

"And I said to God that I was not His." Her voice did not change. "In the compound at Leipzig, in the snow, at ten past six in the evening on the nineteenth of January 1945, I said it out loud. *Then I'm not yours any more.* And I have kept my word for fourteen years, and I have kept it exactly, and I want you to understand that it was not a thing said in grief. I have never once been sorry for it. Not once. I have been sorry for a great many things but I have never been sorry for that, and I am not sorry for it now."

"Why are you here?"

"Because I am tired," she said. "And because a good man is going to be consecrated a bishop in the spring and I want to be able to look at him."

"That's not a confession. That's an explanation."

"It's all I have."

"Then I'll ask you a question and you will answer it, and you will not explain." The grille was between them and he could not see her face and he was glad of it, because his hands were shaking. "Do you want to be forgiven?"

Silence.

"Yes," she said.

"Say it properly."

"I want to be forgiven."

"By whom?"

"Don't."

"By whom, Zofia?"

She was quiet for a long time. In the church, the wind came under the door and moved along the floor like something alive.

"By Him," she said. "And that's the thing I can't get past, father, so you may as well know it now. I don't believe He's there. I have not believed He's there since January 1945. And I still want Him to forgive me, and I still say *Him* and not *it*, and I have been standing in the dark for fourteen years saying a word I do not believe in, and I am not able to stop." Her voice cracked, and she caught it, and held it. "There is a name for that, I think."

"Yes," he said. "It's called being a human being."

"Don't be kind. Be a priest."

"All right." He put his hands together. "Then here is the priest. You are not here to be told that what you did to Szymon Wróbel was not your fault. It was your fault. You did it with your own hand and your own opinion and you were thirty years old and you knew what a prison was, and if I told you otherwise I would be stealing the only thing you have left, which is the truth about yourself. The men who killed my father wore boots. You wore a good suit and you had a fountain pen, and you did more damage in twelve years than any of them, because you were clever and you were sincere and you were in a room with a green lamp." He stopped. "And here is the other thing, and it is the same priest who says it. You are not the worst thing you have done. You are not the sum of your files. God does not keep files. That is the entire difference between Him and the Polish People's Republic, and it is the only difference that matters, and I would stake my life on it, and in about five months I am going to have to, in public, in front of a lot of people."

She made a sound in the dark, and it took him a moment to understand that she was laughing and crying at once.

"I absolve you," he said, "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Go, and say the Our Father every night for a year, and say it for Wróbel's mother, and do not say it for yourself. Not yet. Say it as a debt."

"I don't believe it."

"Then say it as a debt."

She said the Act of Contrition. She got halfway through it — *I firmly resolve, with the help of Thy grace, to sin no more* — and she stopped.

"I can't," she said. "I can't promise that. I'm going to be in Poznań with a file in my desk and I am going to write four notes a year on a man I love, and I am going to do it because it is the only way to keep him alive, and I will do it well. I can't say I'll sin no more. I'd be lying in a confessional, which I imagine is a bad place to start."

"Then say what you can say."

"I don't know what I can say."

"Say: Lord, I want to want to."

She was silent for a moment.

"Lord," she said, in the dark, in a voice like a girl's, "I want to want to."

"Amen," he said.

---

They sat in the sacristy afterward with the stove between them and the door open, and she told him what she had written.

"Read it to me," he said.

"No."

"Zofia."

"It's a state document. It's the best thing I've ever written and it will make you a bishop and it is a lie from beginning to end, because everything in it is true and none of it is the reason." She looked at the stove. "I have described you as a man of predictable convictions. I have described you as politically inert. I have described you, in the fourth paragraph, as — " She stopped, and her mouth twisted. "As *a priest whose influence derives from personal austerity rather than from any political programme, and who may therefore be expected to govern a diocese without either enthusiasm or obstruction.*"


"That's fair," he said.

"It's obscene."

"It's accurate, and it's what they need to hear, and you have spent three weeks finding the only sentence in the Polish language that could get a man like me approved by a government like that, and you will never be able to tell anyone." He leaned forward. "Zofia. What have you done to yourself?"

"I have spent myself," she said. "It's different from destroying myself. I want you to know the difference, because you're going to be a bishop and you're going to spend the next thirty years watching men destroy themselves and calling it martyrdom." She stood up and pulled on her gloves. "I am thirty-six years old. I have a husband who is better than I am and a son who is braver than I am, and I have a job in Poznań in a section with four clerks, and I have a pension, and I will die in a flat in Poznań in about 1995 and there will be eleven people at the funeral, and on the day I die I will know that I did one thing in my life that was not a document." She looked at him. "That's not nothing, father. That's a whole life."

He stood up.

"Zosia."

"Don't."

"I'm not going to."

"I know." She put her hand on the door. "That's what I love about you. God help me, it always was."

## November–December

The declaration was published on the fifteenth of November with twenty-two names and photographs of four of the signatories, and Father Czesław Dembiński's was the largest, and by the end of the month there was a joke about him going round the sacristies of Warsaw that Aleksander never repeated and always laughed at.

The Office for Religious Affairs gave its opinion on the twenty-first. The Director, Adam Turski, read Zofia's report twice, and then sent for her, and looked at her over his desk for a while, and said, "You've written a very clever document, Zofia." And she said, "Yes." And he said, "It's the best thing you've ever written and I am going to sign it, and I want you to know that I know exactly why you wrote it, and that I am signing it because you are right, and not because I am being kind." And then he said, "Poznań is a good city. The theatre is better than ours. Send me a postcard at Christmas."

The Primate's office was informed on the twenty-eighth. Rome confirmed on the eleventh of December, in a consistory that also dealt with two archbishops in South America and a bishop in Ireland, and the news came to Warsaw by telephone from a monsignor at the Vatican Secretariat of State who had been at the seminary with Bishop Rożek in 1911, and who said, before he hung up, "Tell the young man that he is the youngest since the eighteenth century and that half the episcopate will hate him for it, and that the other half will pray for him, and that he should not confuse the two."

Father Tadeusz Rybak's case was discontinued on the sixteenth of December. No reason was given. He came into the presbytery kitchen at four in the afternoon with the paper in his hand and sat down and cried for about a minute, and then blew his nose and said, "Father, I want to be a saint," and Aleksander said, "Then learn to keep your mouth shut," and Tadeusz said, "That's not what saints do," and Aleksander said, "It's what this one is going to do."

Bishop Julian Rożek died on the second of January 1959, in his two rooms on Miodowa Street, in the afternoon, with a priest saying the prayers for the dying in one ear and the other ear, the good one, turned toward the window, which was open, in the cold, because he had asked for it to be open. He had lived long enough to hear the confirmation. At the end, according to the priest, he said one thing, which was not a pious word and which Aleksander treasured for the rest of his life.

He said: "Well. Now we shall see."

## Christmas Eve

The baptism was at half past ten at night, in the sacristy at St Kazimierz, before Midnight Mass, with the door locked.

There were four people in the room. Aleksander, in a stole and a white alb. Piotr Leszczyński, ten years old, in a white shirt that his mother had ironed at nine o'clock that night, with his hair combed and wet. Bogdan Leszczyński in his good suit, holding his hat. And Zofia, in a dark coat, standing at the back by the cupboard with her hands at her sides.

There was a brass ewer and a basin, and a candle, and a card on the table on which the priest wrote the name and the date and the names of the godparents, and there were no godparents; Bogdan had asked whether it could be done without, and Aleksander had said yes, that it could be done with the parents alone in a case of necessity, and Bogdan had said, "Then it's a case of necessity."

He began in Latin, because it was the old rite and because there was no congregation and no reason not to; and then, when he came to the questions, he said them in Polish, because the boy was ten and had learned them in a flat on Felińskiego Street from a priest of twenty-six.

"Piotr. What do you ask of the Church of God?"

"Faith."

"And what does faith give you?"

"Life everlasting."

"Do you believe in God the Father Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth?"

"I do."

And so on, through the Creed, the boy's voice clear and high and slightly too loud in the small cold room, and Aleksander's thumb on his ear and his nostrils and his lips — *Ephpheta, that is, be opened* — and then the salt, and then the water.

He poured the water three times over the boy's head, and said the words, and the water went down over Piotr's face and into the collar of the ironed shirt, and the boy laughed and then put his hand over his mouth, and Bogdan Leszczyński made a sound in his throat and turned his head away.

Then the white garment. There was no white garment, so Aleksander put his own handkerchief over the boy's shoulders, which was not in the rubric, and said, "Receive this white garment, and see that you carry it unstained before the judgement seat of Our Lord Jesus Christ, that you may have eternal life."

And then he turned to the parents.

"The Creed," he said. "The godparents would normally say it for him. There are no godparents. One of you must say it."

"I will," said Zofia.

She came forward. The candle was between her and the priest and the light was on the boy's wet hair.

"I believe in God," she said. "The Father Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth. And in Jesus Christ, His only Son, Our Lord, who was conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary — "

She stopped.

The word was there. *Suffered.* She could see it. She had said the Creed every Sunday of her childhood in the church at Wierzbica with her mother's hand on her shoulder, and she knew every syllable of it, and she stood in the sacristy of St Kazimierz on Christmas Eve with her son's wet head in front of her and she could not say *suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried.*

The silence went on for four seconds. It was the longest silence of her life.

Then Bogdan said, quietly, from the back of the room:

"*Cierpiał pod Ponckim Piłatem, został ukrzyżowany, umarł i został pogrzebany. Trzeciego dnia zmartwychwstał.*"

He said it steadily, all of it, to the end — *the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and life everlasting. Amen* — and when he had finished, he put his hat back under his arm and looked at his wife.

"I was baptized in the church at Wąbrzeźno in 1914," he said. "I have not been inside one since 1949. I did not think I remembered it."

"*Amen,*" said Aleksander.

Then he took the candle and lit it from the one on the table, and gave it to Piotr, and said the last prayer, and it was over.

The boy went out first, holding the candle in both hands, walking very carefully, up the steps and through the door into the church, where the organ was already playing for Midnight Mass and the church was full and somebody was moving the crib into place under the pulpit.

Bogdan went after him, and paused at the door and looked back at Zofia, and said, "I'll be in the third pew from the back. On the left." And went out.

She stood in the sacristy with the priest.

"Thank you," she said.

"You should not thank me. I should not have done it in a sacristy at half past ten at night with the door locked, and I did it because you asked me and because I love your son, and I will have to answer for it, and I find that I do not care." He was putting the stole away. His hands were not steady. "It will be in the register. I will write it in the register tonight, and the register is seized twice a year by the district office and photographed, and in about four years somebody will read it and they will come and ask me questions. I want you to know that I know that."

"Then don't write it."

"No," he said. "It is a baptism. It goes in the book. If it is not in the book, then in fifty years there will be a man of sixty in this city who cannot prove that he is a Christian." He closed the cupboard. "That is what the book is for, Zofia. It is not a file. It is a book of names."

She looked at him.

"This is the last time, isn't it," she said.

"Yes."

"Say it plainly."

"I am going to Sandomierz in February for the consecration," he said, "and you are going to Poznań in the second week of January, and there will be no letters, and no telephone calls, and no notes through the offices, and we will not meet at the station. And in twenty years, if you are in a church in Sandomierz on a Sunday, you may stand at the back and I will know you are there, and I will not come down out of the pulpit." He said it without any drama at all, and his voice was terribly gentle. "Because you were right, in the churchyard. You were right in 1945 and you have been right all along. A dead girl is easier to be a martyr for than a living woman. And I am going to be a bishop, and a bishop who is in love with a woman in Poznań is a man with a weakness that every clever man in this country will find in a month. They would not destroy me with it. They would destroy you, and my Church, and in the end they would destroy the boy, and they would do it slowly, with files, and they would call it administration."

"I know."

"So this is the last time."

"Give me the letter," she said.

"What?"

"The letter. The one I gave you. The first one, from June 1945." She put out her hand. "I want to take it to Poznań. I want to have one thing in that flat that a man wrote to me in his own hand when he thought I was dead."

He went to the drawer and took it out and gave it to her. It was worn at the folds now, from a month of being carried in a breviary.

She folded it and put it inside her coat, against her ribs.

"Plant the willow," she said.

"What?"

"At Sandomierz. There'll be a bit of ground. There's always a bit of ground. Plant it in the spring and water it in August, because August is when it dies if you don't." She picked up her gloves. "It's a withy from the tree at Wierzbica. The one I planted the day you went away to Warsaw. It's fourteen years old and it's eight metres high and it doesn't care about either of us."

He laughed.

"I'll plant it," he said.

"Good." She put her hand on the door. "Merry Christmas, father."

"Merry Christmas, Zosia."

She went out into the church. It was full — three hundred people, more, standing in the aisles — and the candles were lit, and the smell of the pine and the incense and the wet coats came over her, and she went up the side aisle to the third pew from the back on the left and slid in beside her husband and her son.

The organ began.

And Zofia Kędzierska, who had not sung in a church since 1944, opened the book that Bogdan held out to her and found the hymn, and sang.

## The first note

She wrote it on the train, in the second week of January, in a compartment with a soldier and two nuns and a man with a hen in a basket.

*Notatka służbowa. Dot. ks. A. Zaremby. Okres: grudzień 1958 — styczeń 1959.*

*Subject was in Warsaw throughout the period. He conducted the Christmas services at the parish of St Kazimierz, Żoliborz, with the usual attendance (the Midnight Mass was full; the dawn Mass at six o'clock had approximately eighty persons, which is higher than last year). He visited the sick in the district, twenty-two visits according to the parish register. He received three letters from Kraków and one from Kielce, all of which appear to be from clergy.*

*He was confirmed by the Holy See on 11 December. The consecration is expected in February.*

*Assessment: no change. Subject remains what he has always been. He will not conspire and he will not sign. He is predictable and he is honest and I do not believe that he can be bought or frightened into anything, and I would advise that he be left alone, because a man who is left alone does less harm than a man who is watched.*

*Kędzierska Zofia.*

She read it over. It was two pages. Everything in it was true. There was nothing in it that could be used.

She put it in an envelope and sealed it and wrote on the front, in her upright hand, *Colonel B. Rygier, MSW, Warsaw*, and put it in her bag, and looked out of the window at the flat white fields going past and the lines of willows along the ditches, and the smoke going up from the chimneys of villages whose names she did not know.

The soldier was asleep with his mouth open. One of the nuns was saying the rosary with her lips moving. The hen made a small sound in its basket.

Zofia took out the letter — the one from the fourth of June 1945 — and unfolded it, and read it the way you read a thing you know by heart, for the pleasure of being right.

## Wierzbica, 4 August 1959

The anniversary Mass was at nine in the morning, and there were sixty people there, and the bishop said it in the old church of St Bartholomew with Father Radoń, who was seventy-two and almost entirely deaf, sitting in a chair in the sanctuary in his vestments, and the whole village came, and afterward there was bread and salt and a barrel of beer in the churchyard, and it was, by the standards of Wierzbica, a great occasion.

He took off his vestments in the sacristy and came out into the churchyard in a black cassock with the purple at his waist, and the people came up to him — the old women first, and then the farmers, and then the children, who had been told to kiss his ring and did not know how, and a man of about forty with one arm who had been in the forest at Wygoda with Antek Kędzierski and who said, "You don't remember me, Excellency," and Aleksander said, "I do. You were the one who could not sing."

She was at the back, by the cemetery wall, in a grey coat, with a small boy of eleven standing beside her and a man of forty-five in a good suit standing a little apart with his hat in his hands, looking at the graves.

He came down the path with his hands behind his back, and stopped in front of her, and the village watched with the intense, polite, devouring attention of a village.

"Pani," he said.

"Excellency," she said.

"I heard you were in Poznań."

"I heard you were in Sandomierz."

There was a pause. The boy looked from one to the other.

"Piotr," said Zofia. "Go and find your father. He's looking at the stones by the wall."

The boy went. The bishop watched him go.

"He's grown," he said.

"He's eleven. He serves at the eight o'clock Mass at St Adalbert's in Poznań, and he is insufferable about it." She looked at the churchyard, at the sixty people, at the beer barrel, at the stones. "There is a tree at the presbytery in Sandomierz. I have it from a reliable source."

"It's four metres high. It has taken." He paused. "I water it in August."

"Good."

They stood for a moment in the sun, in the noise of the churchyard, and it was not awkward; that was the strangest thing. It was as though two people who had been holding their breath for twenty-one years had finally, at the edge of a cemetery in a village in the hills, been allowed to put something down.

"I am going to ask you a question," he said, "and you may tell me to go to hell."

"Ask."

"Are you happy?"

She thought about it. She thought about a flat in Poznań with three rooms and a good stove and a window on a courtyard with a lime tree in it, and a job in a section with four clerks, and a husband who had learned, at the age of forty-five, to ask her one real question a week and to cook a tolerable Sunday dinner, and a son who was insufferable about serving at Mass. She thought about the four notes a year, which she wrote in the manner of a woman filling in a form about the weather, and the fact that Rygier had written to her twice and she had answered twice, and the fact that she had said the Our Father every night for nine months, for a woman in Radom she had never met.

"No," she said. "Not happy. Not the way people mean it." She looked at him. "But I am not in the dark either. I have somewhere to put it. That's more than most people get."

He nodded slowly.

"I have to go," he said. "There is a confirmation at Kielce at four."

"I know. I read the programme in the paper. You are to be photographed with the voivode."

"God help me."

"He is a very dull man and he will say something about the harvest." She put out her hand. "Goodbye, Aleksander."

He took her hand. His hand was warm and dry and had not changed at all.

"Goodbye, Zosia," he said.

He went up the path. She watched him go — the black cassock, the purple, the slow, careful walk of a man in his late thirties who has a bad side from a wound in 1944, greeting the old women, stopping to put his hand on the head of a child — and then she turned and walked down through the graves to the river.

The willow was there. It was fifteen years old, and it was nine metres high now, and the wind was coming up the valley and moving the whole crown of it at once, silver on the underside and green on top, and the water below it was brown and slow and full of August light.

She sat down on the root with her knees up, the way she had at seventeen, and she took out of her pocket a wooden rosary with worn beads, and held it in her hands without saying anything, and let the tree do what trees do.

Behind her, up the hill, the bell of St Bartholomew's began to ring for the end of the Mass, in the tower that had stood since 1683, and the sound went out over the village and the fields and the wood and the hills beyond, and the ash of the old burned village lay in the ground under the grass, and the church had a roof on it, and would have one next year, and the year after that. The bell rang and rang and stopped, and the valley went back to its own noise, which was the river and the wind and a dog barking at nothing at all.

She did not drop the coal.

She carried it.

*Here ends* Two Fires.

## A Note on History and Invention

The world of this novella is real; the people in it are not.

Between 1945 and 1989 the Polish state managed its churches through an Office for Religious Affairs, which vetted candidates for bishoprics, licensed catechists, approved or refused the building and closure of churches, and maintained, in cooperation with the Security Service, files on priests. The Church's own position in those years was not simple: the 1950 agreement between the Episcopate and the state conceded a great deal, and the Primate, Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński, was detained from 1953 to 1956. The confrontation over the Millennium — the Church's Great Novena and the state's counter-celebration of a thousand years of Polish statehood — was gathering through the late 1950s, and the campaign of "patriotic priests" and loyalty declarations was a real instrument of that struggle. The Warsaw Rising of 1944, the pacification of villages in the Kielce region, the deportations to forced labour in Germany, and the long, quiet war over the catechism in a flat on a back staircase are all drawn from the historical record.

Everything else — Wierzbica as it appears here, the Kędzierskis, the Zarembas, the parish of St Kazimierz in Żoliborz, Colonel Rygier, the see of Sandomierz and the men proposed for it — is invented. No character in this book is a portrait of a real person. Where the historical record was ambiguous, I have preferred the version that best served the truth of the lives rather than the truth of the file.

The Polish is left in the Polish. A few words, for the reader who wants them: *pani* (madam, Mrs), *panna* (miss), *ksiądz* / *ojciec* (Father), *towarzyszka* (Comrade, fem.), *powiat* (district), *voivodeship* (the largest regional administrative unit), *dwór* (the manor house), *gimnazjum* (academic secondary school), *złoty* (the currency), *śliwowica* (plum brandy).

## A Note on the Two Fires

The proverb in the epigraph is not in any collection. It was put into the mouth of a woman who could neither read nor write, and who taught her daughter the faith by the only method she had, which was to say the same true thing every day of her life until it wore a groove in the child. It is the kind of thing such a woman would have said. That is how folk sayings begin, and it is also, I have come to think, how novels begin.
