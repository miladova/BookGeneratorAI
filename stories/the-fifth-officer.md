# The Fifth Officer

### A story of the *Titanic*, April 1912

*For everyone who was told a thing that was not true, and believed it, and lived.*

---

## I. The New York

At noon on the tenth of April, the *Titanic* sounded her whistle, and across the water the *New York* came loose from her moorings.

The sound was not a horn. It was something vast being torn, a noise that came up through the deck and through the soles of Arwen's boots and settled in her back teeth. Beside her, Aunt Gwladys said, "Sweet Mary," and crossed herself, which was a thing she did in ships and thunderstorms and was not, strictly speaking, Anglican.

The *New York* was a small liner, black-hulled, tied up at a dock across the water. The *Titanic*'s engines had barely turned over, but the water between the two ships had begun to move in a long, slow suck, and the *New York*'s hawsers went off one after another like a boy snapping a whip: crack, crack, crack. The last of them parted with a sound like a bell being struck. Then the smaller ship swung her bow out into the channel and came on towards them, sliding sideways, patient and enormous and entirely without malice.

Men were running on the quay. A tug put her nose into the gap and blew black smoke, and somewhere below decks the *Titanic*'s engines reversed with a shudder that ran the length of the ship and came out at Arwen's heels. The *New York* came within a hundred yards. Then fifty. Then a distance no one on the boat deck could measure except by the sudden quiet of two thousand people holding their breath.

The tug *Vulcan* took the strain. The *New York* checked, swung, and lay still.

The crowd along the quay began to cheer, because a crowd will cheer anything that does not kill it. On the boat deck, a steward in a green baize apron picked up a dropped tray and said, to no one, "Well."

"Well indeed," said Gwladys. Her hand was still flat against her collarbone. "That is an omen, and I do not care what the Bishop says."

"It is displacement, Aunt. All that water has to go somewhere."

"You," said Gwladys, "are the only woman on this ship who would explain a miracle." She gathered her stole around her shoulders. "The very first thing the boat does is nearly drown another boat, and my niece gives me the physics."

Arwen laughed, which surprised her. She had not laughed in some weeks, and it came out rusty, like a hinge. Below them the water churned itself calm again, and the *Titanic* gathered way, and the docks of Southampton began, without any ceremony, to be somewhere else.

She was thirty years old. She had a first-class ticket in her glove, a cabin on C deck, two trunks and a hatbox and a japanned tin deed box, and a black coat with a collar of astrakhan that her father had bought her in Shrewsbury the winter before he died. She was going to New York because her father's business partner had written to her in February about a signature that was required, and because a woman of independent means may travel where she likes, and because a town the size of Barmouth is a very small room in which to keep a secret.

The secret was in the tin box. Two letters, tied with the ribbon off a box of chocolates, in her father's hand: *My dear Sir, I must tell you plainly that my daughter is not for you* — and then, further down the page, the lie, written out in the same round commercial hand in which Robert Prys had invoiced coal and rope and ships' biscuits for thirty years. He had written it twice. He had written it to two different men. The second one was the mate of a coastal steamer who had asked, very respectfully, for permission to walk out with her, and who had received in reply the news that Miss Prys was promised elsewhere.

She had found them in the autumn of 1909, in the bureau in the back parlour, in the compartment where he kept the deeds and the insurance and the bottle of gin he believed nobody knew about. She had sat on the floor with them in her lap for two hours. Then she had put them in the tin box and put the tin box in her trunk and gone down to the kitchen and made tea, and told the girl that the fire wanted another scuttle, and taught four children their scales that afternoon, and played the offertory on Sunday, and been, in every particular that anyone could see, Miss Arwen Prys of Abermaw, who kept her father's house and had a lovely touch and had never married.

At Cherbourg, the tenders came out to them in the dark and the first-class passengers who had come down from Paris clattered aboard, and a woman in a sable coat complained about the stairs. At Queenstown, the next morning, the tenders brought the Irish. Arwen stood at the rail of the after promenade and watched them come up the gangway with their cardboard suitcases and their bundles tied in twine, a boy carrying a canary in a cage, a woman with a shawl over her head who stopped at the top of the gangway, turned round, and looked back at the green land for a long moment before she stepped out of it. The sea was flat and grey and the gulls were screaming over the tender, and Arwen thought: *That is what it looks like. That is the face a person makes.*

That night the ship began to run. The engines settled into a note you could feel in the deck rather than hear, and the cold came down out of the north, and the *Titanic* went on across the Atlantic at twenty-one knots, all her lights burning, her brass polished, her paint still soft in the corners of the smoking room, as though she had been built for a world in which nothing bad had ever happened.

## II. A Voice in the Corridor

She saw him on Friday, at six in the evening, on the boat deck, and she did not know him at first. That was the strangest part, afterwards. She had spent eleven years able to summon his face at will — the crooked tooth, the sun-cracked skin across his nose, the way one eyebrow went up when he was teasing — and the man coming along the deck towards her, in a blue serge jacket with two gold stripes on the cuff, had a face like a locked door.

It was the voice that did it.

He was walking with the second officer, a tall, fair, flat-footed man named Lightoller, and they had stopped by number six boat, and the tall man was saying something about the falls, and then the other one said, in Welsh, half under his breath, "Duw â'n helpo ni, mae hi'n oer," and laughed.

The deck tilted under her. Just slightly. It was not the ship.

She had been four days at sea and had not heard a Welsh voice once. She had heard Lancashire and Boston, and a man from Buenos Aires who had made a fortune in frozen beef and told her so twice, and a colonel who explained the Empire to her over the soup. She had begun to think of Barmouth as a place that had been taken off the map while she was not looking, and then here it was, coming out of the mouth of a stranger who was not a stranger, in the ordinary complaint of a man who is cold and says so.

"Arwen," she said. Her own name, out loud, which was not what she had meant to do.

He turned. She watched him come up out of his own face the way a man comes up out of deep water: slowly, and then all at once, and then badly.

"Miss Prys," he said.

"Mr. Lowe."

They stood there. Lightoller looked from one to the other of them, and made the sort of noise a man makes when he has decided not to be told something, and went away along the deck.

"You are on this ship," Harold said. It was not a question, but it had the shape of one.

"I am in C deck. Number seventy-four." She heard how stupid it sounded. "I did not know you were at sea. That is — I knew you were at sea. I did not know you were here."

"No," he said. "Nor me." He took his cap off and put it on again, which was a thing he had always done when he did not know what to do with his hands. His hair was thinner at the front. There were two deep lines between his brows that had not been there at twenty. "I am fifth officer," he said, as though she had asked him to account for himself. "I came aboard at Belfast in March. Before that the *Belgic*, before that the *Tropic*. This is my first trip over the Western Ocean, if you can believe it. Thirty years old and a beginner."

"Twenty-nine."

"Thirty in November." A pause. "You have not changed."

"That is a lie," she said, "and you know it."

Something moved at the back of his eyes, and for a moment he was the boy on the harbour wall again, and the deck was the sand at Abermaw at low tide with the sun going down behind the hills and the whole grey estuary laid out like a table for a feast nobody had invited them to. Then the moment closed over, and he was an officer of the White Star Line, standing under the davits with his cap on straight.

"I had heard you were married," he said.

"Then you heard wrongly."

"I heard it in Barmouth. In — " He stopped. "It does not signify."

"It signifies a great deal," said Arwen. "Who told you?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me."

"Your father," he said. "In a manner of speaking."

The wind came across the deck and took the ends of her veil and pulled them out straight, and she stood there with her hands at her sides and her heart going like a bird in a box, and she thought, with a clarity that frightened her: *He does not know. For eleven years nobody has told him, and he has believed it all this time, and he has been walking about the world under it.*

"I am sorry," he said, misreading her face. "It was a long while ago. I should not have—"

"When?" she said. "When did he tell you?"

"Nineteen-two. The spring." He looked at the sea, not at her. "I wrote to you from Las Palmas. I had been three weeks off my head with the fever and I wrote to you the first day I could hold a pen, and I got it back in the autumn with a note from your father saying you were to be married at Michaelmas and that it would be kinder if I did not write again."

The wind went on pulling at her veil. Somewhere below, a steward was ringing the dressing bell, and the sound came up through the deck with the flat, cheerful persistence of a thing that has no idea what it has interrupted.

"Michaelmas," Arwen said.

"I burned it," he said. "You may think what you like of me for it, but I burned it, and I have been glad of it since."

"Harold."

He looked at her then.

"There was no marriage," she said. "There has never been any marriage. I was told that you died of fever on the coast. In April of nineteen-two. My father told me, and the minister told me, and there was a service, and I wore black for a year, and my mother's sister — Gwladys, she is with me, she is on this ship — she sat up with me for three nights because I would not stop crying, and I was nineteen years old, and I believed it because I could not think of a single reason why two men would lie to me."

The deck was very quiet. The dressing bell had stopped.

"Your father," he said at last, in a voice that had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a man taking a bearing in fog, "is a thoroughgoing—"

"He is dead," she said. "Three years. There is nobody left to be angry with but me."

"Why you?"

"Because I have known since nineteen-nine," she said, "and I did not write to you."

She had thought about this moment for three years. She had rehearsed it on the walk up to Dinas Oleu and in the back of the shop and in the small hours when the tide was coming in and the whole house smelled of salt. In none of her rehearsals had she said the true thing first, and now that she had, she found that there was nothing else to say, and that the two of them were standing in the cold off the Grand Banks of Newfoundland with a gulf between them wider than the one the ship had crossed.

"I wrote to Liverpool," she said. "The White Star office. It came back unopened. I did not know what ship. I did not know if you were alive. And after a while I decided that a man who wanted to find a woman would find her, and that I had already made a fool of myself once over a dead sailor, and that I would not do it twice." She lifted her chin. "There. That is the whole of it. You may think what you like of me for it."

"I will tell you what I think," he said, "when I have had a night to think it."

He put his cap on. He was steady. She had expected anger, or tenderness, or some large weather, and instead there was this — a man in a blue jacket with gold on his cuff, holding himself still, the way men in his profession are trained to hold themselves still when something has gone wrong at sea.

"I am on watch until ten," he said. "Tomorrow I am off at four. The boat deck, aft of the second funnel. If you would come."

"People will talk."

"Let them," said Harold Lowe, and went away down the deck with his hands behind his back.

## III. The Reading and Writing Room

The reading and writing room was on A deck, forward, and it was the most beautiful room Arwen had ever sat in, and she hated it.

It was all cream and rose, with windows on the sea and little writing desks set into the walls, each with its own brass lamp and its own small pot of ink and its own leather blotter, and the chairs were upholstered in a silk the colour of weak tea, and there were flowers, real flowers, brought up from somewhere below by a boy whose whole employment, she suspected, was to keep them from dying. It was a room designed on the theory that a woman with a pen in her hand is a woman at her ease. Arwen had conducted most of her correspondence in her life at a table in the back parlour with the shop ledger on one side and the cat on the other, and she had never once, in either place, felt as though somebody had built a room for her.

She sat down on Saturday afternoon with a sheet of the ship's paper in front of her — *RMS Titanic*, embossed at the top, with the White Star burgee — and she wrote, *Dear Harold*, and then she looked at it for a long time.

Here is what she wrote, and did not send.

> Dear Harold,
>
> I have been sitting here for an hour, and I have begun this letter eleven times, and each time I have got as far as your name and stopped, so I am going to write it all down now and decide afterwards what to do with it.
>
> You asked me why I did not write. I have given you the answer that a proud woman gives, which is that I did not know where you were. That is true. It is not the whole truth.
>
> I did not write because I was afraid. Not of you. Of the letter coming back. Of finding out that the man I had been grieving for had a wife in Liverpool and three children and a house with a gate, and that my grief had been, all along, a small ridiculous private thing that nobody else in the world was attending to. I had made myself the widow of a man who was not dead. I had got used to it. It was the only shape my life had. If I wrote to you and you answered, I would have to put the shape down, and I did not know what I would be then.
>
> And there is this, which I have never said to anyone, and which I will say to you because you are the only person living who has any right to it: I was angry with you for dying. I was angry with you for eleven years. I thought, if he had loved me he would not have gone to Africa, he would not have gone to sea at all, he would have stayed and swept out the shop and been a man in a town and been nothing, and I would have had him. And then I found out that you had never died, and that you had written to me, and that my father had made a fool of us both, and the anger had nowhere to go, so it came home. It has been living in this house with me since I found them out. It has been very good company. It has kept me from writing this letter.
>
> I am going to New York because Mr. Hughes requires a signature, and because I have never seen anything, and because I cannot walk down the street in Abermaw without wondering which of the people who nod to me know that Robert Prys's daughter was promised to a dead man who was not dead. That is the truth of it. It is not a very dignified reason for crossing an ocean.
>
> I have written some songs. That is a thing I do now. I have set six of the old poems and one of my own, and a Mr. Aldrich in Boston has agreed to look at them, and I have brought them in my trunk wrapped in my mother's shawl, and I have not the least idea whether they are any good.
>
> I am thirty years old. I have a house and an income and a good name and no one to spend it on. I have never in my life chosen anything for myself. I want you to know that I am trying to begin.
>
> If you would rather I did not come tomorrow, send word by the steward and I will not. I will not think less of you. I have thought a great deal less of you for a great deal less reason.
>
> Arwen

She read it back twice. The second time she found that her hands were shaking, and that the pen had left a small blue star on the corner of the paper where she had pressed too hard.

She folded it, and put it in the blotter, and did not send it.

At half past six she went down and dressed for dinner, and at eight she sat at a table for six with her aunt and the colonel and a Mr. and Mrs. Cardew of Philadelphia, and ate consommé and salmon and a saddle of lamb, and talked about the weather, and did not once think about the letter because she was thinking, instead, about the boat deck at four o'clock, and the way he had said *let them*, and about how a man's voice can cross an ocean and a lie and a funeral and come out the other side saying something about the cold.

At nine the band played in the reception room: a waltz, and then a selection from *The Merry Widow*, and then, quite unexpectedly, a hymn tune Arwen knew before the second bar — Joseph Parry's *Aberystwyth*, the tune they had sung at the chapel at home on Sunday nights all her life, written by a Merthyr man who had gone to America and come home again. She put her cup down. Gwladys glanced at her and said nothing, which for Gwladys was a feat of enormous self-control.

Later, when the first-class passengers had gone in to their coffee and the second-class passengers had begun their hymn singing in their own saloon below, the sound of it came up the stairwell, thin and brave and slightly flat: *Jesu, Lover of my soul, let me to thy bosom fly.* Two hundred people who had never met her, singing a thing she had known since she could stand. She stood in the corridor with her hand on the rail and listened until it finished, and then she went to her cabin and took off her earrings and sat on the edge of the bunk with her coat still on, and thought: *Tomorrow at four. Tomorrow at four.*

At twenty minutes to twelve, the ship's engines stopped.

She noticed the quiet before she noticed the sound. That was the thing she could never explain afterwards, to the men with notebooks in New York: that the first sign had not been a noise but the absence of one, the great humming floor of the world going out from under her, and then, a heartbeat later, a long soft grinding somewhere far forward, like a giant moving a chest of drawers across a stone floor.

The glass of water on the washstand rang once, faintly, against the marble, and was still.

## IV. Ice on the Promenade

Her first thought, and she was ashamed of it for the rest of her life, was: *this will spoil tomorrow.*

She got up. She put on her wrapper, lit the lamp, and looked at her watch: a little after midnight. The ship had a way of trembling when she slowed — a fine vibration through the floor, like a cat purring — and it had stopped, and in its place there was only the sea going past her porthole, and the sound of somebody laughing in the corridor, and then somebody saying, quite clearly, "No, no, it's nothing at all, the stewards say it's nothing."

She sat down on the edge of the bunk. She had crossed to Dublin twice and to London a dozen times and on none of those crossings had the engines stopped in the night.

At five minutes past twelve she went out. Gwladys was already in the corridor with her hair in a plait over her shoulder and her dressing gown buttoned to the throat and her Bible in her hand, which was the most Gwladys thing she had ever done.

"Well," said Gwladys. "The berg."

"What berg?"

"There is ice on the promenade deck," said Gwladys. "The steward told Mrs. Foster, and Mrs. Foster told everyone, and now the whole ship knows except you, apparently. There is ice, and a man with a camera has been up to photograph it, and Mr. Cardew from dinner has given his account to four separate strangers in my hearing. He says it was a hundred feet high. It was not a hundred feet high. It was not twenty feet high, because I have seen a twenty-foot wall of a thing in my time and I know what it looks like."

"Aunt."

"It is very cold," said Gwladys. "Put your coat on. And put your boots on, not your slippers. And bring your pocketbook."

They went up. On the A deck promenade a group of men in evening dress stood around a heap of ice the size of a footstool, and one of them was prodding it with the toe of his shoe. The deck was dusted with something that crunched. Two boys were sliding on it. A steward went by with a tray of whiskies and said, "Mind your step, madam, the deck's slippery," in the voice of a man who had said it four hundred times.

It was, in every particular, a joke. That was the thing Arwen could never make the inquiry understand, later, when the senator kept asking her in what tone of voice she had been told. The ship was not behaving like a dying animal. She was standing still, all her lights on, her boilers banked, her orchestra gone to bed, her deckhands in no particular hurry, and around her eleven hundred people were deciding, one after another, that they were cold and would go back inside. It was not terror that kept them at the tables. It was that the *Titanic* was the safest word in the English language and they had bought a ticket that said so.

She found Harold at half past twelve by the forward starboard davits, with his jacket off and his sleeves rolled, shouting at a knot of men who had come up from second class with their lifebelts on back to front.

"Not like that. Not like — here." He took the thing off a man's shoulders and turned it round. "The tapes go at the front. You are not carrying a sack of coal. If you go into the water in that, you will go in upside down and I will not be responsible." He looked up and saw her. "You should be dressed."

"I am dressed."

"You are dressed for dinner." He took her by the elbow and walked her four steps away from the crowd, and his hand was very hot through her sleeve, and his voice when he spoke again was low and quick and entirely without ornament. "Listen to me. The ship has struck ice. She is making water forward. I do not know how much and neither does anybody else, and I will not have you standing about in a velvet coat while other people are being told to put their lifebelts on. Go down. Put on everything you have got, every woollen thing, and your boots, and take your aunt, and come back up to the port side and stay there until somebody tells you what to do. And bring a woollen hat."

"Aunt Gwladys says it was a very small berg."

"Aunt Gwladys is a better seaman than half the crew." He looked past her at the boat deck, at the empty falls, at the boats swinging gently in their davits above the glass-smooth sea, and something went across his face that she would see again in other men, in other years, in a field hospital in Étaples: the face of a man who has done the arithmetic and does not intend to say the answer out loud. "There are sixteen boats," he said. "Sixteen. For two thousand two hundred people."

She said, "That cannot be right."

"It is not right," he said. "It is the law." He put his jacket on and began buttoning it. "Go and put your boots on, Arwen."

She went.

## V. The First Boat Away

She came back at ten minutes past one, wearing two skirts, three petticoats, a woollen jersey under her blouse, her hat pinned down hard, and her father's astrakhan coat, which had been a mistake, because it was the heaviest coat she owned and she would not take it off for two days. Gwladys came behind her in a black coat and a hat with a pheasant's feather in it that she had worn to her own sister's funeral and evidently considered appropriate to the occasion.

The boat deck had changed. It had become a place where women were crying quietly and men were being loud, and where an officer Arwen did not know was standing at number six with his arm out straight, saying, "Women and children only, please, this way, no men, stand back, sir, stand back—" and a woman was saying, over and over, "But he is only eleven, he is only eleven," and the officer was saying, "Then let him come, madam, quickly."

A rocket went up. It made a soft thud and then a hiss, and then a white star opened over the ship and hung there, and in its light Arwen saw the whole boat deck at once: the boats swung out and hanging, the crowd black and packed at the rail, the officers running, the ice on the deck like spilled sugar, and beyond the rail, out to port, the flat black nothing of the Atlantic, going on and on and on.

"Gwladys Prys," said her aunt, in the voice she used for the front pew. "If you think I am getting into a boat before every mother on this ship has got her children into one, you have not been paying attention for thirty years."

They stood together at the port rail by number eight. The boat was lowered and lowered and then stopped, and a woman in it called up, "There's only one seaman aboard, we can't row," and a man's voice from the deck said, "Then learn," and the boat went down the rest of the way into the black.

"Where is he?" Gwladys said.

"Starboard side. Loading the aft boats."

"And you want to go to him."

"Yes."

"Then go," said Gwladys, "and do not be foolish about it. I am seventy-one years old, I have buried a husband and two brothers and a sister, and I have been perfectly content on this ship until this evening, and I do not intend to be the reason that a girl of thirty stands on a sinking deck holding my hand." She took her niece by the chin, the way she had done when Arwen was six. "If you are going to die, do it in a way you can tell me about afterwards. Now go."

She went. Afterwards she could not remember the distance from the port rail to the starboard side — perhaps eighty feet, perhaps a hundred — and she could not remember who she pushed past, or what she stepped in, or whether she was afraid. She remembered the cold. She remembered a woman's fur coat lying on the deck where it had been dropped, and a shoe, one shoe, a small black shoe with a buckle, and a man sitting on a coil of rope with his head in his hands, and a boy of about fourteen handing out lifebelts from a heap and saying, "Take two, take two, there's plenty."

There were not plenty.

On the starboard side, number five was swinging out. Harold was at the falls, and there was a crowd around him, and in the middle of the crowd, in a black coat and a bowler hat and a face like a man who has bought a ship and is now watching it be mismanaged, was Mr. Bruce Ismay.

"Lower away," Ismay said. "Lower away. Lower away!"

Harold turned round. Arwen heard it from twenty feet away, in the flat, carrying, entirely level voice of a man who has reached the end of his patience in public: "If you will get out of the way, I will lower away."

The crowd made a sound. Ismay stepped back. Harold put his hands on the falls and said, "Right. Women and children. Into the boat, into the boat, come along, ma'am, take the child, take the child — no, sir, not you, sir, this boat is for women."

A man in a dinner jacket said, "I am her husband."

"Then you may stand here and wave to her," said Harold, and the man went on saying something, and Harold said, without heat, "Sir, I have forty boats' worth of people and sixteen boats. If you would like to make a speech about it, do it on that side of the rail."

Arwen came up beside him. He looked down at her, and his hands did not stop moving on the rope, and his face did not change at all, and he said, "Get in the boat."

"No."

"Arwen—"

"There is a woman at the rail behind you who has been trying to get forward for ten minutes and cannot, because there is a wall of men in front of her, and she is holding a baby that is not hers and she has lost the woman who gave it to her." Arwen's voice was not steady, but it was loud enough. "Her name is Mrs. Dooley and she is from Kerry, and if you put her in that boat I will get in it with her and not before."

For one second — one second and no more — Harold Lowe looked at her, and in his face she saw something she had no business seeing on that deck, in that hour, in front of four hundred strangers: not love, or not only love. Recognition. The look of a man who has been given an unexpected thing in the middle of a battle and has decided, on the spot, that it is worth keeping.

Then he turned and bawled, "Make a lane there! Let the woman through! You — you with the child — come along, come along."

Mrs. Dooley came through with the baby. Arwen put her hand under the woman's elbow. The boat was level with the deck now, grinding softly against the ship's side, and a seaman was saying, "Step in the middle, ma'am, step in the middle, don't look down," and Arwen looked down.

The sea was not far below at all. That was the first thing the inquiry could never make sense of, and she tried four times to explain it: the sea was not far below. It was perhaps fifteen feet below the rail, and the great black hull went down and down from it into nothing, and the water was so still and so black that she could see the ship's lights in it, all of them, perfectly still, as though there were another *Titanic* down there, upside down, going about her business.

"Arwen."

She turned. Harold had her by the wrist.

"I am going to ask you a question," he said, "and you will answer it truthfully, because I have not got time to be lied to. Can you swim?"

"Yes."

He nodded once, as though she had passed an examination.

"Then listen. When you get to the water, pull for that light." He pointed. There was a light out on the black, low down, far off — a ship, or a star on the water, so small that she had to look away and look back to find it again. "The boats will go in different directions and the current will take them. Row for the light. If you cannot see the light, row for the dark place and keep rowing. And if you see anyone in the water who is alive, you pick them up. Do you understand me? Not the dead. The living. It is no good filling the boat with the dead."

"Harold—"

"Get in the boat."

She got in the boat.

The boat was lowered. She sat between Mrs. Dooley and a girl of sixteen who was crying without making any noise at all, and she watched the ship from below as she had never seen her, a black cliff with golden windows, and she listened to the creak of the falls, and she heard, from somewhere far up above her, a man's voice shouting, "Both falls together! Both falls! For God's sake, keep her level!" and then a sound like a whip, and then the boat dropped.

They hit the water with a slap that went through her spine. Water came over the gunwale in a black sheet, and the women screamed, and a seaman at the bow shouted, "Bale! Bale! Use your hats, use your shoes, bale!" and Arwen took off her hat — the good hat, the one with the bird — and bailed with it, and it was ruined, and she laughed, and Mrs. Dooley looked at her as though she had gone mad, and perhaps she had.

## VI. The Boat with the Man in It

Number fourteen was a lifeboat of the old pattern, wooden, clinker-built, thirty feet long, with a square stern and a mast lying along the middle and oars in the thwarts and a plug in the bottom that nobody could find. There were fifty-odd people in her by the time she was away. Arwen counted them at some point in the night and lost count and counted again, and the number came out different every time.

The officer in the stern sheets had his cap off and his hair down over his forehead and he was standing up, which is a thing you do not do in a lifeboat, and he was using an oar as a boathook, and he was shouting at the ship.

"Keep back! Keep back there! I say keep back!"

Number fourteen had come down opposite the open deck space where the third-class passengers had been kept and then released, and the water had reached it, and the ship was listing to port so that the rail was almost at the sea, and there were men on the rail, and there were men in the water, and there were men already swimming towards the boat with their lifebelts up around their ears.

The boat began to be pulled over. Not by one man, or two, but by a great soft sideways weight of them, hands on the gunwale, hands on the oars, hands on the shoulders of the women, a man's face coming over the side close to Arwen's, mouth open, eyes white, streaming water, and under the water his hands were gripping the boat's planking and the whole boat was going over.

The officer in the stern stood up straight.

He had a revolver in his hand.

There was a shot. Arwen did not see where it went. The second shot went into the air and the third one, she learned later, went along the side of the hull into the water, and the men in the water let go, and the boat came back up off her beam ends with a lurch that threw three women off the thwarts, and the officer said, in a voice that carried the length of the boat and out across the water, "Get back, or I will shoot you all like dogs. I mean it. Get back." And then, to the rowers, in a completely different voice, quiet and almost apologetic, "Give way. Give way there. Get her away from the ship."

The boat pulled off into the dark. Behind them the *Titanic* stood up out of the sea, not sinking, not dying, just standing there, golden, enormous, with her band playing somewhere aft and her boats gone and her bows settling slowly under.

Arwen sat with her hands in her lap and her ruined hat in her hands and thought: *That is my first love. That is the boy who carried my books up Dinas Oleu and bought me a paper cone of chips with his own money and was sick over the harbour wall on his first trip out and wrote to me every month for four years.*

"Are you all right, ma'am?" said the seaman beside her.

"Yes," she said. "No."

"You'll be better at the water," he said. "Everyone's better at the water."

At a quarter past two, the ship's lights came on again whiter and brighter than before, and the whole sea around was lit up like a ballroom, and a woman behind her said, "Oh, thank God, she's got her engines back," and the officer in the stern said, "No. That's her generators giving out. Hold on to something."

Then the lights went out.

Not all at once. It was like watching a person go to sleep. The lights went out along the decks and then out along the boat deck and then, far aft, in the second-class saloon where Arwen had heard two hundred people singing *Aberystwyth* the night before, they went out, and there was a sound of boilers, and then the stern came up. She stood up out of the sea like a horse going over backward, and she was black and huge against the stars, and for a long moment — a minute, perhaps, two — she simply stood, and the shape of her was so enormous that it made no sense at all, that a thing that large could be held up by water that quiet.

Then she went.

Arwen had expected — and here she had been wrong in a way that only somebody who has never seen it could be — that she would go down like the pictures: bow first, sliding, a smooth descent. She did not. She went down like a building coming apart, with a roar that went through the sea and through the boat and through Arwen's chest, and the roar went on and on, and she understood with a horror that she had no words for at the time and has never found any since, that the sound was not the ship. It was everything. It was china and coal and pianos and glass and the ship's great engines tearing loose from their beds and every door on every deck banging and every bell in the world ringing at once, and inside the sound the small human noises were already inaudible, already gone, already past.

The wave came out from her. It was not a great wave. It came through the boats, a low, runnelling swell that lifted number fourteen and put her down again, and then the sea was quiet, and where the ship had been there was nothing at all but a wide, still, grey place, and things floating in it.

The officer in the stern said, "Now."

He said it to himself. He said it the way a man says it when he has come to the end of a thing he has been doing for two hours and has to begin the next. Then he raised his voice: "Out oars! Out oars! Everybody, get an oar — you, ma'am, you there with the hat — get hold. We're going back."

Nobody answered him at first. Then a woman said, "Back?"

"Back," he said. "There's people in the water."

"We can't," said a man's voice from the bow. "There'll be hundreds of them. They'll swamp us. I saw what happened the first time, I saw—"

"I saw it too," said the officer. "I was there." He was stripping off his coat now, and his cap was gone, and he was undoing his boots one at a time and throwing them into the bottom of the boat. "There's no room for a rush, and no good going in among them with a full boat, so we're going to do it properly. You — Scarrott — you come with me, and you — " he pointed, "— you, and the boat's crew, that's five of us, and the rest of you stay at the oars and pull when I say pull. We'll take one at a time. We'll take what we can."

"Who's in charge of the boat?" a woman said. "If you go in the water, who's in charge?"

Arwen got up. She had been sitting with her hands in her lap for a full eleven minutes, and she found that her legs worked, and that her voice, when she spoke, came out perfectly calm.

"I am," she said. "Go on. I'll keep them at the oars."

She could not see his face in the dark. She could see him turn. Then he said, "Miss Prys has got the boat," as though he were reading it off a list, and swung his leg over the gunwale, and was gone into the water.

Somebody gave her a tiller. Somebody gave her a rope. The men at the oars were a shoemaker from Cork and a kitchen porter of nineteen and a man from the steerage who could not speak English and a woman in a fur coat whose rowing, once she understood what was wanted, turned out to be the best in the boat. Arwen stood in the stern with the light of nothing at all to steer by and said, "Give way. Easy. Easy. There. Hold her."

They went back through the dark.

It was the noise she could not bear. Not what the inquiry wanted to know about, and not what a newspaper could print, and not what she ever said aloud afterwards except once, in a garden in Deganwy, in a low voice, to her daughter. It was the noise of a great many people in the water, a thousand or more, at a distance of four hundred yards, and the noise they made was not screaming. It was calling. One man shouting *here*, and another answering, and another, and a boy's voice going up high and breaking, and somebody in the dark beginning the Lord's Prayer at a gallop and getting as far as *thy will be done* and then not being able to go on. And underneath it a sound like a crowd in a stadium when something has happened at the far end, a low, sighing, terrible sound, that Arwen understood, and wished with her whole heart she had not, was the sound of people who had understood that nobody was coming.

And then a woman's voice, very close, quite calm: "If you please. I have a baby."

The two seamen brought her in over the side. She was a broad-faced young woman with a shawl over her head and she had no baby with her, and when they had got her into the bottom of the boat she said, "It was in a boat. It went in a boat. I gave it away. She said she would take it in the boat," and then she said, "I want to sit down," and did.

They got three more. A fireman, oil to the eyebrows, who had been in the water twenty minutes and whose legs did not work. A young man in evening clothes who could not remember his own name and who was dead by dawn and who had forty-two pounds in his pocket, and a letter from his mother, and a ticket stub from a theatre in London. And a Jap, or so Arwen took him to be, who was alive and said nothing at all and lay in the bottom of the boat looking up at the stars, and who was in the end the most famous of them all, though he never knew it.

They did not find anybody else, because by then there was nobody else to find. The officer came back over the side with his shirt plastered to his back and his teeth going and his hair full of something Arwen decided not to look at, and he said, "Nobody. Nobody there," and sat down hard on the middle thwart, and put his face in his hands.

Then he stood up again and began, methodically, to get the mast up.

"Put the sail on her," he said. "I want everyone who can stand to get under the thwart and move about. There's no good sitting down and being cold. You, madam — Mrs. — the lady in the coat — if you will take that tiller, I will see to the mast."

The woman in the fur coat objected to being called madam and said her name should be Countess of anything, and he said "Then you'll hold the tiller, my lady," and she did, and rowed, and steered, and would later write a letter to the White Star Line that Arwen wished, very much, she had thought to write first.

At some point in the small hours, when the sky began at last to pale out at the edge of the world, Arwen sat down beside him.

He was in his shirt, no coat, no cap, no boots, and he had given one of the blankets to the woman who had lost the baby and put the other over the shoulders of the boy in evening clothes, who was by then past caring. Somebody had found a bottle of brandy in a pocket of the night, and it was going round, and nobody was drinking much of it.

"Arwen," he said.

"Yes."

"You did well."

"I did nothing."

"You steered," he said. "You kept the women at the oars. There was a minute, when I was in the water, that I thought the boat had gone over, and I looked up, and there was the boat, and you were standing there shouting at a countess, and I thought, well." He stopped. He had a way of stopping, she remembered, that made you want to fill the space, and she did not fill it, and after a moment he went on. "I don't remember what I thought."

"You thought I was a nuisance," she said.

"I thought I should like to marry you," said Harold Lowe, "and that it was a pity neither of us would ever see the land again."

The sea was pale. Somewhere to the north there was a light, a good red light, coming on. The men at the oars stopped and stood up in the boat, and someone shouted, and a fireman fired a green flare from a box on the floor, and the light came towards them slowly, growing a funnel and a deck and aerials and the shapes of six thousand people lining her rails.

"We'll do it in the morning, then," Arwen said.

He turned his head. He had been up for twenty-two hours. His face had paint and salt and something worse on it, and one of his eyes was completely red where a cinder had got into it in the stokehold, and he was beginning, very slowly, to shake, in the way that men do when the thing they have been holding off is finished at last.

"Do what?"

"Get married," she said. "In the morning. On the ship. You said we wouldn't see land again. So it will have to be at sea."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, cracked, entirely ungovernable laugh, out there on the grey Atlantic with the boats coming together and the *Carpathia* sounding her whistle across the water, and Arwen thought it was the best sound she had ever heard in her life, and she took his hand, which was cold and filthy and shaking, and held it in both of hers.

## VII. The Carpathia

The *Carpathia* had no room for them and took them anyway.

They came up the side in a sling, one at a time, the women first, and the Cunard stewards caught them at the top and wrapped them in blankets and gave them hot brandy and tried to take their names. The ship had been bound for Fiume with a hold full of Hungarian emigrants' luggage and a first-class list of people going to the Adriatic for their health, and by eight in the morning she had seven hundred survivors on her decks and no beds and no coffee and no idea what to do next, and she did it all perfectly.

Arwen came up last but three. She had refused to leave until the boat was empty, because she had been in charge of it, and because Harold had looked at her once, across the thwarts, and said nothing at all, and she had understood that this was the last thing he would ever ask her and that she would do it.

The deck was a wall of people. She stood in a borrowed coat with her ruined hat in her hand and heard, from somewhere in the middle of it, a voice she had known all her life, saying: "—and I tell you, madam, that if you had been in my boat you would have had a great deal more to complain of than the damp—"

"Aunt Gwladys."

Gwladys turned. Her hat was gone. Her plait had come down and her hair was standing out around her head like a dandelion, and there was a great smear of something black down the front of her coat, and she had a cup of tea in one hand and a Cunard blanket round her shoulders, and she was, Arwen saw with a lurch, entirely herself.

"There you are," said Gwladys. "I have been asking after you for two hours and nobody on this ship knows anything. They put me in a boat with a woman from Manchester who talked the entire time about her sister's kidneys." She put the tea down on the deck. Then she opened her arms. "Come here."

They stood in the middle of the crowd and held on to each other. Gwladys smelled of wet wool and the tea and, faintly, of the lavender water she had put on before dinner on Sunday, which was a hundred years ago.

"I lost my hat," Gwladys said into Arwen's shoulder.

"I lost mine."

"Yours was a better hat." Gwladys let go, and straightened her sleeves, and picked up her tea. "Now. Where is your officer?"

"He is not my officer."

"He has been my officer since Friday evening," said Gwladys, "when you came back to the cabin with your face like a girl in a novel and told me you had been talking to a man about the weather. I am seventy-one. I am not blind. Where is he?"

Below, on the boat deck, men were still coming up out of the last boats. Arwen looked over the rail and saw him standing in the stern of number fourteen with his shirt open at the throat, holding the boat steady against the swell while the last three women were lifted off, and she thought — with a clarity that came to her, she found later, only in the worst hours of her life and never in the good ones — *There. That is what he is. That is what he has always been. Everything else was just the weather.*

---

She did not see him again until the afternoon.

By then the *Carpathia* had turned her nose for New York and was going as hard as her engines would stand, and the crew had cleared one of the dining saloons and put mattresses on the floor and the survivors were lying on them in rows. Arwen had spent the morning with a pencil and a piece of the ship's notepaper, going from mattress to mattress, writing down names and addresses and the names of people who had not been saved, because a woman in a black dress with a baby had asked her to, and then another woman had asked her, and then it had become her occupation.

Harold came in at three o'clock with a list of his own in his hand and his cap on straight and his hair wet, and he did not look at her. He went to the purser's table and put the list down and said, "That's mine. Number fourteen. Sixty-odd living when she left the ship, four more out of the water, four dead in the bottom of her. The dead are in the forward baggage space if you want them counted properly. I have put the names on the back."

"Thank you, Mr. Lowe."

"There was a boy," Harold said. "In evening clothes. About twenty. He had a letter from his mother in his pocket. If you've a man who can write a letter, the address is on the envelope, and I want it done today, before the newspapers get hold of it and put his name in a list and get it wrong."

"I'll see to it."

"Thank you."

He turned round then, and saw Arwen with her pencil and her notepaper, and for a moment something in his face came loose.

"Miss Prys has been taking names," said the purser, who had no idea.

"Yes," said Harold. "She would."

---

They found a place on the after deck at dusk where the wind was less, behind a ventilator, and sat down on a coil of rope like two people who had run out of chairs.

"I want to say something," he said, "and then you can say what you like to me."

"Go on."

"I've been going over it since four o'clock this morning. I have gone over it about a thousand times and I am no better at it now than I was then. When I came back to the ship's side — the second time, when we went in — there was a man clinging to an oar. I got him off the oar and into the boat. And there was another man, further out, and I could not see him properly, and I went for the one I could see. And when I looked again the other one was gone." He was looking at the deck. "There was a woman on the top of a deckhouse. I heard her. I could not find her in the dark. There were six of us in the water and there were a thousand of them, and I could not take a thousand, and I knew it, and I chose. That is what I did. I chose."

"Yes," said Arwen.

"You are supposed to tell me that I did all a man could."

"I know what I am supposed to tell you."

"And what will you tell me?"

"That you did what could be done, in the dark, with a boat that would have gone over, and that the choosing was not a sin, it was the arithmetic." She had her hands folded in her lap. It was very cold. "And that if you had been a worse man you would not have gone back at all, and you would be sitting on the other side of this ship this evening with a dry coat on, telling some passenger that you were sorry but it had been every man for himself. There were boats that did not go back. You know that better than I do. You went. Now you will carry the rest of it for the rest of your life, and you will find — I am sorry to tell you — that it does not get lighter. It gets further away. That is not the same thing."

He was quiet for a while.

"How do you know that?" he said.

"Because I have been carrying a funeral for a man who was not dead," said Arwen, "for eleven years, and I have got very good at it, and I would not recommend it. Harold. Look at me. I am not going to tell you that you are a hero, because I know you and you would not believe me and it would be a lie of a kind. I am going to tell you that you are alive, and that I am alive, and that the sun is going down on Monday, and that there is a very great deal to be done."

He took her hand. It was the first time he had done it in daylight, and his fingers were cold, and he held on hard.

"Will you marry me," he said, "on this ship, tomorrow, before we dock? The captain can do it. They do it at sea. I have seen it done."

"No."

He looked up, startled.

"Not because I do not want to," she said, "and not because you are not the man I have been waiting for since I was seventeen. Because I will not marry you in a borrowed coat with a press photographer at the rail, and I will not have the first thing we do be a thing the newspapers can print the day after. And because, Harold — you are not yourself. You are the fifth officer of the *Titanic* this week. Every man in America is going to want to shake your hand and every newspaper is going to want a piece of you, and if I marry you on Wednesday I will never know, for the rest of my life, whether you married me on Monday because you meant it or because you were frightened. So. No. Ask me in the autumn, in Barmouth, when it is raining, and there is nobody in the room but us and my aunt, who will have opinions."

He laughed. It was not the cracked laugh of the boat; it was a small one, tired, but it was the right one.

"You are a hard woman, Arwen Prys."

"I am a woman who has been dead once already," she said. "I do not intend to be a widow twice."

## VIII. The Warrant

New York was worse than the sinking.

That is a strange thing to write, and she wrote it in her journal only once, in 1912, and never again. But it was true. The sinking had been a thing that happened to her, in the dark, and she had done what was in front of her to do. New York was a thing that people did to her, in the light, for eleven days.

They came into Pier 54 on Thursday evening, the eighteenth, at half past eight, and there were thirty thousand people on the streets. There were boats on the river with searchlights on them. There was a woman on the pier with a megaphone reading out names, and each time she read one the crowd made a sound, and each time she read a name with a number after it the crowd made a different sound. Gwladys held Arwen's arm and said, "Do not look at them. Look at the ground and keep walking and do not answer a single question." Arwen looked up once, and a man in a bowler hat took her photograph, and the flash went off close enough that she saw white for an hour afterward.

They were taken to the Waldorf with the other survivors and given a room and a bath and a dinner that Arwen could not eat, and at nine the following morning there were three reporters in the lobby who had somehow got her name, and by noon they had printed it wrong. The *New York Evening Journal* called her "Miss Arwen Price, of Wales, a heroine of the boats." The *Times* did not mention her at all. A man from the *World* came up to her in the corridor and asked her to describe, in her own words, the moment the ship went down, and when she said that she would rather not he said, "It's for the record, ma'am. People want to know," as though that settled it.

She wrote to Mr. Aldrich in Boston on the second day and received, on the fourth, a note in a large, kind, absolutely illegible hand that said he had read the songs, that he would see her on Friday, and that she was to bring the seventh. He kept her for two hours in a room full of books and made her play the second one through four times, and then he said, "These are not very good yet, Miss Prys. But the third one is good. And the seventh one is very good, and I do not think you know it." He offered her fifty dollars for nothing at all, which she refused, and a letter to a publisher, which she took.

On the fifth day a boy brought a card to the hotel. It said *Harold G. Lowe, Fifth Officer, RMS Titanic*, and underneath, in pencil, in a hand that had been writing in a hurry: *I am not allowed out. They have served a warrant on me. Come to the Waldorf at four and bring your aunt, if she will come, as a chaperone, and I will buy you both the worst tea in New York.*

The American inquiry had begun on the nineteenth. They had served him with a warrant the moment he set foot on the pier, as though he were a criminal, and he had spent two days in a courtroom in the Munsey Building being asked by a senator from Michigan what an iceberg was made of.

"I said, 'Ice, I suppose, sir,'" he told them, over the tea, which was indeed terrible. "The man had been at me for an hour about the colour of the ice and the shape of it and whether it was a berg or a floe, and I had not slept, and I had just come from a room where they had showed me photographs of my own boat full of dead people, and he asked me what an iceberg was composed of, and I answered him."

Aunt Gwladys laughed until she had to put her cup down.

"Did you tell them about the water?" Arwen asked.

"I told them everything I could remember and then they asked me the same thing in eleven different ways, and I could not make them understand that a boat is not a room, and that a man in the water cannot be heard unless he is close, and that we were four hundred yards away and the current was taking us south." He turned his cup round on the saucer. "They want a villain. They have got Ismay, if they want one, and they will have him by the ears before the week's out, and I will not help them do it. I was not on the bridge. I do not know what the captain knew. I know what I did, and I have told them what I did, and now they will write it down and make of it what they like."

"They will call you a hero," said Gwladys.

"They will call me a man who fired a revolver at drowning men," said Harold, "because that is also true, and they will print it in a paper next to a picture of a woman with a baby, and by Friday there will be two Harold Lowes in the world and neither of them will be me."

They sat in the great gilded lobby of the Waldorf with the tea going cold, and Arwen looked at him, and saw that he was thinner than he had been on Friday, and that the skin was drawn tight under his eyes, and that his hands, which had been steady enough on the falls, were not steady now.

"Then we will not read the papers," she said.

"You will read the papers."

"Only the shipping news."

---

They called her to the inquiry in the second week.

She had not expected it. She was a first-class passenger, a woman, a nobody from Merionethshire with a tin box and a letter from a music publisher, and she sat in the great hot room with her gloves in her lap and answered questions for two hours about the boats.

The senator was a large, patient, thorough man with a way of asking a question and then waiting, which she recognised, because her father had done it across the shop counter for thirty years. He asked her how many people had been in her boat. He asked her whether she had been told the ship was unsinkable. He asked her, four times and in four ways, whether the officers had done their duty.

"He asked me the same thing in eleven different ways, too," she said afterwards.

"Miss Prys," the senator said, "you have testified that you steered the boat while the officer went into the water. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"Was that usual?"

"I have no idea. I had never been in charge of anything before in my life."

There was laughter in the room. The senator did not laugh.

"Did you see the officer fire his weapon?"

"I saw a flash and I heard three reports."

"At what?"

"I do not know, sir. I was at the tiller of a boat that was going over, and there were men coming in over the gunwale on both sides, and I was trying to keep four women at the oars."

"Would you say the officer acted properly?"

And here Arwen Prys, who had been brought up to say nothing in public, and who had been taught by her father that a lady's opinion is a private thing, and who had spent three years being quiet in a small town, took a breath and heard her own voice, quite steady, going out across a room of three hundred people.

"I would say," she said, "that he was twenty-nine years old and had a boat with sixty people in it that was filling with water, and that he used the only means he had to keep it from going over, and that afterwards he took his boots off and went into the sea to fetch more, and that when he came back he could not stop shaking for an hour and did not let anybody see it. I would say that the Senate may sit in judgment on him if it likes, and that I do not think it will get a truer account of that night than the one he gave you himself, unprompted, on Monday, when he could have said nothing at all."

The room was entirely silent.

"Thank you, Miss Prys," said the senator. "That will be all."

She stood up. Her knees were not working properly. She got to the door of the room before the noise began behind her, and a boy with a notebook caught her sleeve in the corridor, and she said, "Not one word from me, if you please," and went down the stairs and out into the street, where it was raining, and where she stood on the pavement for a moment with her hand over her mouth and thought: *Well. Now you have done it. Now there is no going back to Abermaw.*

---

He was waiting for her on the corner under an umbrella that was not his.

"You said it," he said.

"You were there?"

"Two rows from the back. Lightoller got me in." He was white to the lips. "Arwen. You have made me—"

"Do not," she said, "tell me what I have made you."

"I am going to have to live in that. Do you understand? I have got to go home to Barmouth and be that man. There will be a reception. There will be a gold watch. And every man in that town whose son did not come back will look at me and think—"

"That you lived," said Arwen. "Yes. They will. And you will stand there and take it, because you are not a coward and I have seen you do worse things than stand still. And then you will come to the shop and have your tea, and I will tell you that you are a fool, and we will go on." She took the umbrella out of his hand and held it over both of them. "Do you know what I did in that room? I told the truth, on the record, in public, in front of a senator of the United States, because a man I love was being made into a villain by men who were not there, and it is the first thing I have ever done in my life that nobody told me to do. I am not going to apologise for it. I would do it again tomorrow."

He stood in the rain with her. Somewhere behind them a newsboy was shouting something about the *Titanic*, and would go on shouting it, in one form or another, for the rest of their lives.

"I have to go back on the *Adriatic*," he said. "The second of May. And then the inquiry in London, and then whatever they give me next." He swallowed. "Ask me in the autumn."

"I intend to."

---

She gave him the letter on the last evening, in the lobby, with Gwladys sitting ten feet away pretending to read a newspaper.

"I wrote it in the reading and writing room," she said, "on the Saturday, before any of it happened. I have carried it about ever since. I was going to burn it, and then I thought that you had a right to it, and that I had been a woman who kept things in a tin box for three years, and that I was finished with that."

He read it standing up, with his cap under his arm. She watched him get to the part about being angry, and stop, and read it twice, and then go on.

When he had finished he folded it along its folds and put it inside his jacket, against his chest.

"I will not answer it in writing," he said. "I am no good in writing. I will answer it in the autumn, in Barmouth, when it is raining."

## IX. Barmouth, in the Rain

The town met him at the station.

They had put bunting across the street and the Band of the Royal Welsh Fusiliers had come down from Wrexham, and there were children sitting on their fathers' shoulders along the whole length of the High Street, and the *Cambrian News* said afterwards that there were thirteen hundred people in the Picture Pavilion, which had been built for eight hundred, and that the crowd outside had been unable to get in and had stood in the road singing.

Arwen did not go.

She stood at the window of the shop — *R. Prys & Daughter, Chandlers*, though the sign had not been repainted since the daughter was nineteen — and watched the tops of hats going past towards the Pavilion, and made a pot of tea she did not drink, and answered the door three times to people who wanted to know whether she was going down to see the officer.

At half past four the horns began, and she put her cup down and sat still.

At six there was a knock at the shop door, and she opened it, and Harold Lowe stood there in his best blue, with his cap in his hand and his hair not sitting down properly, and a small square parcel under his arm.

"You did not come," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because there were thirteen hundred people in the room and I did not want to be the fourteenth hundred," said Arwen, "and because I wanted to have this conversation somewhere there was not a band."

He came in. He had to duck under the beam: the shop was low, and smelled of tar and rope and the paraffin they kept in the back, and there were coils of marline and a tin of brass screws and a heap of ships' biscuit on the counter, and it was the least nautical place that a chandler's shop could possibly be, and he stood in the middle of it and looked round like a man come into a church he had not been in since he was a boy.

"I have brought you a watch," he said.

"I know. It is in the paper."

"They gave me this one as well this afternoon. The Mayor. It has got the date of the sinking on the back of it and the words *gallant services*." He put the parcel down on the counter. "I would like you to have it."

"Harold."

"I have got a watch. I have got a watch from the town and a watch from the company and a medal from the Board of Trade and a piece of paper from the Senate of the United States, and every one of them says something about me that I'm not sure is true. I want one thing in this world that says something about me that is." He pushed it across the counter. "Open it."

She opened it. It was a travelling clock, not a watch — a little brass clock in a leather case with a strap, and the card inside said, in a draper's hand: *For Miss A. Prys, who steered No. 14, from H. G. Lowe, who did not do it alone.*

She stood in the shop and cried, which she had not done once, in New York, in the Senate room, or on the deck of the *Carpathia* with a thousand people around her.

"It is raining," she said, when she could.

"It has been raining since Tuesday."

"Then you had better ask me now."

He put his cap on the counter. He was thirty years old, and he had been at sea since he was fourteen, and he had been in a boat on the Atlantic in the dark and he had not been afraid then, and standing in a chandler's shop in Barmouth with the rain coming down in the street behind him, he went the colour of a beetroot and could not find the words.

"Arwen Prys," he said at last. "I have loved you since I was seventeen years old and I have never once managed to say it in the right order. Will you have me?"

"Yes," she said. "But not because of the *Titanic*. Because of the coasting boats and the letters and because you carried my books up Dinas Oleu and because you wrote to me from Las Palmas when you could not hold a pen. Now come into the back and have your tea, and then you may tell me the whole of it again, slowly, and I will tell you what a fool you are, and then we will go and see my father's grave, and you may stand there and be angry with him for as long as you like, because I have been doing it for three years and I have got very good at it."

He laughed, that small tired laugh, and put out his hand, and she took it, and they went through into the back parlour, where the fire was in and the kettle was on and the cat was asleep on the ledger, and where the tin box sat open on the table with two letters in her father's hand lying beside it.

Harold picked one up. He read it standing in front of the fire, with the rain running down the window and the band playing somewhere off towards the Pavilion, and when he had finished he folded it and put it back in the box and closed the lid.

"Three years," he said. "You have had these three years."

"I have."

"I would like to have met him," said Harold, "so that I could have hit him."

"You would have liked him," said Arwen. "Everyone liked him. That was the trouble with him." She poured the tea. "Sit down. There is a great deal to arrange and no time at all. I want to be married in the autumn, before the weather, and I want it done in the chapel and not in the paper, and I want Gwladys to have a new hat."

"That can be arranged."

"And Harold—" She put the cup in front of him. "When they ask you, in fifty years, what it was like — the ship, and the boats, and the water — you may tell them whatever you like. But you will not tell them that you were brave. You will tell them that you were terrified and did your work, and that there were a thousand people in the sea, and that you went back for four of them, and that four was not enough. And they will not understand it. But it will be true."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the rain went on coming down on Barmouth, on the slate roofs and the harbour wall and the grey estuary where two children had once sat and watched the ships go out.

"All right," he said. "I'll tell them that."

---

They were married in the October of the following year, in the chapel on the hill, with Gwladys in a hat that was, by general consent, a great deal too large. They lived at sea and then ashore, and there were two children, and a house at Deganwy with a garden that ran down towards the water. The little brass clock stood on the mantelpiece in the front room for thirty-one years, and it kept excellent time, and it was never once wound by anybody but her.

She never spoke of the *Titanic* in public. He spoke of it as little as a man can who is asked about it in the street. But there was an evening in the garden in the summer of 1926, when their daughter was nine and had just learned to row, and the tide was coming up over the sands and the light was going gold behind the hills, when the child asked him what it had been like, and he was quiet for such a long time that Arwen thought he would not answer.

Then he said: "There were a thousand people in the water, and I had a boat, and I went back for four."

The girl waited. When nothing else came, she said, "Was that enough?"

"No," said her father.

And Arwen, who was standing at the end of the garden with the secateurs in her hand, said, "It was the arithmetic. Go and wash your hands for supper."

## A Note on the History

Harold Godfrey Lowe (1882–1944) was a real man: born at Eglwys Rhos in North Wales, raised at Barmouth, at sea from the age of fourteen, fifth officer of the *Titanic*, and the only officer to take a lifeboat back through the wreckage after the ship went down. He was thirty years old in April 1912 and had never before crossed the Western Ocean on a White Star liner. He answered Senator Smith's question about icebergs with "Ice, I suppose, sir," was served with a warrant the moment he landed in New York, and came home to a reception in Barmouth at which thirteen hundred people crowded into the Picture Pavilion. He was awarded the Board of Trade's Sea Gallantry Medal and, later, the Royal Naval Reserve decoration; during the war he served in the Royal Naval Reserve and rose to commander. In 1913 he married Ellen Marion Whitehouse, and they settled at Deganwy and had two children.

Arwen Prys is invented, as is her family, her songs, and the letters in the tin box. The *New York* did part her lines in Southampton harbour; the band did play Parry's *Aberystwyth*; there was a *Carpathia* steward with too few blankets and too much tea. The rest is fiction, and means no disrespect to a man who went back for four.
