# The Name Beneath the Ink

*A short story*

*Warsaw, September 1943*

## I

The officer knew her before she knew him.

Marianna Frontczak was counting her children at the mouth of the passage: Janek beside the wall, Aniela carrying the potatoes, Piotr with his hand inside her coat pocket because the cuff of his own coat had worn through. Three. All three. A truck had stopped across the street, and counting was something her body did before she could forbid it.

Two soldiers climbed down. Neither carried his rifle as though he expected to use it, which meant nothing. A woman ahead of them turned back into the passage. Janek moved half a pace in front of his mother.

“Stay,” she said.

The officer emerged from a staff car behind the truck. He was broad through the shoulders, his field-grey tunic pulled tight when he bent to retrieve a glove. A major, she thought. She had learned ranks as other women learned the price of flour.

Then he straightened.

There was a pale break in his left eyebrow.

For an instant she was twenty-two again, instead of forty-two, standing in a rented room while a young man held a bloodied towel to his forehead and accused a cupboard door of attempting murder.

The street continued without her. A wheel struck a stone. Piotr tugged her pocket. The officer looked up, and she saw recognition take possession of his face.

“Marysia?”

He said it quietly. That made it worse. The little name crossed the pavement with the intimacy of a hand laid on her neck.

Janek turned towards her.

She had not seen Klaus Adler since February 1924. Nineteen years and seven months. There had been times when she could not have said whether his eyes were grey or blue. Now she remembered the small brown fleck in the right one, and the recollection filled her with such disgust that she almost let go of Piotr.

The major came towards them.

“Frau Tarnowska?” he said, correcting himself too late.

“Frontczak.”

Her voice sounded ordinary. She was grateful to it.

“Of course. I beg your pardon.”

Behind him, a soldier was consulting a paper. The street had become interested in them while pretending not to be.

“Your children?” Klaus asked in German.

She answered in Polish. “Yes.”

Janek was seventeen, taller than she was, with Antoni’s long fingers. Aniela, thirteen, had been watching the soldiers’ boots. Piotr was eight and still believed adults sometimes asked questions because they wanted to know the answers.

“Are you a friend of Mama’s?” he asked.

“No,” Marianna said.

The word seemed to strike Klaus physically. He lowered the glove he had been holding.

“We knew one another a long time ago,” he told the boy in careful Polish.

Marianna turned Piotr by his shoulder. “We must get home.”

“Your husband—”

She looked directly at the eagle above his breast pocket.

“What about my husband?”

He did not finish. Something in her face had taught him what questions cost.

The soldier with the paper approached, saluted, and spoke of an address, a courtyard, an inspection. Klaus listened without looking away from Marianna. Then he took a pencil from his pocket.

“Where do you live?”

“No.”

“Please. I only wish—”

“No.”

She stepped around him. For three strides she expected a hand, an order, Janek doing something unforgivable. Nothing happened. The truck’s engine coughed behind them.

At the next corner Aniela whispered, “Mama, the potatoes.”

The paper bag had split. Two potatoes lay in the gutter twenty paces back.

“Leave them.”

“We paid—”

“Leave them.”

They walked home with the remaining potatoes held in Aniela’s skirt. Marianna counted the children again at the stairs, and again inside the room, though she had watched each one enter.

Only after she had bolted the door did she discover that she was still waiting for the young man to come after her, laughing, without the uniform.

She bent over the washbasin and was sick.

## II

Rywka was sitting under the table, mending a stocking in the best light. She had arranged the hanging cloth so that she could see the window without being seen from it.

“Someone came,” she said.

Marianna gripped the basin.

“Who?”

“Mrs Róża. She left a turnip.”

On the table lay a turnip with one rotten side neatly cut away.

Janek shut the window. Their room smelled of wet wool and the soap Marianna made by stretching the last proper bar with things she preferred not to name.

“Who was he?” he asked.

“An acquaintance.”

“He called you Marysia.”

“Take your brother downstairs. Not into the street.”

“I’m not a child.”

“Then do something useful without making me ask twice.”

He looked at her, hurt before he was angry. She had meant to protect him and had found the quickest way to make him feel small. That, too, had become a household economy.

Aniela took Piotr instead. Janek remained by the window until Marianna sat down.

Rywka drew her legs farther beneath the table. She was twelve, though hunger sometimes made her appear younger and the way she listened sometimes made her seem old. In the room she answered to her own name. On the stairs she was Zosia, Marianna’s niece from near Lublin, whose mother had died of a fever. Her mother had in fact been called Estera, and Marianna did not know where she had died, or whether there had been time for anyone to learn.

Mrs Róża, the midwife downstairs, had brought Rywka in June. The girl had previously been hidden by a family who could no longer keep her. She had arrived with a change of underwear, a spoon, and a paper giving her a different childhood.

“Was it about me?” she asked.

“No,” Marianna said. “He knew me before.”

“Before the war?”

“Before all of you.”

Janek turned. “You knew a German officer?”

“He wasn’t an officer then.”

“What was he?”

“A man who came here to help sell railway equipment.”

The inadequacy of this description was almost comic. Klaus had been twenty-four and convinced he could make a living translating engineering specifications while writing a book about bridges. He had stayed with a merchant who bought suits from Marianna’s father. She had copied invoices in her father’s shop, her German learned from a grandmother who had insisted no language was the property of the people presently abusing it.

Klaus had asked her to correct a phrase. She had corrected three.

He had come back the following afternoon with another document containing an error so foolish that even then she had suspected him.

Janek said, “And you were friends.”

“Yes.”

She would not put the rest into the room while Rywka sat beneath the table calculating whether an unfamiliar man might kill her.

Their father’s coat hung on the nail behind the door. Antoni had been arrested in November 1941. Someone at the workshop had been found with illegal papers; the Germans had taken six men. There had been one message from Pawiak, written in a hand she knew better than her own. Since then there had been rumours of a transport, a returned parcel, a man who thought he had seen him. Marianna had learned to distrust certainty and to crave it.

Janek touched the coat’s sleeve.

“You mustn’t see him again.”

“I did not arrange to see him today.”

“If people noticed—”

“I know what people notice.”

Rywka’s needle had stopped.

Marianna knelt beside the table. “You can come out. There’s nothing happening now.”

“Not now,” the girl repeated, testing the limits of the promise.

“No. Not now.”

She would give her no larger lie.

## III

The summons arrived two mornings later in an envelope addressed to Frau Marianna Frontczak, née Tarnowska.

The inclusion of her maiden name frightened her more than the official stamp. Someone had looked for her and found the right woman.

The bearer was a Polish clerk with an inflamed eyelid. He waited while she opened it. She was instructed to present herself at a military supply office concerning the provision of laundry services.

“Who told them I take washing?”

The clerk shrugged. “You can tell them you don’t.”

He meant it as a joke. When she failed to smile, he examined the stair rail.

She went after leaving the children precise instructions. If she had not returned by dusk, Aniela was to fetch Mrs Róża. No one was to seek information at a German office. Janek protested at this last instruction, which was how she knew it was necessary.

The supply office occupied a school. Its banister still bore the scars left by generations of children sliding downstairs. In the corridor a soldier carried a stack of clean towels past a chart showing the human skeleton, whose Polish labels had been crossed out.

Klaus rose when she entered his office.

She remained by the door.

“You found my address.”

“I asked.”

“People answer you.”

He glanced at the chair he had pulled out. “Please sit down.”

She did not.

There was more grey in his hair than she had seen in the street. His wedding ring had worn a shallow groove around his finger. On the desk stood a photograph turned away from her.

“I should not have spoken to you like that,” he said.

“Which part?”

“Your name. In the street.”

“No.” She watched him struggle to understand. “You should not have come here.”

A typewriter worked in the next room. Each pause seemed an opportunity for someone to listen.

“I wanted to know whether you needed help.”

“Is this a question you ask everyone?”

He drew the chair back under the desk.

“I heard about your husband.”

“What did you hear?”

“That he was arrested. Nothing more.”

“Then you heard what everyone in my building knows.”

“I can make inquiries.”

“And what will you ask for in return?”

He reddened. It comforted her, briefly, to find she could still hurt him. Then she thought of the uniform and felt foolish for wanting so little.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing is usually what people call a price they haven’t named.”

He went to the window. Outside, someone shouted at a cart driver.

“I deserved that.”

“I’m not here to give you what you deserve.”

She had not planned these sentences. On the stairs she had been afraid she would weep or plead or become the girl who had once believed his opinions worth arguing with. Instead there was this sharp, capable stranger speaking through her mouth.

Klaus turned back.

“The laundry contract is real. The field hospital needs civilian washing done outside the city. There are payment vouchers. Soap. Transport.”

“I wash for people who pay in potatoes.”

“Then this might be better.”

“For whom?”

He said nothing.

She looked at his desk. Requisition orders, a telephone, a rubber stamp on a little wooden stand. So many harmless objects gathered together to make a dangerous room.

As she reached for the door, he said, “I wrote to you.”

She stopped.

“For a while,” he added.

“Yes. I remember.”

“I thought you might have married.”

“After you stopped writing.”

“My father was ill. The business—”

“You needn’t improve the story. You left, and then you ceased to answer. It was an ordinary thing to do. I survived it.”

He flinched at the last word.

She wished she had chosen another. He had no right to hear it as reassurance.

## IV

In 1923, Klaus had owned one good jacket and a bicycle he had bought from a man who may not have owned it. He wore the jacket to meet her father and rode the bicycle into a ditch on the way to meet her aunt.

For years she had kept these incidents separate from the serious part of her life, like buttons saved from a dress that no longer fitted. Now she found she had preserved the whole dress.

They had walked beside the river when the evenings grew long. He had talked about inflation at home, about his mother pawning a necklace, about the stupidity of men who thought another war could repair the last one. Once, when she asked whether all Germans spoke so solemnly about foolishness, he had kissed the inside of her wrist instead of answering.

He had never promised to stay in Poland. She had never promised to leave it. They had behaved as if this was a difficulty that affection would eventually grow competent to solve.

When his father fell ill, he went to Breslau. The first letters were long. The later ones concerned duties and money. Her final letter came back months afterwards, the envelope grubby and the address crossed through.

She had not met Antoni until that winter.

Antoni repaired printing machinery and rarely said anything that required a second meaning. On their third walk he brought an umbrella although the sky was clear, because she had said her shoes leaked. When they married in 1925, she was not heartbroken. She was happy in a way she had not thought to expect: without having to watch herself being happy.

Now, while the children slept, she lay beside the space where he should have been and resented Klaus for having become part of the room.

At dawn she went downstairs to Mrs Róża.

The midwife listened while cutting strips from an old sheet. She was sixty, broad-handed, with the dry patience of a woman who had spent her life arriving after other people’s decisions.

“Did you love him?” she asked.

“A long time ago.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“Yes.”

“And now?”

“He’s a German major.”

Róża measured a strip against her forearm.

“You can hate a man and still remember his mouth. It doesn’t mean you have to do anything about either.”

Marianna looked at her.

“I delivered babies before you were born,” Róża said. “I’ve heard something about people.”

Marianna told her about the contract and the offer to inquire after Antoni.

“Don’t give him names,” Róża said.

“He already has mine.”

“Not yours.”

They sat in silence. From upstairs came Piotr’s cough, thin through the ceiling.

Then Róża opened her sewing box and took out a folded notice.

A number of properties in the district were to be cleared for German military use. Their building appeared on the list. Residents would receive instructions concerning departure and the movement of possessions. Those failing to comply would be removed.

“The caretaker got it yesterday,” Róża said. “He wanted to find out whether someone could be paid before he told everyone.”

“When?”

“Six days.”

Marianna read the list again. She knew every household on their staircase. There were old women who could scarcely get to the pump. There was a man upstairs who had walled up a cupboard around things he would not name. There was Rywka.

“Where do they expect us to go?”

“They don’t.”

At the bottom of the notice was a signature she had not seen for nineteen years until the previous morning.

Adler.

He had still made the final stroke too long.

## V

She put the notice on his desk.

He looked at it, then at her.

“You knew,” she said.

“I knew the block was being requisitioned. I didn’t know your address when the order was prepared.”

“But you knew when you offered me soap.”

“Yes.”

It was the first answer he had given without arranging himself inside it.

“Can you cancel it?”

“No.”

“Can you delay it?”

“Possibly. A day or two.”

“For everyone?”

He hesitated.

“For everyone,” she repeated.

“The buildings have been assigned. There are other offices involved.”

She thought of the towels going past the skeleton, of a child once putting chalk into a teacher’s chair. The school had not ceased to be a school because men put desks in it. Her building would not cease to be homes because Klaus had signed a paper.

“You can leave Warsaw under the laundry contract,” he said. “A contractor near Błonie has lost workers. I can put your household on the transport list.”

“My household.”

“You and the three children.”

“And my niece.”

He glanced down at the notice.

“What niece?”

“My sister’s child.”

He knew she had no sister. Or he had known it nineteen years ago.

“A cousin’s daughter,” she said. “We call her my niece.”

He opened a drawer, closed it, and asked her age.

“Twelve.”

“Does she have papers?”

“Yes.”

“Good ones?”

Marianna could hear the typewriter through the wall. A woman was typing with two fingers, striking one key harder than the others.

“Good enough to be a child’s,” she said.

He sat very still.

“What are you asking me to do?”

“Put five names on your list.”

“Marysia—”

“Don’t call me that here.”

He took a blank sheet.

“The transport is for workers and dependants. Your elder son can be listed as a loader. You as a laundress. The others as dependants.”

“Will there be an inspection?”

“Of course.”

“Then what use is it?”

“A military movement order should get you through the first checks. The contractor would have to confirm your employment at the other end.”

She knew a woman who knew someone near Błonie. Not enough for a plan. Enough to prevent her leaving.

“What must I sign?”

“An undertaking to work for the contractor.”

“How long?”

He did not answer at once, and she understood that escape could be arranged on paper as another kind of confinement.

“I won’t give you my son,” she said.

“I’m trying to get him out.”

“You’re trying to decide what help looks like before I’m allowed to ask for it.”

He set down the pen.

“What do you want?”

“Two days for the building. A place for five of us on the lorry. No inquiries about the girl.”

“That last thing may not be within my power.”

“Then don’t promise it.”

He looked older when she said that. She could almost see the young man being withdrawn from his face, leaving someone she had no claim to know.

“There is a collecting yard,” he said. “You would need to reach it yourselves. The lorry leaves on Thursday morning.”

She took the pen before he could offer it.

Under occupation she had signed for bread, for work, for the right to remain in a room she rented. Now she wrote her name beside a declaration that her household would wash the occupier’s sheets.

Her hand was steady until she came to the children.

## VI

“No,” Janek said.

They were in the little shared kitchen. Marianna had sent the younger children upstairs with the potatoes, but she knew they would be listening at the stairhead.

“You haven’t heard the arrangement.”

“I heard the part where he gives the orders.”

“He already gives the orders.”

“You don’t know what I’m doing here.”

She looked at him. For months he had come home at odd hours with explanations too elaborate for errands. Once there had been printer’s ink along his wrist. She had washed it off before Piotr noticed.

“I know enough not to ask you in a kitchen.”

He flushed.

“Then you know I can’t just go.”

“Speak to whoever needs to be spoken to. You have four days.”

“Do they know about him?”

“About whom?”

“Your German.”

She slapped him.

The noise was small and appalling. He did not move. A red mark began to appear beneath his eye.

She had struck him twice when he was little, both times in fear, both times telling herself afterwards that she would never be that sort of mother. Now he was almost a man and she had found a third occasion.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

He looked away.

“Janek.”

“Is he?”

“Is he what?”

“Mine.”

She stared at him before she understood.

“No. Your father is Antoni. I hadn’t seen that man for two years before you were born.”

His mouth altered, not quite with relief. Shame followed, and then anger at being ashamed.

“I didn’t mean—”

“I know what you meant.”

From upstairs a floorboard creaked. She hoped it was Aniela and not Piotr.

Janek sat on the edge of the coal box.

“He could be using you.”

“He is.”

He raised his head.

“He wants to be remembered by someone who knew him when he wasn’t this,” she said. “He wants to do something he can call good. I don’t know what else he wants.”

“Then why trust him?”

“I don’t. I’m deciding whether to use the things he can sign.”

That evening, Róża brought a visitor, a narrow woman with a market basket and rain in the hem of her coat. She gave her name as Ewa. Marianna did not believe it was the only name she used.

Ewa examined Rywka’s papers. She asked the girl three questions about the village where Zosia was supposed to have been born. Rywka answered two correctly and hesitated over the third.

“People don’t all remember the same things,” Ewa said. “If you have to invent, invent something small.”

Then she asked Marianna to show her the transport form.

“You should understand,” Ewa said, “that a German officer’s favour can become dangerous to everyone around you.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

Marianna’s face grew hot. “Yes.”

Ewa folded the form along its original creases.

“There is a woman outside Błonie who might take the girl for a short time. I can send word. You mustn’t go to her directly from the contractor.”

“Can you take all of us?”

“Not there.”

“Then somewhere else.”

“Perhaps. I’m not a railway timetable.”

The sharpness passed between them and settled into something more useful than politeness.

Ewa gave instructions for contacting a carter after their arrival. She made Marianna repeat them, then Janek.

At the door she paused.

“Bring the papers he gives you. Don’t tell him where you mean to go.”

“I hadn’t intended to.”

“Good. Keep not intending to.”

## VII

Klaus came to the building on Monday afternoon.

The caretaker hurried ahead of him as though escorting a visiting relation. Doors closed along the landing. Someone drew a child indoors by the sleeve.

Marianna met him before he reached her room.

“You can’t come here.”

“I brought the revised notice.”

“Give it to the caretaker.”

“There are details about Thursday.”

“Then I will come to the office.”

He looked past her. In the room, Piotr was sitting on the floor with a shoe in his lap. Rywka had gone behind the wardrobe curtain. Marianna could see the toe of one stocking beneath it.

Klaus saw it too. His gaze moved away.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “At eleven.”

He left the notice in her hand. The clearance had been postponed until Saturday. Two days, as she had asked. On the stairs his boots sounded indecently heavy.

That night someone wrote a word in chalk beside their door.

Janek found it first. Marianna saw him standing with a damp rag and knew before he stepped aside.

She had heard the word shouted at women who walked with Germans. She had sometimes looked away. Now it occupied the plaster beside the place where Antoni hung his cap.

“Don’t,” she told Janek.

He kept rubbing. Chalk water ran down the wall.

“Let me.”

“I can do it.”

“I know.”

She waited until he handed her the rag.

On Tuesday she found Klaus eating black bread at his desk. He pushed the plate away when she entered, as if she had caught him at some private luxury.

“Do not come near my children again.”

He stood. “Has something happened?”

She told him.

“I’ll speak to—”

“No.”

He stopped.

“You don’t get to cure what happens when you visit by visiting with more soldiers.”

He sat down slowly.

For the first time she looked at the photograph. A woman in a summer dress stood beside two boys on a garden path. One boy had Klaus’s uncertain ears. The other was squinting at the camera.

“How old?” she asked.

“Twelve and nine.”

“And your wife?”

“Her name is Elisabeth.”

“Does she know where you are?”

“She knows I’m in Warsaw.”

“What do you tell her?”

His eyes went to the photograph.

“About the shortages. The work. Ordinary things.”

“Do you write that you moved families out of their homes?”

“No.”

The answer came quietly.

She sat without being invited. She was suddenly tired enough to feel every stair she had climbed that morning.

“Where were you before Warsaw?”

He rubbed the side of one finger with his thumb. It was an old gesture. She hated that she remembered it.

“Near Minsk. Supply administration.”

“Nothing to do with you, then.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“What did you do?”

He was silent for so long she thought he would refuse.

“There was an attack on a convoy. A village was cleared afterwards. I signed for the vehicles.”

She waited.

“I was told the people were being moved for questioning.”

“And were they?”

“Some.”

The woman at the typewriter began again in the next room. Marianna watched a crumb on Klaus’s plate fall apart where his thumb had crushed it.

“How long did you go on being told things?”

He covered the photograph with one hand, then seemed to notice the gesture and took his hand away.

“I knew enough.”

She stood up.

He did not ask her to stay.

## VIII

For the rest of that day she could not bear the sound of wheels.

A cart in the street became the vehicles he had signed for. A chair scraped above her and became something being dragged. She was furious with herself. She knew what Germans did. The knowledge was not new merely because someone she had kissed had put his name beneath it.

Rywka found her cutting a damaged sheet into squares.

“Will we go on Thursday?”

“Yes.”

“You’re angry.”

“Yes.”

“With me?”

The scissors stopped.

“No.”

Rywka sat on the other side of the table. She had become very still again, as she had been during her first week in the room, before she learned that she could sometimes drop a spoon without consequences.

“I could go with the woman. The one with the basket.”

“She has no place for you yet.”

“She said there was a woman.”

“For after we get out.”

“Oh.”

Marianna put down the scissors.

“If you think I’m angry because taking you is difficult, you’re mistaken.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“No.”

Rywka picked a loose thread from the tablecloth.

“My mother used to be angry when she was frightened. Then she would give me something to eat.”

Marianna looked at the cupboard.

The girl gave a brief, surprised laugh.

“I didn’t mean you had to.”

There was half an onion and a heel of bread. Marianna cut the bread into two pieces and put the larger in front of Rywka. Neither pretended it was enough.

“What was she like?” she asked.

Rywka considered the question with care.

“She sang badly. My father said she frightened the neighbours’ canary. But we didn’t have neighbours with a canary.”

“Perhaps he meant before.”

“No. He was making a joke.”

“I see.”

“She didn’t, at first. Then she did. That was why it was funny.”

They ate. In the courtyard, Piotr and another boy were rolling a hoop with a stick. The iron struck the cobbles unevenly.

“Estera,” Marianna said.

Rywka looked up.

“Your mother’s name. I remember.”

The girl lowered her eyes to the bread.

“I know.”

On Wednesday the caretaker began carrying furniture out of the top-floor flat. The owners had left early to stay with relatives. He claimed they had given him the wardrobe. No one believed him and no one could spare the strength to argue.

Aniela packed their things in a case with one broken clasp. She had selected clothes, needles, two cups, the family documents, and a book of poems with loose pages. Antoni’s coat lay on the bed.

“We can’t take everything,” she said.

Marianna picked up the coat. A shiny patch at the cuff marked the place where Antoni rested his wrist on his workbench. She put it on. The shoulders hung below hers.

“I’ll wear it.”

“It’ll look strange.”

“Then let them look.”

Aniela folded one of his shirts into the case without comment.

That evening Janek came home before curfew. He had a little bread and a fresh bruise on one knuckle. When Marianna looked at it, he said, “I spoke to them. I can go.”

She nodded, unable to trust her voice.

“And you don’t have to tell me anything,” she said.

He sat beside her on the bed.

“I know.”

After a while he leaned against her, just enough for her to feel the weight of him through Antoni’s coat.

## IX

The order was ready on Wednesday afternoon. Klaus had sent a clerk rather than coming himself.

Marianna went back with the man to collect it. She wanted every condition explained where she could make him repeat it.

Five names. A departure hour. The collecting yard. The contractor’s address. A statement that the people listed were attached to a consignment of laundry equipment and were to be conveyed with it.

“Your son helps load,” Klaus said. “You supervise the smaller articles. It is better if you are seen working.”

“And the papers?”

“Keep them together. Show the movement order first.”

“If they ask for hers?”

He knew which child she meant.

“Give them what she has.”

Marianna folded the document.

“You think it’s that simple?”

“No.”

“Have you put us on another list?”

“What do you mean?”

“A list someone will inspect afterwards. A list of people you’ve helped. Something you’ll keep.”

He looked at her sharply, then away.

“There is an office copy.”

“Of course there is.”

“I can’t make an official transport without a record.”

She knew it. She wanted him to understand what his help added to the world as well as what it removed.

He opened a drawer and brought out a small envelope.

“I have some money. For the children.”

“No.”

“You will need—”

“Put it away.”

He did, with an obedience that irritated her more than argument would have.

A telephone rang in the next room. Someone answered and began speaking rapidly.

Klaus said, “I haven’t found anything about your husband.”

She held herself still.

“I made one inquiry. I did not give your present address. The answer was that the records would have to be sought elsewhere.”

“Don’t make another.”

“I might be able—”

“Don’t.”

Hope had a physical appetite. She could feel it trying to lift its head inside her, ready to feed on a rumour, a reference number, a man who said he might know a man. She had children to move before morning.

“All right,” Klaus said.

He took something else from the drawer. A postcard, brown at the edges, showing the river and a strip of the old city.

She recognised her handwriting before he turned it over.

“I found it among my papers when I was posted here.”

On the back she had written in German that Warsaw would be improved if he returned to it, though she would deny having said so if he became conceited. It had seemed a daring joke. She remembered waiting three days to post it, for fear she had sounded too eager.

“You kept that.”

“Yes.”

“Through all these years.”

He nodded.

She looked at the stamp, at the careful address of the man who had ceased to answer her.

“Leave it with the rest of your papers.”

He put it face down.

“Was any of it real to you?” he asked.

She had expected this question since the street. Now that it had come, she discovered she would not lie for the satisfaction of denying him something.

“Yes.”

He closed his eyes briefly.

“That makes this worse,” she said. “Not better.”

When he opened his eyes she was already at the door.

“The yard opens at six,” he said.

“I can read.”

Outside, she stood beneath the skeleton chart until her hands stopped trembling. Then she checked the five names again. Rywka’s borrowed name had been spelled correctly.

## X

At half past five, Mrs Róża came upstairs with boiled water and a scarf.

“The scarf isn’t a gift,” she told Marianna. “I expect it back.”

“Come with us.”

“We’ve had this conversation.”

“Have it again.”

The midwife tucked the ends of the scarf inside Piotr’s collar. She was going to stay with a former patient across the river. There were women in the district who knew where to find her, and two who expected babies before the month ended.

“I can’t take your children’s places,” she said.

“There isn’t a fixed number of places in the world.”

“On your paper there is.”

Rywka kissed her cheek. Róża held her for a moment longer than the girl expected, then let go and complained about the draught.

Downstairs the chalk stain was still visible beside the door. Marianna did not look at it directly. Janek took the case, Aniela the bundle, and Piotr insisted on carrying the two cups wrapped in a shirt.

Rywka carried nothing but her papers inside her dress.

At the gate she paused.

“What is it?” Marianna asked.

“I’m trying to remember the turn.”

“The turn?”

“In case I ever have to come back.”

Marianna waited while the girl looked at the doorway, the broken edge of the kerb, a stain beneath the first-floor window. Then they walked on.

The collecting yard contained two lorries, a wagon with a damaged wheel, and stacks of wooden crates. Civilians stood near a shed while a corporal checked names against a board. One woman had brought a live hen in a basket. Its offended muttering went on through every conversation.

Marianna showed the order.

The corporal read it, looked at Janek, and pointed to the crates.

“You. Over there.”

Janek put down the case. She wanted to stop him going out of reach. Instead she took his cap and told him not to tear his good shirt.

A driver with cracked lips directed Aniela and Marianna to a pile of canvas sacks. Piotr and Rywka sat on the case beneath the shed’s overhang.

For twenty minutes there was work. Work was a mercy. Marianna could tie knots, count sacks, distinguish clean linen from packing cloth. She could avoid looking towards the gate each time someone entered.

Then a second vehicle arrived and men began unloading office furniture. The driver swore. The furniture was for another destination; his papers had been changed. Someone fetched a sergeant. The sergeant fetched a clerk.

The delay grew. The woman with the hen began to cry without making a sound.

At seven, two military policemen came into the yard.

The corporal saluted. One policeman wore a metal gorget that caught the morning light. He took the board and began walking along the line of civilians.

“Documents.”

Marianna wiped her palms on Antoni’s coat.

Janek was still beside the lorry, holding the end of a crate. She caught his eye and moved her head once: stay there.

The policeman examined the first family, then the next. He asked the man with the damaged leg to roll up his trouser cuff. He made the woman open the hen basket.

When he reached Marianna, he took the movement order without looking at her face.

“Frontczak.”

“Yes.”

He counted them. Four at the shed, one beside the lorry.

“Everyone’s identity papers.”

She passed them over. He went through them one by one.

At Rywka’s he stopped.

## XI

The pause lasted only a few seconds. Marianna would remember it afterwards as a room in which she had lived for a long time.

“Zofia Kulesza.”

Rywka stood. He questioned her in Polish, slowly but clearly.

“Where were you born?”

She gave the village name.

“Your father?”

“Stanisław.”

“What did he do?”

“He repaired shoes.”

The policeman looked down at the paper. Marianna had not heard that answer before. She could not tell whether it was invention or something Ewa had taught her.

“And why are you here?”

“My aunt takes care of me.”

“What happened to your mother?”

“She died.”

“Of what?”

“A fever.”

The man rubbed the edge of the photograph with his thumb.

“This stamp is poor.”

Marianna said, “It was like that when—”

“I asked the girl.”

He had not asked the girl anything. Marianna bit the inside of her cheek hard enough to taste blood.

“Who gave you this?” he asked Rywka.

“The office.”

“What office?”

“The one where my aunt took me.”

His gaze moved to Marianna.

“Which office?”

She gave the district named on the document. She could hear the hen scratching inside its basket. Behind the policeman, Janek had lowered his end of the crate.

“Stay there,” she said in Polish.

The policeman turned.

“My son,” she said. “He thinks we’re ready to leave.”

“Perhaps you aren’t.”

He set Rywka’s paper apart from the others.

A voice behind him said, “What is the delay?”

Klaus stood at the shed entrance. Marianna had not heard his car. He wore the same gloves she had seen in the street, and there was a shaving cut beneath his chin.

The policeman saluted and explained that there was an irregularity in a child’s document.

Klaus held out his hand.

Marianna watched him read the paper. She wanted to tell him not to perform recognition, not to look too long, not to make the girl memorable. He gave her no glance at all.

“They are on my transport order.”

“That does not establish identity, Herr Major.”

“No. It establishes their destination and the authority responsible for them.”

“The stamp—”

“Have you found evidence of alteration?”

“It merits examination.”

“Then make a note of your objection.”

The policeman looked at him. Neither man raised his voice. Marianna understood suddenly that there was no single German authority, only men calculating what another man could require of them, and that her children were inside the calculation.

“I can send her for verification,” the policeman said.

“Yes,” Klaus answered. “You can.”

He looked down at the order again.

“And I can ask your office why a military consignment was held on suspicion of a badly struck stamp. Write your name and the time. I’ll countersign it.”

The policeman’s jaw moved.

It was not courage in a storybook. Klaus did not denounce anything. He offered a quarrel about responsibility, and for several terrible seconds Marianna did not know which responsibility the other man would prefer.

Then the policeman put the papers together.

“The contractor is to register them on arrival.”

“He has instructions.”

Klaus handed the documents to Marianna.

She took them. She could not feel her fingertips.

The policeman went to the next family.

Klaus said, loudly enough for the corporal, “These people should already be loaded.”

Janek lifted the case. The driver lowered the tailboard. One by one the children climbed up among the sacks and crates.

Marianna put her foot on the step, then turned.

Klaus was standing near enough for her to see the old break in his eyebrow.

“Get in,” he said quietly.

She did.

## XII

The lorry did not leave at once.

There was a missing waybill. There was an argument over a crate of brushes. The driver went somewhere and returned chewing. Piotr fell asleep against Aniela, his mouth open, while Marianna sat with every muscle braced for someone to call them down.

Through a gap in the canvas she could see the office door.

Klaus came out once and spoke to the corporal. He did not approach the lorry. A clerk followed him with papers, and Klaus signed one against the side of a crate.

Then he was gone.

Marianna had imagined she would feel relief when she ceased to see him. Instead she felt a furious, humiliating wish to know what he had looked like as he walked away. Not because she wanted him back. Because some part of her still insisted that the departure of this particular man should concern her.

She pressed her hands beneath her thighs until the wish passed.

Janek sat opposite her. The mark from her hand had faded. The bruise on his knuckle had not.

“Did he know?” he whispered.

She looked at Rywka, who was studying the grain of a plank.

“I think so.”

Janek nodded. He did not say what the knowledge changed. Marianna could not have told him.

The lorry started with a jerk that knocked Piotr awake. He began to cry in outrage before he remembered where he was. Aniela put the wrapped cups into his lap and told him he was responsible for them.

At the gate they stopped again. A guard looked at the driver’s papers, lifted the canvas, and counted heads. He smelled of onions and tobacco. His gaze moved over Rywka without stopping.

Then the canvas fell.

The engine changed note. They were moving.

Warsaw passed in fragments: cobbles visible through a crack, the bright side of a tram, a woman’s shoes beneath the tailboard. At another checkpoint the driver argued without getting out. Beyond it the road grew rougher and the buildings began to loosen around patches of open ground.

Marianna had not realised how hard she was holding the documents until Aniela touched her wrist.

“You’ll tear them.”

She eased her fingers.

Rywka leaned towards her. “I did invent it.”

“What?”

“The shoes. About my father.”

“You answered well.”

“My father kept accounts.”

“I know.”

“He could mend things, though. Once he mended the handle of a cup with wire.”

Piotr looked down at the bundle in his lap.

“Did it work?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Could you still drink from it?”

“Yes.”

He considered this and seemed satisfied. Rywka sat back. The conversation had required something of her, but it had also returned something; Marianna could see it in the way she let her shoulders rest against the sacks.

They reached the laundry contractor’s yard before noon. The owner was a stout woman with red hands who counted the equipment first and the people afterwards. She gave Marianna a look that said she understood there would be trouble and would prefer not to learn its particulars.

“You’ll sleep in the outbuilding,” she said. “Work begins at five.”

Janek glanced at his mother.

“For tonight,” Marianna answered him under her breath.

There were more decisions waiting. Ewa’s carter might not come. The woman beyond Błonie might have room only for Rywka. They would need food and different papers and somewhere to go that did not appear on Klaus’s order. Leaving the city had not settled any of these things.

But for the moment the children were being given water. No one was asking their names.

Marianna sat on an overturned pail while the contractor argued with the driver about a missing bundle.

Her knees had begun to shake.

## XIII

The carter came at dusk with three sacks of fodder and the wrong phrase.

Marianna nearly sent him away. He scratched his ear, looked at the children, and said the phrase again, changing the last word.

“My wife told me,” he said. “I don’t have a head for that sort of thing.”

“Then what did she say about the scarf?”

“That you’re supposed to return it. How should I know whose scarf?”

Marianna sat down on the edge of the cart.

The man waited. After a little while he looked away, giving her the privacy she could not obtain by moving.

They left separately from the yard: Janek first, as if sent on an errand; Aniela with Piotr; then Marianna and Rywka. The contractor did not stop them. Whether she had been paid, persuaded, or merely chose not to see, Marianna never learned.

They reassembled beside a roadside shrine whose flowers had gone brown in their jar. The carter laid a blanket over the fodder and told the children to sit low.

“Not because I think they’ll be looking,” he said. “Because I don’t know.”

His name was Władek. He had a split thumbnail and a way of speaking to the horse that suggested a long, disappointing marriage. After half an hour he offered Piotr a small apple.

Piotr looked at his mother before taking it.

“Thank the gentleman,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me till you’ve checked for worms.”

Piotr bit cautiously. Aniela began to laugh, caught herself, and then laughed again. Władek looked pleased without turning round.

At the edge of a village they passed a group of women carrying buckets. One watched the cart too closely. Marianna lowered her head. The habit would remain after the woman had long forgotten them.

Their destination was a smallholding behind a stand of birches. Ewa was waiting in the yard beside a woman wearing a man’s waistcoat over her dress.

“Three nights,” the woman said before anyone climbed down. “After that we discuss it.”

“Three nights,” Marianna agreed.

She had learned the value of a promise whose edge she could see.

Inside, there was soup made mostly of cabbage, a loaf with a hard crust, and a place for the younger children to sleep above the warm kitchen. Janek would sleep in the barn. Marianna could have the settle.

Rywka stood by the ladder, holding its lowest rung.

“With them?” she asked.

“For tonight,” Ewa said.

The girl climbed.

When the children had eaten, Ewa sat with Marianna at the table. She asked about the journey. Marianna described the policeman, the stamp, Klaus’s intervention. She put the order between them.

Ewa read it twice.

“Keep this?” Marianna asked.

“For now. We may need to know exactly what was recorded.”

“Not with the children’s things.”

“No.”

Marianna waited while Ewa folded it into a cloth wallet containing other papers. It seemed strange to watch an afternoon of terror become one thin item among many.

“Will he look for you?” Ewa asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Will you write to him?”

“No.”

“Even to say you arrived?”

“No.”

Ewa accepted this without approval or disapproval. Marianna was grateful.

Later, lying on the settle, she thought of the postcard in his drawer. Her young handwriting had travelled safely through years in which people had disappeared without a trace. He had kept a joke about returning to Warsaw. He had signed for vehicles. He had come to the yard that morning.

She would not make one of those facts destroy the others. She would have preferred to. The effort of holding them apart exhausted her more than the journey.

Above her, someone turned over in sleep. A cup knocked gently against the wall.

Marianna listened until she could identify each child’s breathing.

## XIV

On the third morning, Rywka asked for paper.

The farmer’s wife gave her the blank back of a seed invoice and a pencil so short it had to be held between the nails. The girl went to the step outside, where the light was better.

Marianna found her there after hanging the children’s stockings along a fence.

“What are you writing?”

“Things I remember.”

“About home?”

Rywka covered the page, then moved her hand.

There were names, an address, a description of a blue teapot. Beside Estera’s name she had written: *Sang while cutting bread. Not only songs. Sometimes just the words she was saying.*

Marianna did not read farther.

“I could get you another sheet.”

“This one isn’t finished.”

“All right.”

The girl bent over it. Beneath the invoice’s printed columns, her handwriting was small but growing less careful as she found what she wanted to say.

Inside, Janek was asking the farmer about work. Aniela was helping separate good beans from a sack containing a discouraging quantity of stones. Piotr had been given the important task of keeping hens away from the kitchen door, which he was performing with more noise than success.

They were not safe in any lasting sense. The farmer’s wife had already explained which neighbour must not see the extra washing. There was a patrol on the main road. Ewa had gone to find another arrangement and had not said when she would return.

Marianna still had Antoni’s last message, folded into the lining of his coat. Before leaving Warsaw she had thought of putting a note in their room: where they had gone, whom he should ask for. Róża had stopped her.

“Give him my name,” she had said. “If he comes, I’ll find you.”

If he came. Marianna had repeated the phrase while they packed. She repeated it now, without asking it to become either a promise or a farewell.

Rywka looked up.

“How do you spell your name before?”

“Before?”

“Before Frontczak. The man in the yard had it on the paper.”

“Tarnowska.”

Marianna spelled it. Rywka wrote slowly, then checked the letters with her.

“Why do you want that one?”

“So I know.”

Marianna sat beside her on the step. The wood was warm where the sun had reached it and damp along the shaded edge.

For a while she watched the pencil move. She thought of herself at twenty-two, with invoices spread across her father’s counter, certain she could recognise an error whenever she saw one. She wanted, absurdly, to warn that girl about everything except the things that had brought Janek and Aniela and Piotr into the world.

There was no useful warning to send.

“Is Marianna with two n’s?” Rywka asked.

“Yes.”

“People leave one out of mine sometimes.”

“Do they?”

“My real one.”

Marianna waited.

“Will you spell it with me?”

They bent over the paper together. Rywka said each letter, and Marianna repeated it. The pencil made a faint tearing sound across the rough back of the invoice.

At the kitchen door Piotr was negotiating with a hen. Aniela called to him to stop shouting, and Janek laughed at something the farmer said. For once Marianna did not turn to count them. She knew exactly where they were.

When Rywka had finished, she held the page out to dry an accidental smear, though there was no ink on it to dry.

Marianna steadied the curling corner with one finger and left the names uncovered.
